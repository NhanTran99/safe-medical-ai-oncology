# 1. Educational Question

## Primary Question

**What is targeted therapy for gastric cancer, how is it different from chemotherapy and immunotherapy, when can it be used, and why do biomarker results matter?**

PP-0209 is the foundational treatment-modality package for targeted therapy.

It explains the clinical bridge:

**cancer biology**

→ **molecular or protein target**

→ **biomarker information**

→ **potential treatment eligibility**

→ **targeted therapy**

→ **clinical benefit and limitations**

The package is intentionally positioned between the upstream biomarker-testing and biomarker-directed-selection packages and the downstream target-specific therapy packages.

It is therefore broader than any single drug package, but narrower than the complete treatment architecture for advanced gastric cancer.

# 2. Educational Objectives

After reading this Population Package, the reader should be able to:

1. Explain what targeted therapy means in gastric cancer.
2. Explain why targeted therapy is linked to biomarkers.
3. Distinguish targeted therapy conceptually from chemotherapy.
4. Distinguish targeted therapy conceptually from immunotherapy.
5. Understand that a therapeutic target does not have to be a DNA mutation.
6. Recognize HER2, CLDN18.2, and VEGFR-2 as major targeted-therapy paradigms represented in the project evidence.
7. Recognize selected actionable alterations such as NTRK gene fusions, BRAF V600E mutations, and RET gene fusions.
8. Understand why the treatment line and prior therapy matter.
9. Understand why targeted therapy may be combined with chemotherapy or, in selected settings, used without conventional chemotherapy.
10. Understand that a biomarker result does not guarantee treatment response.
11. Understand the concepts of resistance, tumor heterogeneity, and possible biomarker reassessment.
12. Recognize the principal benefits, limitations, and safety considerations of targeted therapy.
13. Know which detailed questions belong to downstream therapy-specific Population Packages.
14. Use the package to formulate informed questions for the cancer care team.

# 4. Core Concept — What Is Targeted Therapy?

Targeted therapy uses a drug or other therapeutic substance to act on a specific biological feature associated with cancer.

The target may be:

- a protein;
- a receptor;
- a signaling pathway;
- a cell-surface molecule;
- a gene fusion;
- a gene alteration;
- an amplification or overexpression state;
- another molecular characteristic that can be therapeutically exploited.

This means targeted therapy is defined by **what the treatment is designed to target**, not by the requirement that every target be a DNA mutation.

For gastric cancer, the project sources provide several examples:

- HER2;
- CLDN18.2;
- VEGFR-2;
- NTRK fusions;
- BRAF V600E;
- RET fusions.

The target is therefore the biological anchor of the treatment strategy.

# 5. Why Targeted Therapy Belongs to Precision Oncology

Precision oncology attempts to connect the biological characteristics of an individual cancer with treatment selection.

A simplified pathway is:

**Cancer diagnosis**

→ **clinical setting**

→ **biomarker assessment**

→ **target identified**

→ **clinical actionability considered**

→ **targeted-treatment option considered**

→ **benefit and burden reassessed**

The presence of a target does not remove the need for clinical judgment.

Treatment selection still depends on:

- disease setting;
- treatment line;
- prior treatment;
- performance status;
- comorbidities;
- organ function;
- toxicity;
- other biomarkers;
- patient goals;
- availability of an appropriate therapy;
- evidence supporting the proposed treatment.

# 6. Targeted Therapy and Biomarker Testing

## The central relationship

A targeted treatment generally becomes clinically relevant because a test provides information about the target.

Examples include:

| Biomarker / target concept | Representative targeted-treatment paradigm |
|---|---|
| HER2 overexpression/amplification | Trastuzumab-based therapy; later-line trastuzumab deruxtecan |
| CLDN18.2 positivity | Zolbetuximab-containing therapy |
| VEGFR-2 pathway | Ramucirumab |
| NTRK gene fusion | TRK-directed therapy in selected circumstances |
| BRAF V600E mutation | BRAF/MEK-directed therapy in selected circumstances |
| RET gene fusion | RET-directed therapy in selected circumstances |

This table is a **conceptual map**, not a treatment prescription.

The detailed testing layer belongs upstream.

The detailed treatment layer belongs downstream.

# 7. Targeted Therapy Is Not the Same as Chemotherapy

Chemotherapy and targeted therapy can both be used to control gastric cancer, but they operate through different conceptual mechanisms.

### Chemotherapy

Chemotherapy generally acts on cellular processes associated with cancer-cell growth and division and may affect normal cells that also divide rapidly.

### Targeted therapy

Targeted therapy is designed around a more specific molecular or biological feature.

This distinction should not be overstated.

Targeted therapy is not synonymous with:

- complete tumor specificity;
- absence of toxicity;
- guaranteed response;
- superiority over chemotherapy in every situation.

Targeted treatment can itself produce clinically important adverse effects.

In many gastric-cancer settings, targeted therapy is used **together with chemotherapy**, rather than as a universal replacement for chemotherapy.

# 8. Targeted Therapy Is Not the Same as Immunotherapy

### Targeted therapy

The therapeutic target is generally a molecular or biological feature associated with the cancer.

### Immunotherapy

The treatment modifies or activates immune mechanisms to help the immune system recognize or control cancer.

The two approaches can appear in the same treatment strategy.

For example, the project NCCN source describes selected first-line combinations in which chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy may be combined when the relevant biomarkers and clinical circumstances are present.

Therefore:

> **Targeted therapy and immunotherapy are distinct treatment concepts, even though they may be used together.**

# 9. Target Does Not Always Mean Mutation

A critical patient-facing concept is that a therapeutic target does not have to be a mutation.

### HER2

Treatment may be guided by HER2 protein overexpression/amplification status.

### CLDN18.2

Treatment may be guided by expression of the CLDN18.2 protein.

### VEGFR-2

Ramucirumab illustrates targeting of a receptor/pathway involved in angiogenesis.

### NTRK

A gene fusion can create an actionable target.

### BRAF V600E

A specific mutation can create an actionable treatment opportunity.

### RET

A gene fusion can create an actionable target.

Therefore, biomarker testing in targeted therapy is broader than “looking for mutations.”

# 10. Major Targeted-Therapy Paradigm — HER2

HER2 is one of the clearest examples of biomarker-directed treatment in gastric cancer.

At a high level:

**HER2 assessment**

→ **HER2-positive result in the relevant clinical context**

→ **HER2-directed treatment becomes a potential option**

The supplied NCCN v2.2026 material describes trastuzumab-containing first-line systemic therapy for appropriate HER2-positive advanced disease and also identifies fam-trastuzumab deruxtecan as a preferred subsequent-treatment option for HER2 overexpression-positive adenocarcinoma.

NCI and ACS likewise identify trastuzumab and trastuzumab deruxtecan among targeted therapies used for stomach cancer.

PP-0209 uses HER2 to teach the targeted-therapy principle.

Detailed HER2 eligibility, drug selection, efficacy, resistance, toxicity, and monitoring are delegated to **PP-0210 — HER2-targeted Therapy**.

# 11. Major Targeted-Therapy Paradigm — CLDN18.2

CLDN18.2 demonstrates how a tumor-associated protein can become a therapeutic target.

At a high level:

**HER2-negative advanced disease**

+

**CLDN18.2-positive tumor**

→

**CLDN18.2-directed treatment may become an option**

The supplied NCCN v2.2026 material identifies zolbetuximab-containing therapy with chemotherapy as a preferred first-line option in the appropriate CLDN18.2-positive setting.

NCI summarizes the clinical evidence supporting zolbetuximab-containing treatment in selected CLDN18.2-positive, HER2-negative advanced disease.

PP-0209 therefore uses CLDN18.2 to demonstrate:

- biomarker-defined treatment selection;
- target-directed therapy;
- combination therapy;
- the importance of clinical context.

Detailed CLDN18.2 testing and zolbetuximab treatment belong to **PP-0211 — CLDN18.2-targeted Therapy**.

# 12. Major Targeted-Therapy Paradigm — VEGFR-2 / Anti-Angiogenic Therapy

Ramucirumab demonstrates a different targeted-treatment model.

The target is not a tumor-specific mutation.

Instead, ramucirumab acts on VEGFR-2, a receptor involved in signaling associated with tumor angiogenesis.

The clinical lesson is:

> **Targeted therapy can interfere with a biological pathway or receptor that supports tumor growth, even when the target is not a tumor-specific genomic mutation.**

The supplied NCCN and NCI materials support ramucirumab-containing treatment in selected previously treated advanced gastric cancer settings.

PP-0209 introduces the concept.

Detailed anti-angiogenic therapy, treatment-line architecture, and toxicity management belong to **PP-0212 — Anti-angiogenic Therapy**.

# 13. Other Actionable Alterations

The project sources also support selected targeted-treatment options for less common molecular alterations.

## NTRK gene fusions

Selected NTRK fusion-positive tumors may be treated with TRK-directed therapies such as entrectinib, larotrectinib, or repotrectinib in appropriate circumstances.

## BRAF V600E

The supplied NCCN framework identifies dabrafenib plus trametinib for selected BRAF V600E-mutated tumors.

## RET gene fusions

The supplied NCCN framework identifies selpercatinib for selected RET fusion-positive tumors.

These examples are important because they show how rare genomic alterations can create treatment opportunities.

They should not be interpreted as implying that every gastric cancer should undergo the same treatment pathway or that a rare alteration automatically determines therapy without clinical review.

# 14. Targeted Therapy Can Be Combined With Other Treatments

Targeted therapy can appear in several patterns.

### Pattern A — Targeted therapy + chemotherapy

Examples include:

- trastuzumab + chemotherapy;
- zolbetuximab + chemotherapy.

### Pattern B — Targeted therapy as a selected single-agent strategy

The ACS source describes selected advanced cancers with NTRK, BRAF, or RET alterations for which a targeted drug alone may be an option.

### Pattern C — Targeted therapy within a broader combination

In selected HER2-positive disease, targeted therapy may be combined with chemotherapy and, when the relevant immunotherapy biomarker is present, immunotherapy.

The existence of these patterns does not create a universal rule.

The appropriate pattern depends on the target, treatment setting, treatment line, prior therapy, evidence, and patient factors.

# 15. Treatment Setting and Treatment Line Matter

Targeted therapy is a **modality**, not a treatment intent.

The same conceptual target may be relevant at different points in the disease course.

Important variables include:

- unresectable locally advanced disease;
- recurrent disease;
- metastatic disease;
- first-line treatment;
- second-line treatment;
- later-line treatment;
- previous exposure to targeted therapy;
- previous exposure to checkpoint inhibitors;
- performance status;
- treatment tolerance.

For example:

- HER2-directed therapy can be incorporated into first-line systemic treatment in appropriate HER2-positive advanced disease.
- Ramucirumab has an established role in selected subsequent-line settings.
- T-DXd has a role in selected previously treated HER2-positive disease.
- Rare alteration-directed therapies may be used in selected circumstances.

Therefore:

> **“There is a targeted therapy for this target” is not the same statement as “this drug should be given now.”**

# 16. Expected Benefits

Depending on the target and clinical context, targeted therapy may:

- shrink tumors;
- delay disease progression;
- improve disease control;
- prolong survival in selected populations;
- relieve symptoms when tumor burden decreases;
- provide an additional treatment option after progression on previous therapy.

The evidence should always be interpreted at the population level.

A trial showing benefit in a biomarker-defined population does not guarantee the same benefit for an individual patient.

The benefit also needs to be weighed against:

- toxicity;
- treatment burden;
- competing treatment options;
- prior therapy;
- patient goals;
- quality of life.

# 17. Why a Positive Biomarker Does Not Guarantee Response

A biomarker identifies a biological feature.

It does not prove that every tumor cell depends on that feature to the same degree.

Several factors can explain incomplete or absent response:

- tumor heterogeneity;
- variation in target expression;
- coexisting molecular pathways;
- inadequate drug exposure;
- resistance mechanisms;
- differences between the tested specimen and other disease sites;
- limitations of the assay;
- differences between clinical-trial populations and individual patients.

Therefore:

**Biomarker positive**

does not equal

**response guaranteed**.

# 18. Treatment Resistance

Cancer can change during treatment.

A simplified model is:

**target present**

→ **targeted treatment**

→ **tumor initially controlled**

→ **tumor evolves**

→ **resistant disease emerges**

→ **treatment reassessment**

Resistance can arise through biological mechanisms that are specific to a target or treatment.

PP-0209 only introduces this concept.

Detailed resistance mechanisms for HER2-directed therapy, antibody-drug conjugates, anti-angiogenic therapy, or other individual targets are outside scope and belong to downstream packages.

# 19. Tumor Heterogeneity and Molecular Evolution

A gastric cancer is not necessarily biologically uniform.

Different tumor regions or metastatic sites may contain different cellular populations.

This matters because a biomarker result obtained from one specimen may not perfectly represent every tumor site.

Molecular characteristics can also change during disease evolution or treatment.

The practical implication is not that every patient must automatically be retested.

Rather:

> **Biomarker information must be interpreted in the context of the specimen, disease course, treatment history, and the clinical question.**

Repeat testing is therefore a context-dependent clinical decision rather than a universal rule.

# 20. Biomarker Reassessment

The supplied NCCN framework includes circumstances in which repeat biomarker assessment may be considered during progression of advanced/metastatic disease.

PP-0209 should teach the concept without creating a universal retesting algorithm.

Potential reasons for reassessment include:

- new treatment options becoming available;
- disease evolution;
- a change in the clinical question;
- limited or older original tissue;
- concern that the current tumor biology may differ from the original specimen.

The package does not prescribe:

- when every patient should be retested;
- which assay must be used;
- which specimen must be selected;
- how a treatment should be changed after a result.

Those decisions belong to detailed testing and treatment packages.

# 21. Targeted Therapy and Safety

“Targeted” does not mean “side-effect free.”

Targeted agents can produce:

- infusion reactions;
- gastrointestinal effects;
- blood-count abnormalities;
- organ-specific toxicities;
- cardiovascular effects;
- pulmonary toxicities;
- hypertension or vascular effects;
- other drug-specific adverse events.

The type and frequency of toxicity depend on the individual therapy.

For example, the NCI PDQ describes clinically important toxicities with trastuzumab deruxtecan, including neutropenia, anemia, and drug-related interstitial lung disease.

PP-0209 only teaches the general principle.

Detailed toxicity management is delegated to the therapy-specific packages.

# 22. Targeted Therapy and Patient Goals

The clinical decision is not only about whether a target exists.

Patients and clinicians also consider:

- expected benefit;
- treatment burden;
- toxicity;
- convenience;
- previous treatment;
- current symptoms;
- functional status;
- quality of life;
- patient preferences;
- availability of clinical trials.

This is especially important in advanced disease.

A biomarker result opens a possible treatment pathway.

It does not independently determine the final treatment decision.

# 23. Common Misconceptions

## Myth 1 — “Targeted therapy means there are no side effects.”

**Fact:** Targeted therapies can cause clinically important adverse effects.

## Myth 2 — “If my tumor has the target, the drug will definitely work.”

**Fact:** A target supports treatment rationale or eligibility in the appropriate context but does not guarantee response.

## Myth 3 — “Targeted therapy is always better than chemotherapy.”

**Fact:** The appropriate treatment depends on disease setting, target, evidence, prior therapy, toxicity, and patient factors.

## Myth 4 — “A target always means a mutation.”

**Fact:** Targets can include proteins, receptors, pathways, gene fusions, amplifications/overexpression states, and selected mutations.

## Myth 5 — “Targeted therapy is always given alone.”

**Fact:** Many gastric-cancer targeted strategies are combined with chemotherapy; selected molecularly defined settings may use targeted therapy alone.

## Myth 6 — “A biomarker result tells the doctor exactly which drug to prescribe.”

**Fact:** Biomarker information is one part of treatment selection and must be interpreted with clinical context and current evidence.

## Myth 7 — “If a biomarker was positive once, it can never change.”

**Fact:** Tumor biology can evolve, and reassessment may be considered in selected circumstances.

## Myth 8 — “If the biomarker is negative, the cancer has no molecular abnormalities.”

**Fact:** A negative result is specific to what was tested, how it was tested, and the specimen analyzed.

# 24. Patient-Facing Decision Ladder

A useful way to understand targeted therapy is:

### Step 1 — What is the clinical situation?

Is the cancer:

- localized;
- unresectable;
- recurrent;
- metastatic?

### Step 2 — What treatment line is being considered?

Is this:

- first-line;
- second-line;
- later-line treatment?

### Step 3 — What biomarker information is available?

Examples include:

- HER2;
- CLDN18.2;
- MSI/MMR;
- PD-L1;
- selected genomic alterations.

### Step 4 — Is there an actionable target?

If yes, the corresponding targeted-treatment pathway may become relevant.

### Step 5 — Does the treatment fit the current clinical context?

Consider:

- previous therapy;
- performance status;
- comorbidities;
- toxicity;
- patient goals.

### Step 6 — What are the expected benefits and burdens?

The final decision is a clinical decision, not a biomarker-only decision.

# 31. Expanded Clinical Reasoning Framework

## 31.1 Why the Target Matters

A targeted therapy becomes clinically relevant because it is designed to act on a particular biological feature. The reasoning therefore begins with the feature that is present, not with the drug that happens to be available.

The clinical chain is:

**Target exists** → **target can be characterized** → **test produces a result** → **result is interpreted** → **treatment relevance is considered** → **targeted therapy may become an option**.

The biomarker result is therefore not the treatment itself.

---

## 31.2 Why Clinical Context Remains Necessary

Even when a target is present, treatment selection still depends on disease setting, treatment line, previous therapy, performance status, comorbidities, organ function, toxicity, patient goals, available alternatives, and current evidence.

A molecular result is one component of a larger clinical decision.

---

## 31.3 Why “Actionable” Does Not Mean “Obligatory”

An alteration may be clinically actionable because an available treatment or trial could be relevant.

That does not mean:

- the treatment is appropriate for every patient;
- the treatment must be given immediately;
- the treatment will definitely work;
- the treatment is the only reasonable option.

Actionability is a clinical-relevance concept, not an automatic prescription rule.

# 33. Why Different Tests May Be Needed

Targeted therapy can depend on different biological target types. One universal test therefore cannot necessarily answer every targeted-treatment question.

Depending on the clinical question, relevant information may come from:

- immunohistochemistry;
- in situ hybridization;
- targeted molecular testing;
- PCR-based approaches;
- broader molecular profiling / NGS.

PP-0209 does not teach the technical methodology.

The educational point is:

> **The testing strategy must match the biological question.**

---

# 34. Tissue and Broader Molecular Information

The project NCCN source recognizes both target-specific testing and broader molecular profiling in appropriate circumstances.

Target-specific testing answers a defined question.

Broader profiling can evaluate multiple molecular events at once.

Neither should automatically be described as universally superior.

The appropriate approach depends on the clinical question, available tissue, prior testing, disease setting, available therapies, laboratory capability, and guideline context.

---

# 35. Why a Negative Result Needs Context

A negative biomarker result is specific to:

- what was tested;
- how it was tested;
- the specimen analyzed;
- the limitations of the assay.

Therefore:

> **Negative does not mean “there are no molecular features in this cancer.”**

It means that the relevant target was not identified within the scope and limitations of the test performed.

---

# 36. Why a Positive Result Needs Context

A positive result must still be evaluated for:

- validity;
- clinical significance;
- treatment availability;
- treatment indication;
- treatment line;
- previous therapy;
- current disease state.

This is particularly important for rare genomic alterations.

A molecular finding should not be detached from the clinical setting.

# 38. Why Treatment History Matters

A patient may already have received chemotherapy, trastuzumab, immunotherapy, or another targeted therapy.

Previous treatment can affect:

- eligibility;
- treatment line;
- expected benefit;
- toxicity;
- sequencing;
- available alternatives.

Therefore a targeted-therapy package cannot be used independently of treatment history.

---

# 39. Why Performance Status Matters

Patients with the same biomarker may receive different treatment because they differ in:

- functional status;
- frailty;
- comorbidities;
- organ function;
- symptom burden;
- treatment tolerance.

Targeted therapy is therefore not a purely molecular decision.

---

# 40. Why Patient Goals Matter

Patients may value:

- longer survival;
- tumor shrinkage;
- symptom relief;
- preserving function;
- minimizing toxicity;
- reducing treatment visits;
- maintaining quality of life.

The presence of a target does not remove these priorities.

---

# 41. Benefit-Burden Reasoning

A useful patient-facing model is:

**Potential benefit**

versus

**Potential burden**.

Potential benefit may include response, disease control, progression delay, survival benefit, or symptom improvement.

Potential burden may include adverse effects, infusion/administration burden, monitoring, repeated visits, cumulative toxicity, and effects on quality of life.

The final decision requires both dimensions.

# 42. Resistance — Expanded Concept

Resistance does not necessarily mean that the original target was absent.

A cancer may initially respond and later progress.

Possible conceptual explanations include:

- changes in the target;
- alternative signaling pathways;
- selection of resistant clones;
- changes in tumor biology;
- altered drug sensitivity.

The exact mechanism differs by therapy.

Therefore:

> **Targeted therapy can stop working even when the original treatment was biologically well matched to the tumor.**

---

# 43. Tumor Evolution — Patient-Facing Explanation

Cancer cells can change over time.

A treatment may suppress sensitive cells while resistant populations become more prominent.

This can alter:

- target expression;
- molecular profile;
- treatment sensitivity.

At progression, a new clinical and sometimes molecular assessment may therefore become relevant.

This does not mean every patient automatically needs repeat testing.

---

# 44. What Targeted Therapy Cannot Do

Targeted therapy cannot:

- guarantee cure;
- guarantee response;
- eliminate every cancer cell in every patient;
- replace clinical judgment;
- eliminate the need for monitoring;
- eliminate toxicity;
- guarantee that a biomarker will remain unchanged.

---

# 45. Patient Questions to Bring to the Care Team

1. What target has been found in my tumor?
2. How was that target tested?
3. How confident are we in the result?
4. Does the target have a guideline-supported treatment?
5. Is this treatment appropriate for my current treatment line?
6. Is it given alone or with chemotherapy?
7. What benefit can realistically be expected?
8. What are the most important side effects?
9. What monitoring will I need?
10. What would happen if the cancer progresses?
11. Could the target change over time?
12. Would repeat testing be useful if the cancer progresses?
13. Are there relevant clinical trials?
14. What alternatives exist?

These are educational prompts, not individualized recommendations.

# 49. Expanded Key Message Set

1. Targeted therapy is based on a specific biological feature.
2. Biomarker testing helps identify whether that feature is present.
3. Not every gastric cancer has the same target.
4. A target can be a protein, receptor, pathway, fusion, amplification/overexpression state, or mutation.
5. HER2, CLDN18.2 and VEGFR-2 are major examples.
6. NTRK, BRAF V600E and RET illustrate selected rare actionable alterations.
7. Targeted therapy can be combined with chemotherapy.
8. Selected molecularly defined cancers may receive targeted therapy alone.
9. A positive biomarker does not guarantee response.
10. Targeted therapy can have important toxicity.
11. Cancer can evolve and become resistant.
12. Treatment decisions remain clinical decisions, not biomarker-only decisions.


# 50. Expanded Clinical Teaching Examples

## Example A — HER2

A patient has advanced gastric cancer and the tumor is HER2-positive in the clinically relevant testing framework.

The correct educational interpretation is:

**HER2-positive**

→ **HER2-directed treatment may be relevant**

not:

**HER2-positive**

→ **one automatic drug for every patient**.

The treatment line, previous therapy, PD-L1 status, performance status, toxicity, and current guideline context remain relevant.

## Example B — CLDN18.2

A patient has advanced HER2-negative disease and the tumor is CLDN18.2-positive.

The educational interpretation is:

**CLDN18.2-positive**

→ **CLDN18.2-directed therapy may be relevant**

with treatment context determining whether the option applies.

## Example C — NTRK

A tumor contains an NTRK gene fusion.

The educational interpretation is:

**NTRK fusion**

→ **a TRK-directed option may be relevant in the appropriate clinical setting**.

The rarity of the alteration and the specific treatment evidence must be preserved.

# 51. Conceptual Summary

Targeted therapy is best understood as a bridge between:

**what the cancer is biologically**

and

**how the cancer may be treated**.

The bridge is not automatic.

It requires:

- reliable biomarker information;
- clinical interpretation;
- appropriate evidence;
- treatment availability;
- consideration of patient-specific factors.

This is the defining educational role of PP-0209.
