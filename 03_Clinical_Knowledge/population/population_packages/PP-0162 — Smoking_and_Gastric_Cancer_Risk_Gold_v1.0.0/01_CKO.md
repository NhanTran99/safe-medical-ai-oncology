## Educational Objectives
Explain how cigarette smoking affects gastric-cancer risk, how risk differs between current and former smokers, and why smoking cessation is a gastric-cancer prevention strategy.

## Scope

### Included
- Cigarette smoking as a gastric-cancer risk factor.
- Strength and magnitude of the association.
- Current versus former smoking.
- Exposure concept.
- Sex-specific population-level evidence.
- Risk reduction after cessation.
- Smoking prevention/cessation as gastric-cancer prevention.
- Smoking within the environmental-risk framework.

### Not Included
- Generic tobacco education.
- Secondhand smoke, vaping, or smokeless tobacco.
- Detailed nicotine-dependence treatment or cessation pharmacotherapy.
- Pack-year calculators or individualized absolute-risk calculation.
- Universal time-to-baseline-risk thresholds.
- Detailed molecular carcinogenesis.
- Alcohol, diet, obesity.
- H. pylori testing/eradication.
- Precursor-lesion management.
- Gastric-cancer screening, prognosis, or recurrence.

## Clinical Knowledge Blocks

### KB1 — Smoking and Gastric Cancer
Smoking is an established gastric-cancer risk factor. NCI characterizes the evidence as solid and identifies cigarette smoking as a cause of stomach cancer.

### KB2 — How Much Does Smoking Increase Risk?
A systematic review/meta-analysis summarized by NCI found approximately a 60% increase in gastric-cancer risk in male smokers and 20% in female smokers compared with nonsmokers. These are population-level estimates, not individualized predictions.

### KB3 — Current Versus Former Smoking
NCI reports an average relative risk of about 1.6 for current smokers and 1.2 for former smokers. Persistent smoking therefore carries greater risk than former smoking.

### KB4 — Time Since Cessation
Risk decreases among former smokers compared with persistent smokers, with increasing time since cessation. The evidence supports benefit from stopping but does not establish one universal time at which individual risk becomes identical to that of never-smokers.

### KB5 — Smoking Exposure
Evidence has assessed different levels of smoking exposure. Exposure-response is clinically relevant, but the supplied sources do not support a validated individual pack-year risk calculator.

### KB6 — Smoking as a Modifiable Risk Factor
Unlike family history or established precursor conditions, smoking is modifiable. Prevention of smoking and cessation therefore represent actionable gastric-cancer prevention opportunities.

### KB7 — Smoking Within the Environmental Risk Framework
NCCN identifies smoking alongside H. pylori infection, high salt intake, and other environmental factors, particularly in relation to intestinal-type gastric cancer. This package does not assign an individual combined-risk estimate.

### KB8 — Smoking and Other Risk Factors
Smoking may coexist with H. pylori infection, family history, obesity, dietary exposures, and precursor conditions. PP-0162 does not own interaction models or individualized combined-risk calculations.

### KB9 — Smoking Cessation After Gastric Cancer
NCCN recommends smoking-cessation advice, counseling, and pharmacotherapy as indicated in gastric-cancer care. Detailed survivorship and cessation-treatment protocols remain outside this package.

### KB10 — What We Can and Cannot Infer
Evidence supports smoking as a gastric-cancer risk factor and supports declining risk after cessation. It does not justify claiming that cessation guarantees zero future risk or produces an exact individualized risk trajectory.

## Patient Explanation
Smoking increases the risk of stomach cancer. The risk is higher in people who continue smoking than in people who have stopped, and the risk decreases over time after quitting. Stopping smoking is therefore beneficial not only for general health but also for reducing future stomach-cancer risk.

The exact amount of risk for one person cannot be calculated from smoking status alone. Other factors, such as H. pylori infection, family history, diet, and existing stomach conditions, can also affect risk.

## Common Misconceptions

### Myth 1
“Only heavy smokers are at risk.”
**Fact:** Smoking is a recognized gastric-cancer risk factor; risk assessment should consider the overall exposure history rather than assuming a simple safe threshold.

### Myth 2
“Quitting is too late if I have smoked for years.”
**Fact:** Gastric-cancer risk decreases among former smokers with time since cessation.

### Myth 3
“After quitting, my risk immediately becomes the same as a never-smoker.”
**Fact:** Risk decreases over time; the supplied sources do not establish an immediate or universal return to baseline.

### Myth 4
“Smoking alone determines my gastric-cancer risk.”
**Fact:** Gastric-cancer risk is multifactorial.

### Myth 5
“If I have H. pylori, quitting smoking no longer matters.”
**Fact:** Smoking and H. pylori are separate risk factors; management of one does not make the other irrelevant.

## Key Messages
- Cigarette smoking increases gastric-cancer risk.
- The evidence is characterized by NCI as solid.
- Risk is higher in current smokers than former smokers.
- Risk decreases with time after cessation.
- Smoking cessation is a gastric-cancer prevention strategy.
- Population-level risk estimates should not be converted into individualized predictions.
- Other gastric-cancer risk factors remain relevant.
