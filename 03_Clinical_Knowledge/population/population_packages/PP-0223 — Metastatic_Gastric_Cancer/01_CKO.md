## Educational Objectives

1. Define metastatic gastric cancer and explain the relationship between distant metastatic disease and Stage IV/M1 disease.
2. Distinguish distant metastasis from local invasion and regional lymph-node disease.
3. Explain how metastatic distribution, burden, symptoms, patient fitness, prior treatment and tumor biology influence the overall management framework.
4. Explain why biomarker characterization is clinically relevant in advanced/metastatic gastric cancer.
5. Describe the role of systemic therapy, selected local interventions, clinical trials and supportive/palliative care without reproducing downstream treatment packages.
6. Interpret common patient questions and misconceptions safely, without converting population-level evidence into individualized medical advice.

## Scope

### Included

PP-0223 explains metastatic gastric cancer as a disease state. It covers Stage IV/M1 disease, distant spread, metastatic distribution, disease burden, confirmation and characterization, performance status, nutritional and symptom context, tumor biomarkers, the role of systemic therapy, selected local interventions, clinical trials, reassessment, supportive/palliative care, patient goals, misconceptions and prognosis concepts.

### Not Included

Detailed treatment regimens, drug dosing, therapy-specific algorithms, detailed biomarker laboratory methods, RECIST/iRECIST, detailed imaging methodology, detailed peritoneal management, HIPEC/PIPAC, detailed toxicity management, detailed palliative-care protocols, individualized treatment or prognosis.

## Clinical Knowledge Blocks

### 1. What is metastatic gastric cancer?

Metastatic gastric cancer is gastric adenocarcinoma that has spread from the stomach to distant sites. The term describes the extent and distribution of the cancer; it does not mean that a tumor found in another organ automatically represents a new primary cancer.

In TNM staging, metastatic disease is represented by M1 disease. The NCI Gastric Cancer Treatment PDQ defines pathologic Stage IV as Any T, Any N, M1. NCCN v2.2026 places cM1 disease in the Stage IV pathway.

Metastatic disease is therefore a disease-state concept rather than merely a list of metastatic organs. The clinical team must understand where disease is present, how extensive it is, whether it is symptomatic, what the tumor biology is, what treatment has already been given, and whether the patient is fit enough for active treatment.

### 2. Metastatic versus local or regional disease

Direct extension into an adjacent structure and regional lymph-node involvement are not identical to distant metastasis. Metastatic disease means distant spread.

This distinction matters because the management architecture changes when distant disease is established. Local and regional disease may be approached within potentially curative local-treatment pathways in selected patients. Established metastatic disease generally requires a disease-control strategy centered on systemic treatment, with selected local interventions when clinically appropriate.

A patient should not interpret every tumor outside the visible stomach as a new primary cancer. Pathology, imaging, clinical context and, when appropriate, biopsy of a distant lesion establish whether a suspicious site represents metastatic gastric cancer or another process.

### 3. Stage IV and M1

Stage IV gastric cancer is defined by distant metastatic disease in the TNM framework. NCI PDQ gives Stage IV as Any T, Any N, M1.

Stage is important, but it does not provide the complete treatment description. Two patients can both have M1 disease while differing substantially in number of metastatic sites, disease volume, symptoms, performance status, biomarkers, prior treatment and treatment goals.

For this reason, PP-0223 uses Stage IV/M1 as the entry point and then explains the additional dimensions required for clinical characterization.

### 4. How metastatic disease is characterized

The metastatic work-up is intended to establish the extent of disease and provide the information needed for treatment planning. NCCN v2.2026 includes chest/abdomen/pelvis CT with oral and IV contrast, FDG-PET/CT when clinically indicated, and biopsy of metastatic disease when clinically indicated.

The same assessment also interfaces with laboratory evaluation, nutritional assessment and biomarker testing. The purpose is not simply to assign a stage label. It is to build a clinically useful picture of the disease and the patient.

A suspicious distant lesion may sometimes require tissue confirmation, particularly when the result would change management or when imaging cannot reliably distinguish metastasis from another condition. The decision is clinical and context-dependent.

### 5. Metastatic patterns and disease burden

Metastatic gastric cancer can involve different distant compartments. The project has dedicated foundational or downstream packages for liver, lung, bone, brain, ovary, distant lymph nodes and peritoneal disease.

PP-0223 owns the cross-cutting concept: metastatic site and distribution matter. Disease may be confined to one metastatic compartment or involve several sites. Peritoneal disease may coexist with extraperitoneal metastases or, in a specific subgroup, may be the only metastatic disease.

The package should not imply that all metastatic sites have the same biology, symptoms, prognosis or local-treatment options. Organ-specific interpretation belongs to dedicated packages.

### 6. Peritoneal metastasis

Peritoneal metastasis is a metastatic pattern of gastric cancer and therefore belongs in the conceptual map of PP-0223.

However, peritoneal disease has a dedicated downstream architecture. NCCN v2.2026 provides a separate GAST-10 pathway for peritoneal carcinoma as the only disease, including restaging, diagnostic laparoscopy with washings and documented Peritoneal Cancer Index in the relevant pathway, multidisciplinary review, systemic therapy and selected cytoreductive/intraperitoneal approaches.

PP-0223 therefore introduces peritoneal metastasis but does not reproduce peritoneal-specific staging or treatment.

### 7. Performance status and medical fitness

Metastatic treatment decisions depend on more than tumor extent. NCCN's palliative-management pathway distinguishes patients by performance status, including ECOG and Karnofsky categories.

Performance status is a practical description of how well a person can carry out daily activities. It helps clinicians judge whether the potential benefits of active treatment are likely to outweigh treatment burden.

Age alone should not be treated as a complete measure of fitness. Comorbidities, organ function, frailty, nutrition, symptoms, prior toxicity and patient goals may all affect the feasible treatment plan.

### 8. Nutritional status

Gastric cancer and metastatic disease can be associated with reduced intake, weight loss, obstruction, inflammation and treatment-related nutritional problems. NCCN includes nutritional assessment and counseling in the advanced/metastatic work-up.

Nutritional assessment is therefore not an optional cosmetic add-on. It can affect treatment tolerance, functional status and quality of life.

PP-0223 provides the clinical interface and rationale. Detailed nutrition interventions and individualized prescriptions belong to dedicated nutrition/supportive-care packages.

### 9. Tumor biology and biomarkers

Modern metastatic gastric-cancer management is strongly influenced by tumor biology. NCCN v2.2026 identifies HER2, PD-L1, MSI/MMR and CLDN18.2 testing as relevant in advanced/metastatic disease when not previously performed, and states that NGS should be considered through a validated assay.

These tests do not replace clinical assessment. A biomarker result identifies a biological feature that may open or narrow treatment options; the final choice also depends on disease state, prior treatment, performance status, contraindications, availability and patient goals.

Detailed biomarker testing and each therapy-specific pathway are intentionally delegated to downstream packages.

### 10. Systemic therapy as the central disease-directed strategy

When metastatic disease is established and local therapy is not the principal approach, systemic therapy is the central disease-directed modality. NCI PDQ describes stage IV/inoperable/recurrent gastric cancer treatment options as including cytotoxic therapy, targeted therapy, immunotherapy and palliative locoregional therapy. NCCN GAST-9 places systemic therapy within the palliative-management pathway for appropriate patients.

PP-0223 owns the architecture rather than the regimen list. It explains why systemic therapy is central, why treatment may occur in successive lines, and why therapy selection changes with biomarkers, prior treatment, disease behavior and patient fitness.

Specific regimens, dosing and line-by-line treatment choices belong to PP-0208 and the therapy-specific packages.

### 11. Treatment course: first treatment, reassessment and progression

Metastatic disease management is usually longitudinal. A patient may receive an initial systemic treatment, undergo clinical and radiographic assessment, continue if benefit is maintained, or change strategy if the disease progresses or treatment is no longer appropriate.

This package deliberately does not reproduce RECIST criteria or detailed response-assessment rules. Instead, it explains the clinical concept of reassessment and adaptation.

The distinction is important: a treatment plan is not a single permanent decision. It is a sequence of decisions informed by disease behavior, treatment tolerance, new evidence and patient goals.

### 12. Selected local interventions

Systemic therapy does not mean that local treatment is never used. Selected patients may require local interventions for symptoms or complications, such as obstruction or bleeding, and selected metastatic patterns may be discussed in multidisciplinary settings.

NCI describes palliative locoregional approaches among treatment options for advanced disease. The appropriateness of a local procedure depends on the exact disease pattern and clinical purpose.

PP-0223 therefore teaches the principle of selected local intervention without creating a universal metastasectomy, ablation or radiation algorithm.

### 13. Clinical trials

Clinical trials are a legitimate component of metastatic gastric-cancer care. They may provide access to therapies or combinations that are not yet established as routine care, or answer questions about treatment strategies.

A clinical trial should not be presented as automatically better than standard care. Eligibility, scientific rationale, risks, alternatives and patient preferences all matter.

PP-0223 owns the concept of clinical-trial consideration; trial-specific evidence belongs to the relevant study or treatment package.

### 14. Palliative and supportive care

Metastatic gastric-cancer management includes symptom control, quality-of-life support and attention to patient goals. NCCN provides best supportive care as an explicit pathway and NCI describes palliative approaches for advanced disease.

Palliative care does not necessarily mean that anticancer treatment has stopped. It can be integrated with active disease-directed treatment.

PP-0223 establishes this interface while delegating detailed palliative-care and best-supportive-care protocols to PP-0227 and PP-0228.

### 15. Prognosis

Metastatic gastric cancer generally carries a more serious prognosis than disease confined to the stomach and regional nodes. However, the label M1 does not determine an individual's outcome.

Disease burden, metastatic distribution, tumor biology, performance status, symptoms, prior treatment and response all contribute to the clinical picture.

PP-0223 should therefore explain prognostic factors conceptually but must not provide individualized survival estimates or treatment predictions.

## Patient Explanation

Metastatic gastric cancer means that the gastric cancer has spread to a distant part of the body. In staging terminology this is M1 disease and is generally classified as Stage IV.

This does not mean that the cancer has become a different cancer. A tumor in the liver, lung, bone or another site may represent spread from the original gastric cancer.

The next questions are not only “Where has it spread?” but also “How much disease is present?”, “How is the patient feeling?”, “What is the tumor biology?”, “What treatment has already been given?”, and “What are the patient's goals?”

For many patients with established metastatic disease, treatment is focused on controlling the cancer, prolonging life when possible, and relieving symptoms. Systemic treatment is often central, while selected local procedures, clinical trials and supportive or palliative care may also have roles.

## Clinical Importance

Metastatic disease changes the clinical objective from managing disease confined to the stomach/regional field to managing cancer that has demonstrated the ability to spread distantly.

The metastatic label therefore has implications for staging, diagnostic work-up, biomarker characterization, treatment selection, reassessment and supportive care.

It is unsafe to reduce metastatic gastric cancer to a single treatment recommendation. The disease state is heterogeneous and treatment must be individualized.

## Key Concepts

- **M1:** distant metastatic disease in the TNM framework.
- **Stage IV:** gastric cancer with M1 disease.
- **Metastatic burden:** the extent and distribution of distant disease.
- **Performance status:** a standardized description of functional ability.
- **Biomarker:** a measurable feature of the tumor that may influence treatment options.
- **Systemic therapy:** treatment that acts throughout the body and is central to many metastatic-disease strategies.
- **Supportive care:** care directed at symptoms, function and quality of life.
- **Palliative care:** active prevention and relief of suffering that may accompany anticancer treatment.

## Treatment Architecture

The conceptual architecture is:

**Confirm metastatic disease**
→ **Characterize distribution and burden**
→ **Assess patient fitness and goals**
→ **Characterize tumor biology**
→ **Review previous treatment**

This is a knowledge architecture, not an individualized treatment algorithm.

## Patient Questions

### Does metastatic mean the cancer is incurable?

Established metastatic gastric cancer is usually managed as an advanced disease state in which treatment aims primarily at disease control and symptom relief, although individual circumstances differ. The term should not be interpreted as a guarantee that every patient has the same outcome.

### Can metastatic gastric cancer still be treated?

Yes. Depending on the patient's disease and health, treatment can include systemic therapy, selected local interventions, clinical trials and supportive/palliative care.

### Why are biomarkers tested?

Biomarkers can identify biological features that may affect which treatments are relevant. They are one part of treatment planning rather than the entire decision.

### Why does performance status matter?

Treatment can cause burdens and side effects. The team must judge whether a person is fit enough to benefit from active treatment and what intensity is appropriate.

### Does palliative care mean stopping cancer treatment?

No. Palliative care can be provided alongside active cancer treatment.

## Common Misconceptions

### Myth
“Metastatic gastric cancer means there is a new cancer in another organ.”

**Fact:** No. Metastatic gastric cancer means the gastric cancer has spread to a distant site. A distant lesion must be interpreted in clinical and pathological context.

### Myth
“Stage IV means there is nothing that can be done.”

**Fact:** No. Treatment may include systemic therapy, selected local interventions, clinical trials and active supportive/palliative care.

### Myth
“Every patient with metastatic gastric cancer gets the same treatment.”

**Fact:** No. Biomarkers, prior treatment, disease distribution, symptoms, performance status and patient goals affect management.

### Myth
“Metastatic disease always means chemotherapy only.”

**Fact:** No. Modern management can include chemotherapy, targeted therapy, immunotherapy, selected local approaches, clinical trials and supportive care.

### Myth
“A single metastasis should automatically be removed.”

**Fact:** No. Local treatment of metastatic disease is highly context-dependent and should be evaluated by the treating team, often through multidisciplinary review.

### Myth
“A biomarker result tells the doctor exactly which treatment to prescribe.”

**Fact:** No. Biomarkers inform treatment options but do not replace clinical judgment or patient-specific assessment.

### Myth
“Palliative care means cancer treatment has ended.”

**Fact:** No. Palliative care can be integrated with active cancer treatment.

### Myth
“Best supportive care means doing nothing.”

**Fact:** No. It actively addresses symptoms, suffering, function and quality of life.

## Detailed Clinical Reasoning

### Why “metastatic” is more than a location label

A metastatic diagnosis changes the clinical question from “Can the primary gastric tumor be treated locally?” to “How should a cancer that has demonstrated distant spread be controlled over time?”

The second question requires several dimensions at once:

1. **Where is the disease?**
2. **How extensive is it?**
3. **Is it causing symptoms or complications?**
4. **What is the biological profile of the tumor?**
5. **What treatment has already been given?**
6. **How fit is the patient for treatment?**
7. **What outcomes matter most to the patient?**

A high-quality knowledge product must therefore resist the temptation to define metastatic disease only by an M1 label.

### Why M1 does not describe every clinically relevant difference

Two people can both have M1 disease but have very different clinical situations.

One person may have a small number of asymptomatic lesions discovered on staging. Another may have extensive disease involving several organs, weight loss, bleeding, obstruction or other complications.

The stage label is the common starting point. The subsequent characterization provides the information needed for individualized clinical decision-making.

### Why metastatic disease is not automatically equivalent to “no treatment”

NCCN and NCI materials describe multiple management categories for advanced/metastatic disease. These include systemic treatment, selected local or palliative interventions, clinical trials and best supportive care.

The appropriate balance differs between patients.

The educational message should therefore be:

> Metastatic disease is serious, but “metastatic” does not mean “nothing can be done.”

### Why systemic therapy is central

A distant metastatic site is outside the anatomical field that can normally be controlled by treating the stomach alone.

Systemic treatment can reach disease throughout the body and is therefore a major component of treatment for many patients with unresectable/metastatic disease.

This does not mean that local treatment has no role. It means that local procedures are selected according to the clinical purpose rather than being assumed to control all disease.

## Disease Characterization Framework

### Domain 1 — Confirm the disease state

The first task is to determine whether suspicious distant findings truly represent metastatic gastric cancer.

Possible inputs include:

- cross-sectional imaging;
- FDG-PET/CT in selected circumstances;
- pathology from the primary tumor;
- biopsy of a metastatic site when clinically indicated;
- clinical history and examination.

A radiologically suspicious lesion is not automatically equivalent to a pathologically proven metastasis in every situation.

### Domain 2 — Map the distribution

The team needs to understand:

- the primary tumor;
- regional nodes;
- distant organs;
- peritoneal involvement;
- number of involved compartments;
- measurable disease;
- symptomatic disease;
- complications.

This creates the disease map used for treatment planning.

### Domain 3 — Characterize the patient

Important variables include:

- performance status;
- organ function;
- nutritional status;
- weight trajectory;
- comorbidity;
- symptoms;
- previous treatment toxicity;
- patient goals.

This prevents the disease from being treated as if every patient were biologically and functionally identical.

### Domain 4 — Characterize tumor biology

Advanced/metastatic disease is a setting in which biomarker information can have direct treatment relevance.

The supplied NCCN source identifies:

- HER2;
- PD-L1;
- MSI/MMR;
- CLDN18.2;
- consideration of NGS.

The package intentionally stops before the therapy-specific interpretation of each biomarker.

### Domain 5 — Review treatment history

Treatment history may include:

- previous gastrectomy;
- perioperative chemotherapy;
- neoadjuvant or adjuvant treatment;
- chemoradiation;
- prior targeted therapy;
- prior immunotherapy;
- prior toxicity;
- previous response or progression.

The same metastatic finding can therefore lead to different treatment considerations depending on what the patient has already received.

## Metastatic Site Orientation

### Liver

The liver is an important distant metastatic site in gastric cancer.

At PP-0223 level, the key point is that liver metastasis represents distant disease and may influence overall disease burden and treatment planning.

Detailed liver-directed management is not owned here.

### Lung

Pulmonary metastases represent another distant metastatic pattern.

A lung lesion in a patient with gastric cancer may have several possible explanations, so interpretation requires clinical and imaging/pathological context rather than automatic classification.

### Bone

Bone involvement may cause pain or structural complications.

The cross-cutting PP message is that symptomatic metastatic sites may require local symptom-directed treatment in addition to systemic disease management.

### Brain

Brain metastasis is a recognized but less common metastatic pattern in gastric cancer.

The package should acknowledge it without implying that it is the dominant presentation of metastatic gastric cancer.

### Ovary

Ovarian metastasis is a recognized metastatic pattern and has dedicated project-level conceptual coverage.

Detailed ovarian-specific management is delegated.

### Distant lymph nodes

Distant nodal disease may represent metastatic spread depending on the staging framework and anatomical context.

The important patient-facing principle is that not all lymph nodes have the same staging meaning.

### Peritoneum

Peritoneal metastasis deserves special treatment because it can represent a major component of metastatic gastric cancer and has a dedicated downstream pathway.

PP-0223 owns recognition and orientation.

PP-0224–0226 own detailed peritoneal disease, isolated peritoneal disease and HIPEC.

## Disease Burden

“Metastatic” is binary in the sense that M1 is either established or not established, but clinical disease burden is not binary.

Burden may involve:

- number of lesions;
- size;
- number of organs involved;
- peritoneal extent;
- progression rate;
- symptoms;
- organ compromise.

The package must not create an unsupported numerical burden threshold for treatment.

## Treatment Intent

Treatment intent should be explained as a clinical concept.

In established metastatic disease, the primary intent for many patients is:

- disease control;
- prolongation of life where possible;
- symptom relief;
- preservation of function;
- quality of life.

This should not be turned into a promise that every patient will achieve a particular outcome.

## Longitudinal Nature of Metastatic Disease

Metastatic management is usually a sequence rather than one decision.

A conceptual sequence is:

**Baseline characterization**
→ **Initial treatment**
→ **Clinical/radiographic reassessment**
→ **Continue if benefit remains**
→ **Change treatment if progression/intolerance**
→ **Subsequent strategy**
→ **Supportive and palliative integration throughout**

The response-assessment details belong to PP-0217 and PP-0218.

## Common Clinical Misinterpretations to Avoid

### “M1 is the treatment plan.”

No. M1 identifies distant metastatic disease. The treatment plan requires further characterization.

### “A biomarker is the treatment.”

No. A biomarker is evidence about tumor biology. Treatment selection requires broader clinical context.

### “More treatment is always better.”

No. Treatment benefit must be balanced against toxicity, function, quality of life and patient goals.

### “Local treatment has no role.”

No. Selected local interventions may be used for symptoms, complications or highly selected disease patterns.

### “Supportive care is separate from cancer care.”

No. Supportive and palliative care can be integrated throughout metastatic treatment.

1. Where has the cancer spread?

3. How much disease is present?

These questions support shared decision-making without replacing individualized clinical assessment.

## Final Clinical Message

Metastatic gastric cancer should be understood as a complex disease state requiring integrated assessment of tumor, disease, patient and treatment history.

The most important educational transition is:

**“Where has the cancer spread?”**

to

**“What does the complete metastatic disease picture mean for this patient?”**

That second question is where individualized oncology care begins, and it is intentionally beyond the scope of a general Population Package.
