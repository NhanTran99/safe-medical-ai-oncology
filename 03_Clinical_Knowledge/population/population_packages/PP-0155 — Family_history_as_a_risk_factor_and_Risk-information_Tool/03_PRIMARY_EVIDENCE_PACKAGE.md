# Clinical Question

**Primary Educational Question**

> **How can a family history of gastric cancer affect a person's risk, what features of that family history are most informative, and what can family history tell us about the need for further risk assessment?**

---

# Educational Intent

Provide patients and caregivers with an accurate, evidence-based explanation of **family history as both a gastric-cancer risk factor and a clinical risk-information tool**.

The package explains that family history can increase concern about gastric-cancer risk, especially when close relatives are affected, and that the usefulness of family history depends on details such as the number and degree of affected relatives, generations involved, age at diagnosis, cancer type or histology, and other relevant family cancers or conditions.

The package also establishes a critical boundary:

> **Family history is evidence about risk and family pattern; it is not, by itself, a diagnosis of a hereditary cancer syndrome.**

---

# Scope

## Included

- Family history as a recognized gastric-cancer risk factor.
- First-, second-, and third-degree relatives.
- Three-generation family history.
- Maternal and paternal lineage.
- Number and clustering of affected relatives.
- Multiple generations.
- Age at diagnosis.
- Primary cancer site and histology.
- Other relevant cancers and gastrointestinal polyps.
- Accuracy and verification of family history.
- Familial versus hereditary risk.
- Shared genetic and non-genetic explanations for familial clustering.
- Family history as an input to further risk assessment.
- Limitations of family history for individualized risk prediction.

## Excluded

- Formal hereditary gastric-cancer risk-assessment algorithms.
- Exact HDGC genetic-testing criteria.
- CDH1 and CTNNA1 interpretation.
- Genetic testing methodology.
- Genetic counseling.
- Cascade testing.
- HDGC-like family surveillance.
- Risk-reducing gastrectomy.
- Endoscopic surveillance schedules.
- Gastric-cancer screening recommendations.
- H. pylori testing or eradication.
- Individualized numerical cancer-risk calculation.
- Treatment recommendations.

# Evidence Hierarchy

## Level I

### NCI — *Genetics of Gastric Cancer (PDQ®)*

Direct gastric-cancer-specific evidence.

Key support:

- detailed three-generation family history is integral to gastric-cancer risk assessment;
- relevant information includes number of relatives, cancer types/histology, age at diagnosis, and gastrointestinal polyp history;
- family histories may be incomplete or inaccurate;
- pathology documentation can help confirm relevant histology;
- some gastric-cancer histologies are particularly informative for hereditary-risk assessment;
- family history can be associated with substantially increased gastric-cancer risk.

Relevant source locations: pp. 12–13 of the project PDF.

### NCI — *Causes of Stomach Cancer*

Direct patient-facing gastric-cancer risk-factor evidence.

Key support:

- family history is a non-modifiable gastric-cancer risk factor;
- having a first-degree relative with stomach cancer is associated with increased risk;
- a risk factor does not mean that cancer will necessarily develop.

Relevant source locations: pp. 1–3 of the project PDF.

### NCI — *Cancer Genetics Risk Assessment and Counseling (PDQ®)*

General cancer-genetics evidence used for family-history structure and interpretation.

Key support:

- family history is an essential component of cancer risk assessment;
- a three-generation pedigree should include first- and second-degree relatives on both maternal and paternal sides;
- third-degree relatives may also be included;
- clinically relevant information includes primary site, age at diagnosis, pathology, treatment history, genetic testing results, and other relevant factors;
- inaccurate family history can affect risk assessment and downstream clinical decisions;
- multiple close relatives and multiple generations can raise suspicion for hereditary susceptibility.

Relevant source locations: pp. 16–21 and p. 26 of the project PDF.

# Evidence Notes

## 1. Family history is a risk factor, not a diagnosis

NCI explicitly classifies family history as a non-modifiable risk factor for stomach cancer and identifies a first-degree relative with stomach cancer as a specific risk-associated feature.

The package therefore uses the language:

> **“Family history can increase risk.”**

It does not use:

> **“Family history means you have hereditary gastric cancer.”**

This distinction is central to the package boundary.

---

## 2. Family history is most useful when characterized in detail

The gastric-genetics PDQ recommends collecting information on:

- number of relatives with cancers on the same side of the family;
- types of cancers and histological subtype;
- age at diagnosis;
- gastrointestinal polyp history;
- and other relevant family features.

This supports the package's emphasis on family history as a **risk-information tool**, rather than a yes/no field.

---

## 3. Degree of relatedness matters

The cancer-genetics PDQ defines first-, second-, and third-degree relatives and recommends a three-generation family history covering maternal and paternal sides.

The CKO uses these categories educationally but does not convert them into a standalone testing algorithm.

---

## 4. Family pattern matters

Multiple cancers in close relatives, especially across multiple generations, can increase suspicion for hereditary cancer susceptibility.

This is presented as a **pattern-recognition principle**, not as a diagnostic criterion.

Formal syndrome-specific criteria are delegated to the relevant Population Packages.

---

## 5. Age and histology add information

The gastric-genetics PDQ notes that younger age at cancer diagnosis can be a hallmark of hereditary cancer syndromes and that certain gastric histologies, including signet-ring cell and diffuse gastric cancer patterns, can be particularly relevant.

The package therefore teaches that:

> **Who was affected + what cancer they had + when they developed it**

is more informative than the simple statement:

> **“Someone in my family had stomach cancer.”**

---

## 6. Family-history accuracy is a clinical issue

The gastric-genetics PDQ reports that self-reported gastric-cancer histories can be inaccurate and that only a minority to a majority of reported stomach-cancer diagnoses were verified in the cited studies.

The general cancer-genetics PDQ emphasizes that family-history accuracy can directly affect risk assessment and downstream clinical recommendations.

Therefore the package explicitly recommends confirmation of important family-history information when possible.

---

## 7. Family history does not establish a genetic mutation

A family may have multiple gastric-cancer cases without an identified pathogenic germline variant.

Family clustering can reflect inherited susceptibility, shared exposures, or a combination of factors.

The package therefore uses family history as a **risk signal** and **information source**, not as a substitute for genetic evaluation.

---

# Clinical Claims Summary

The evidence consistently supports the following educational messages:

- Family history is a recognized risk factor for gastric cancer.
- A first-degree relative with gastric cancer is important risk information.
- Family-history interpretation should consider several generations.
- Both maternal and paternal family histories matter.
- Number of affected relatives, degree of relatedness, age at diagnosis, cancer type, and histology can add important context.
- Other cancers and gastrointestinal polyps may also be relevant.
- Family-history information is not always accurate and should be verified when clinically important.
- Family history can raise concern for hereditary susceptibility but does not itself establish a hereditary syndrome.
- Family history is best used as a **risk-information tool** that may guide further assessment.

---

# Evidence Consistency Review

No clinically meaningful disagreement was identified among the project sources for the core educational claims.

The NCI gastric-genetics material, NCI cancer-genetics risk-assessment material, NCI gastric-cancer risk-factor material, and ACS patient education materials are consistent in treating family history as clinically relevant risk information.

The main evidence boundary is that **family history alone does not provide a universal individualized risk estimate and does not automatically establish a hereditary syndrome**.

The package therefore avoids universal numerical risk claims and avoids converting family-history patterns into syndrome-specific testing or management recommendations.

---

# Evidence Gaps

The current project source set does not justify:

- one universal percentage increase in gastric-cancer risk for every family-history pattern;
- a single family-history threshold that automatically establishes hereditary cancer;
- a universal screening schedule for people with family history alone;
- individualized risk prediction from family history alone;
- automatic genetic testing for every person with a family history of gastric cancer.

These topics require context-specific guidelines, formal risk models, or dedicated downstream Population Packages.
