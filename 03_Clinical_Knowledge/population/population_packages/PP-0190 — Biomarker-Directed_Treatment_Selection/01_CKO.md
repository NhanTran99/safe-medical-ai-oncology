# Educational Objectives

After reading this Population Package, the reader should be able to:

1. Explain what biomarker-directed treatment selection means in gastric adenocarcinoma.
2. Understand the difference between a biomarker result, clinical relevance, actionability, treatment eligibility, and an actual treatment decision.
3. Explain why biomarker information is interpreted together with disease setting, treatment line, prior treatment, clinical condition, and patient goals.
4. Recognize that different biomarkers can direct different treatment pathways.
5. Recognize HER2-positive status as a clinically important basis for considering HER2-directed treatment in appropriate advanced gastric/EGJ adenocarcinoma settings.
6. Recognize CLDN18.2-positive status as a basis for considering CLDN18.2-directed treatment in the appropriate untreated unresectable locally advanced, recurrent, or metastatic setting.
7. Recognize selected molecular alterations such as NTRK gene fusions, BRAF V600E mutations, and RET gene fusions as findings that may support targeted-treatment consideration in selected circumstances.
8. Understand that a biomarker result can be clinically important even when it does not lead to an immediate or universal treatment recommendation.
9. Understand that a negative biomarker result generally removes or weakens support for a particular biomarker-directed option rather than meaning that no treatment exists.
10. Understand that some biomarkers, including PD-L1 and MSI-H/dMMR, primarily direct immunotherapy pathways and therefore belong to a separate treatment-selection branch.
11. Understand that targeted treatments may be combined with chemotherapy or, in selected settings, with other systemic treatments.
12. Understand why treatment line, previous therapy, and performance status can change the relevance of a biomarker-directed option.
13. Understand why treatment selection can require integration of more than one biomarker.
14. Use a structured set of questions when discussing a biomarker-directed treatment option with the cancer care team.
15. Recognize that the molecular or biomarker report informs treatment selection but is not itself a treatment order.

# Scope

## Primary Educational Question

> **How do biomarker results help determine which targeted treatment options may be considered for gastric adenocarcinoma?**

This Population Package is the **clinical-application bridge** between biomarker testing/result interpretation and downstream targeted-treatment packages.

The package explains how a validated biomarker result can become clinically relevant to targeted-treatment selection while preserving the distinction between:

**biomarker result → clinical relevance → potential treatment option → treatment-context assessment → shared clinical decision.**

It does not provide individualized treatment recommendations.

## Clinical-Educational Intent

The purpose of PP-0190 is not to repeat how biomarkers are measured. Its purpose is to explain what happens **after an interpretable biomarker result is available**.

A patient may have a report saying:

- HER2-positive;
- CLDN18.2-positive;
- NTRK fusion-positive;
- BRAF V600E-mutated;
- RET fusion-positive;
- or another molecular finding.

The central educational question is then:

> **What does this result change about the treatment options that may be considered?**

The answer is context-dependent. A biomarker can identify a potential treatment opportunity, but the care team must still consider disease extent, treatment line, prior therapy, clinical condition, treatment goals, and the evidence/guideline context.

# Clinical Knowledge Blocks

## Block 1 — What Does Biomarker-Directed Treatment Selection Mean?

A biomarker is a measurable characteristic of a cancer that can provide information about how the cancer behaves or how it may respond to a particular treatment.

In precision oncology, a biomarker result can therefore become part of treatment selection.

The basic logic is:

**Biomarker testing**

↓

**Biomarker result**

↓

**Is the result clinically relevant?**

↓

**Is there an evidence-supported treatment associated with it?**

↓

**Does that treatment apply in the current clinical setting?**

↓

**What other clinical factors affect the decision?**

↓

**Treatment decision with the cancer care team**

The most important principle is:

> **A biomarker can direct consideration of a treatment without prescribing the treatment by itself.**

NCI patient-facing treatment information states that biomarker tests may help predict response to targeted therapy and that treatment decisions are made in the broader context of the patient's cancer and care plan.

---

## Block 2 — Detection Is Not the Same as Actionability

A molecular finding can be detected without being an established treatment target.

For example, a broad molecular test may identify many alterations. Some may have:

- established treatment relevance;
- possible relevance in selected circumstances;
- prognostic significance without a targeted treatment;
- uncertain significance;
- no currently established clinical action.

Therefore:

**Detected**

does not automatically mean

**Actionable**

and

**Actionable**

does not automatically mean

**Treatment must be given.**

This distinction is essential to safe patient-facing precision-oncology education.

---

## Block 3 — What Makes a Biomarker Clinically Actionable?

In this package, an actionable biomarker is a finding for which there is a clinically meaningful management implication in a defined context, such as an evidence-supported treatment option.

Actionability depends on more than the laboratory label.

It may depend on:

- the cancer type;
- disease stage or setting;
- exact biomarker definition;
- assay validity;
- treatment line;
- prior treatment;
- current clinical condition;
- guideline recommendations;
- evidence supporting the treatment;
- treatment availability and feasibility.

Thus:

> **Actionability is contextual, not absolute.**

---

## Block 4 — Why the Same Biomarker Can Lead to Different Decisions

A biomarker result does not exist independently of the clinical situation.

For example, NCCN places HER2-directed treatment in specific advanced/recurrent/metastatic treatment pathways and distinguishes first-line treatment from later-line treatment.

NCCN also states that second-line or subsequent therapy is dependent on prior therapy and performance status.

Therefore, the relevant question is not simply:

> “Is the tumor HER2-positive?”

It is:

> **“Is the tumor HER2-positive, and does the patient currently have a clinical situation in which a HER2-directed option is appropriate?”**

The same principle applies to other biomarkers.

---

## Block 5 — HER2 as a Treatment-Selection Biomarker

HER2 is one of the clearest examples of biomarker-directed targeted treatment in gastric cancer.

NCCN recommends HER2 assessment when advanced/metastatic disease is documented or suspected and describes validated HER2 assessment using IHC with ISH/FISH or another appropriate ISH method.

For advanced HER2 overexpression-positive adenocarcinoma, NCCN identifies trastuzumab-containing first-line treatment as a preferred pathway.

NCCN also lists fam-trastuzumab deruxtecan-nxki as a preferred second-line/subsequent option for HER2-positive adenocarcinoma.

The patient-facing logic is therefore:

**HER2 result**

↓

**HER2-positive**

↓

**HER2-directed treatment may be relevant**

↓

**Check disease setting, treatment line, prior therapy and clinical context**

↓

**Select an appropriate treatment option with the care team**

The detailed testing pathway belongs to the HER2 testing package, while the detailed treatment content belongs to the HER2-targeted therapy package.

---

## Block 6 — Why HER2 Testing Must Precede HER2-Directed Treatment

ACS explains that HER2-targeted drugs work when cancer cells have increased HER2 and that tumor samples therefore need HER2 testing before treatment.

This illustrates the broader principle:

> **A targeted treatment requires a sufficiently established target.**

The treatment decision therefore depends on the quality and clinical validity of the biomarker result.

PP-0190 does not teach how the laboratory performs or scores the HER2 test. It teaches how the result enters treatment selection.

---

## Block 7 — CLDN18.2 as a Treatment-Selection Biomarker

CLDN18.2 provides another clear example.

NCCN defines CLDN18.2 positivity for patients with untreated inoperable locally advanced, recurrent, or metastatic gastric adenocarcinoma for whom zolbetuximab is being considered.

The NCCN pathway places fluoropyrimidine/oxaliplatin plus zolbetuximab among preferred first-line options for appropriate CLDN18.2-positive, HER2-negative advanced disease.

The patient-facing logic is:

**CLDN18.2 testing**

↓

**CLDN18.2-positive result**

↓

**zolbetuximab-containing treatment may be considered**

↓

**confirm that the clinical setting matches the treatment pathway**

↓

**consider other clinical factors and treatment alternatives**

This is an important example because it demonstrates that a biomarker result can identify a treatment option while still requiring a broader clinical decision.

---

## Block 8 — Why Biomarker Status and Clinical Setting Must Be Read Together

CLDN18.2 is particularly useful for illustrating this point.

A CLDN18.2 result does not mean that zolbetuximab is appropriate at every stage of gastric cancer.

The source-supported treatment pathway is tied to a specific clinical setting.

This prevents a common misunderstanding:

> **“Positive biomarker” is not equivalent to “treatment at any time.”**

The clinical setting remains part of the meaning of the biomarker.

---

## Block 9 — NTRK Gene Fusions as Selected Actionable Findings

NTRK gene fusions are less common in gastric cancer but are an important example of molecularly directed treatment.

NCCN lists entrectinib, larotrectinib, or repotrectinib as options useful in certain circumstances for NTRK 1/2/3 gene fusion-positive tumors.

ACS similarly describes TRK inhibitors for advanced cancers with NTRK gene changes that remain progressive despite other treatments.

This demonstrates an important precision-oncology principle:

> **A clinically useful targeted treatment may depend on a molecular alteration rather than on the most common gastric-cancer biomarker categories.**

The package does not attempt to teach NTRK assay methodology or detailed TRK-inhibitor management.

---

## Block 10 — BRAF V600E as a Selected Actionable Alteration

NCCN lists dabrafenib plus trametinib as an option useful in certain circumstances for BRAF V600E-mutated tumors.

This is another example in which:

**molecular alteration**

↓

**matched targeted therapy**

may occur in a selected treatment context.

The key patient-facing message is not the drug regimen itself.

It is:

> **A specific molecular alteration can create a treatment opportunity when the alteration and clinical context match an evidence-supported targeted option.**

---

## Block 11 — RET Gene Fusions as a Selected Actionable Alteration

NCCN lists selpercatinib as an option useful in certain circumstances for RET gene fusion-positive tumors.

ACS similarly describes selpercatinib as a targeted option for advanced cancers with RET gene changes that remain progressive despite other treatment.

Again, the package uses this as a representative example of molecularly directed treatment rather than a complete drug catalogue.

---

## Block 12 — Not Every Treatment-Directing Biomarker Is a Targeted-Therapy Biomarker

This is a critical boundary.

Some biomarkers influence treatment through an **immunotherapy** pathway rather than a conventional targeted-therapy pathway.

Examples include:

- PD-L1;
- MSI-H/dMMR;
- TMB in relevant contexts.

NCCN places these findings within immunotherapy treatment pathways.

Therefore:

**HER2 / CLDN18.2 / NTRK / BRAF V600E / RET**

are representative of the targeted-treatment branch covered here.

Whereas:

**PD-L1 / MSI-H/dMMR / TMB**

are primarily delegated to PP-0191 for biomarker-directed immunotherapy selection.

This distinction prevents PP-0190 from becoming an overlapping immunotherapy package.

---

## Block 13 — Why Multiple Biomarkers May Need to Be Considered Together

Gastric cancer treatment selection can involve more than one biomarker.

For example, NCCN distinguishes treatment pathways according to:

- HER2 status;
- PD-L1 status;
- CLDN18.2 status;
- MSI-H/dMMR status.

A patient may therefore have several biomarker results that need to be interpreted together.

The correct conceptual approach is:

> **Do not read one biomarker in isolation when the treatment pathway depends on several pieces of information.**

PP-0190 does not establish a universal priority order among biomarkers.

Instead, it teaches the need for integrated clinical interpretation.

---

## Block 14 — Positive Does Not Mean Automatic Treatment

A positive biomarker result can open a treatment pathway.

It does not automatically mean:

- the treatment is mandatory;
- it is the only treatment option;
- it is appropriate at every disease stage;
- it is appropriate at every treatment line;
- the treatment will definitely work;
- the treatment is available or feasible for every patient.

A positive result is best understood as:

> **evidence that a particular treatment option may be clinically relevant in the appropriate context.**

---

## Block 15 — Negative Does Not Mean “No Treatment”

A negative biomarker result can be clinically meaningful because it may remove support for a biomarker-specific option.

For example:

- HER2-negative disease does not follow the HER2-positive trastuzumab pathway;
- CLDN18.2-negative disease does not support the CLDN18.2-positive zolbetuximab pathway.

But a negative result does not mean:

> “There are no treatment options.”

Other biomarker pathways or non-biomarker-directed treatments may remain appropriate.

---

## Block 16 — First-Line Versus Later-Line Treatment

Treatment line is a major part of biomarker-directed selection.

NCCN identifies different options for:

- first-line advanced disease;
- second-line therapy;
- subsequent therapy.

The later-line pathway is explicitly dependent on prior therapy and performance status.

This means that the same molecular finding can have different implications depending on what treatment the patient has already received.

The patient should therefore ask:

> **“Does this biomarker-directed treatment apply at my current treatment line?”**

---

## Block 17 — Combination Treatment

Targeted therapy is not necessarily used alone.

ACS explains that targeted therapy for stomach cancer can sometimes be used alone or with chemotherapy depending on the situation.

Examples include:

- trastuzumab with chemotherapy in HER2-positive advanced disease;
- zolbetuximab with chemotherapy in CLDN18.2-positive advanced disease.

NCCN also identifies combinations in which a targeted treatment may coexist with an immunotherapy component in selected biomarker-defined settings.

The key concept is:

> **The biomarker may identify one component of a treatment strategy rather than a complete treatment plan by itself.**

Detailed regimen selection and toxicity management belong to downstream treatment packages.

---

## Block 18 — What Happens When There Is No Clearly Matched Targeted Treatment?

A molecular report can contain:

- no actionable alteration;
- an alteration without an established treatment;
- a finding relevant mainly to prognosis;
- a VUS;
- a result that requires additional confirmation.

In these situations, the absence of a matched targeted therapy does not mean that treatment has failed conceptually.

It means:

> **The available biomarker information does not currently establish a specific targeted-treatment pathway.**

Other treatment approaches, clinical trials, or additional clinical assessment may remain relevant.

Detailed treatment alternatives belong to the appropriate downstream treatment packages.

---

## Block 19 — The Role of NGS in Biomarker-Directed Selection

NCCN states that IHC/ISH/targeted gene PCR is preferred initially for specified biomarkers, while validated NGS may be considered later in selected patients with sufficient tumor tissue.

NCCN also describes NGS as capable of assessing multiple mutations and molecular events simultaneously.

This creates an important distinction:

**NGS is a testing approach.**

**Biomarker-directed treatment selection is a clinical application.**

PP-0187 owns the former.

PP-0190 owns the bridge from a clinically interpretable result to treatment relevance.

---

## Block 20 — What About ctDNA?

NCCN describes ctDNA as a blood-based approach that can identify genomic alterations, including mutations/alterations or gene fusions, and notes that these findings may identify targetable alterations or tumor-clone evolution.

However, ctDNA testing has its own scope and limitations.

PP-0190 may therefore recognize a validated ctDNA-derived actionable result as part of the broader biomarker ecosystem, but it does not own:

- ctDNA biology;
- technical ctDNA testing;
- longitudinal ctDNA monitoring;
- MRD;
- ctDNA-specific treatment-switch algorithms.

Those topics remain delegated to the dedicated ctDNA/liquid-biopsy architecture.

---

## Block 21 — Treatment Selection Is a Clinical Decision, Not a Laboratory Decision

The molecular laboratory answers questions such as:

> “What biomarker or alteration was detected?”

The clinical team must answer:

> “What does that result mean for this patient's treatment options?”

That second question requires integration with:

- diagnosis;
- disease extent;
- pathology;
- treatment history;
- performance status;
- organ function and other clinical considerations;
- other biomarkers;
- treatment benefits and risks;
- patient goals and preferences.

Therefore:

> **The biomarker report informs treatment selection; it does not independently make the treatment decision.**

---

## Block 22 — Patient Explanation: A Simple Decision Path

A patient can think about biomarker-directed treatment selection as five questions:

### Question 1
**What biomarker result do I have?**

### Question 2
**Is this biomarker linked to a treatment option for my cancer?**

### Question 3
**Does that treatment apply to my current disease setting and treatment line?**

### Question 4
**Do my other biomarker results or previous treatments change the options?**

### Question 5
**What are the benefits, risks, alternatives, and goals of treatment for me?**

The answers should come from the treating oncology team using the current clinical record and validated test results.

# Clinical Importance

Biomarker-directed treatment selection is one of the central practical applications of precision oncology.

Its importance is not simply that more tests are available.

The important change is that treatment can increasingly be selected according to **specific biological features of an individual tumor**.

In gastric adenocarcinoma, the project Source Materials document multiple examples:

- HER2;
- CLDN18.2;
- NTRK;
- BRAF V600E;
- RET;
- and separate immunotherapy-related biomarkers such as PD-L1, MSI-H/dMMR, and TMB.

The clinical value of a biomarker therefore depends on whether the result changes what treatments can reasonably be considered.

The package should preserve the following conceptual distinction:

> **Biomarker testing answers “what does the tumor show?”**

> **Biomarker-directed treatment selection asks “does that finding change the treatment options?”**

> **Treatment-specific packages answer “what is the treatment and how is it used?”**

# Common Misconceptions

## Myth 1

**“If my biomarker is positive, I automatically need the matching drug.”**

**Fact:**  
A positive biomarker can identify a potentially relevant treatment option, but appropriateness depends on disease setting, treatment line, prior therapy, clinical condition, and other factors.

## Myth 2

**“If my biomarker is negative, there is no treatment.”**

**Fact:**  
A negative result may only mean that one biomarker-directed option is not supported. Other treatment pathways may remain.

## Myth 3

**“Actionable means the treatment will definitely work.”**

**Fact:**  
Actionability means that the finding has a clinically meaningful implication or treatment option. It does not guarantee response.

## Myth 4

**“A molecular result chooses the treatment.”**

**Fact:**  
The result informs treatment selection. The oncology team integrates it with the rest of the clinical situation.

## Myth 5

**“All targeted therapies work alone.”**

**Fact:**  
Some targeted treatments are used with chemotherapy or other systemic treatments.

## Myth 6

**“Every molecular alteration found by NGS is actionable.”**

**Fact:**  
NGS can identify many alterations. Only some have an established treatment implication.

## Myth 7

**“The same biomarker always means the same treatment.”**

**Fact:**  
Treatment depends on disease setting, treatment line, prior treatment, and other clinical factors.

## Myth 8

**“HER2-positive means trastuzumab is always appropriate.”**

**Fact:**  
HER2 status is important for identifying a HER2-directed treatment pathway, but the appropriate regimen depends on the clinical setting and treatment context.

## Myth 9

**“CLDN18.2-positive means zolbetuximab is appropriate for every gastric cancer patient.”**

**Fact:**  
The source-supported zolbetuximab pathway applies to defined clinical settings, particularly untreated unresectable locally advanced, recurrent, or metastatic disease.

## Myth 10

**“A rare alteration such as NTRK or RET is irrelevant because it is not a common gastric-cancer biomarker.”**

**Fact:**  
Selected rare molecular alterations can identify targeted-treatment opportunities in appropriate circumstances.

## Myth 11

**“PD-L1, MSI-H/dMMR, and HER2 all belong to the same treatment-selection pathway.”**

**Fact:**  
They can all affect treatment selection, but they connect to different therapeutic branches. PP-0190 focuses on targeted treatment; PP-0191 owns the immunotherapy-selection branch.

## Myth 12

**“A biomarker test is only useful after treatment stops working.”**

**Fact:**  
NCCN recommends or considers biomarker testing at defined points in gastric-cancer evaluation and treatment planning, including HER2 and CLDN18.2 assessment in appropriate advanced/metastatic settings and broader molecular testing in selected circumstances.

# Clinical Safety Boundary

This package is educational.

It does not determine whether an individual patient should receive:

- trastuzumab;
- trastuzumab deruxtecan;
- zolbetuximab;
- a TRK inhibitor;
- dabrafenib/trametinib;
