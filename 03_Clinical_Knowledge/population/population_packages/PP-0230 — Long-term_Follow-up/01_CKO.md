# Educational Objectives

After reading this Population Package, the reader should be able to:

1. Explain what long-term follow-up means after gastric cancer treatment.
2. Understand why follow-up is broader than cancer surveillance alone.
3. Understand that follow-up changes over time and is adapted to the patient's treatment history and clinical needs.
4. Recognize the major domains assessed during follow-up, including symptoms, physical findings, clinically indicated laboratory testing, disease status, treatment effects, nutrition, function and general health.
5. Understand how follow-up interfaces with surveillance and recurrence evaluation without treating them as identical concepts.
6. Understand why post-gastrectomy patients may require long-term or lifelong monitoring for nutritional and gastrointestinal sequelae.
7. Understand the distinction between gastric-cancer-specific surveillance and ongoing medical/survivorship care after five years.
8. Understand the role of oncology, primary care and other specialists in longitudinal care.
9. Recognize common misconceptions about what follow-up does and does not mean.
10. Know which questions are appropriate to discuss with the treating team.

# Clinical Knowledge Blocks

## Block 1 — What Is Long-term Follow-up?

Long-term follow-up is the continuing process of assessing a person after gastric cancer treatment. It is not limited to checking whether cancer has returned.

Follow-up can address several questions at the same time:

- Is there evidence suggesting recurrent disease?
- Are there symptoms or physical findings that need further evaluation?
- Are there lasting effects from surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy or other treatment?
- Are nutritional or gastrointestinal problems developing?
- Does the patient need preventive or routine medical care?
- Does the patient need oncology, primary-care or specialist support?

The central concept is **continuity of care over time**.

Follow-up therefore sits above several more specialized knowledge nodes. Surveillance, recurrence evaluation, treatment-sequela management and survivorship are related components, but they are not interchangeable.

---

## Block 2 — Why Is Follow-up Needed After Treatment?

Completing cancer treatment does not necessarily mean that all healthcare needs associated with the cancer or its treatment have ended.

The follow-up pathway exists because different clinically important events may occur at different times.

Some gastric-cancer recurrences occur relatively early after local treatment, while a smaller number may occur later. NCCN notes that most relapses occur within the first two years and almost all by five years, but potentially actionable relapses can occasionally occur after five years. For that reason, additional follow-up after five years may be considered according to risk factors and comorbidities.

At the same time, some treatment effects can persist or appear later. This is especially relevant after gastrectomy, where nutritional and gastrointestinal consequences may require long-term or lifelong monitoring.

Thus:

**cancer-specific follow-up needs**

and

**long-term health needs**

may continue on different timelines.

---

## Block 3 — Follow-up Is Not the Same as Surveillance

Surveillance is an important component of post-treatment follow-up, but follow-up is broader.

### Surveillance asks:

> What cancer-related tests or assessments are used to look for evidence of recurrent or residual disease?

### Long-term follow-up asks:

> What needs to be reassessed over time, and how is ongoing care coordinated?

A follow-up encounter may therefore include:

- symptom review;
- history and physical examination;
- clinically indicated blood tests;
- surveillance investigations;
- nutritional assessment;
- treatment-sequela assessment;
- preventive care;
- psychosocial or functional assessment;
- coordination with other healthcare providers.

The detailed surveillance schedule and modality-specific recommendations are owned by **PP-0220 — Surveillance After Gastric Cancer Treatment**.

---

## Block 4 — How Does Follow-up Change Over Time?

NCCN provides a time-based follow-up framework for several gastric-cancer post-treatment groups.

For relevant patients, history and physical examination are recommended every 3–6 months for the first 1–2 years, followed by every 6–12 months for years 3–5. Other investigations depend on treatment type, stage, symptoms and clinical indication.

The important educational point is not that every patient follows one identical timetable.

Instead:

**Early follow-up**

→ closer clinical reassessment

**Intermediate follow-up**

→ progressively less frequent cancer-specific assessment for many patients

**Later follow-up**

→ individualized according to recurrence risk, comorbidities and ongoing health needs

**Long-term care**

→ survivorship and routine medical care remain relevant even when routine gastric-cancer-specific surveillance becomes less intensive.

The exact schedule belongs to the treating team and the relevant surveillance guidance.

---

## Block 5 — What Is Usually Assessed During Follow-up?

The assessment can include several domains.

### 1. Symptoms and concerns

The team may ask about new, persistent or worsening symptoms.

### 2. Physical examination

History and physical examination are central components of follow-up.

### 3. Laboratory assessment

CBC and chemistry testing may be performed when clinically indicated.

### 4. Disease status

Surveillance and other investigations may be used according to stage, treatment history and clinical concern.

### 5. Treatment effects

The team may assess persistent or late effects of treatment.

### 6. Nutrition and gastrointestinal function

This is particularly important after gastrectomy.

### 7. General health

Preventive care, immunizations and management of other health conditions remain important.

### 8. Psychosocial and functional needs

Long-term care may need to address quality of life, fatigue, work, activity and psychosocial concerns.

---

## Block 6 — What Happens If a New Symptom Appears?

A new symptom during follow-up does not automatically mean that the cancer has returned.

The same symptom can have multiple possible explanations, including:

- treatment-related effects;
- post-surgical gastrointestinal changes;
- nutritional problems;
- unrelated medical conditions;
- recurrent disease.

The role of follow-up is to identify the concern and determine whether additional evaluation is needed.

The detailed question:

> “How is gastric cancer recurrence detected?”

belongs to **PP-0221 — Recurrence Detection**.

PP-0230 therefore owns the **follow-up encounter and escalation interface**, not the recurrence diagnostic algorithm.

---

## Block 7 — Why Is Post-gastrectomy Follow-up Different?

Gastrectomy can create long-term health consequences that require ongoing attention.

NCCN identifies long-term issues after gastrectomy including:

- postprandial fullness;
- nutritional deficiencies;
- dumping syndrome;
- gastrointestinal dysfunction;
- diarrhea and other symptoms;
- fatigue and functional effects;
- bone-health consequences.

Patients who undergo gastrectomy, particularly total gastrectomy, may need lifelong monitoring and management of nutritional sequelae.

Potential deficiencies include:

- vitamin B12;
- vitamin D;
- calcium;
- iron;
- zinc.

The exact monitoring and treatment of these deficiencies belong to the appropriate nutritional/supportive-care and toxicity packages. PP-0230 owns the longitudinal follow-up context in which these needs are recognized and reassessed.

---

## Block 8 — Does Follow-up Continue After Five Years?

This requires a careful distinction.

NCCN states that routine gastric-cancer-specific surveillance is generally not recommended beyond five years, while additional follow-up may be considered based on risk factors and comorbidities.

This does **not** mean:

> “All healthcare related to having had gastric cancer stops at five years.”

Long-term care can continue because of:

- treatment sequelae;
- nutritional needs;
- comorbidities;
- preventive health needs;
- second-primary-cancer screening;
- general medical care;
- survivorship needs.

The five-year point is therefore better understood as a potential transition in the **intensity and purpose of cancer-specific surveillance**, not as an automatic end to healthcare.

---

## Block 9 — What Is the Role of Primary Care?

Long-term care is not necessarily provided by oncology alone.

NCCN encourages maintaining a therapeutic relationship with a primary-care physician throughout life.

The oncologist and primary-care physician should have defined roles in survivorship care, and these roles should be communicated to the patient.

Depending on the patient's needs, subspecialists may also be involved.

This creates a longitudinal model:

**Oncology**

→ cancer-specific follow-up and surveillance interface

**Primary care**

→ preventive care, comorbidities and routine health maintenance

**Subspecialists**

→ specific long-term problems

**Nutrition / rehabilitation / supportive services**

→ selected treatment-related needs.

PP-0230 owns the coordination concept, while detailed multidisciplinary management belongs to PP-0232.

---

## Block 10 — What Is a Follow-up Plan?

A follow-up plan should provide enough information for the patient and healthcare team to understand:

- what treatment was received;
- what follow-up is recommended;
- what surveillance is recommended;
- what symptoms should prompt contact;
- what treatment-related effects need monitoring;
- what nutritional or functional needs exist;
- who is responsible for different aspects of long-term care;
- when primary-care or specialist care becomes particularly important.

A survivorship care plan can help preserve this information across transitions in care.

This is particularly important when a patient later sees a healthcare professional who was not involved in the original cancer treatment.

---

## Block 11 — Follow-up and Patient Records

Long-term care may involve multiple clinicians over many years.

Patients benefit from maintaining access to information about:

- diagnosis;
- stage;
- surgery;
- systemic therapy;
- radiotherapy when applicable;
- pathology and biomarker information;
- follow-up recommendations;
- significant treatment effects.

Good documentation supports continuity when care moves between oncology, primary care and specialists.

---

## Block 12 — Follow-up and General Preventive Health

Follow-up should not become so cancer-focused that routine healthcare is forgotten.

NCCN survivorship principles include:

- healthy body weight;
- physical activity;
- healthy diet with treatment-related modifications when needed;
- limiting alcohol;
- smoking cessation;
- preventive health measures;
- immunizations;
- routine cancer screening appropriate to the individual.

The purpose of including these concepts in PP-0230 is to show that long-term follow-up progressively integrates with general health care.

Detailed survivorship behavior guidance belongs primarily to PP-0229.

# Patient Explanation

## “I finished my treatment. Why do I still need follow-up?”

Because finishing treatment is an important milestone, but it is not necessarily the end of healthcare related to your cancer and its treatment.

Follow-up helps your care team:

- check how you are recovering;
- look for problems that need attention;
- monitor for signs that may require evaluation for recurrence;
- identify long-term effects of treatment;
- check nutritional and general health needs;
- coordinate care with other doctors.

---

## “Does every follow-up visit mean I need a scan?”

No.

The investigations used during follow-up depend on your cancer, treatment, symptoms, stage and clinical situation.

A follow-up visit may involve history, physical examination, laboratory testing when indicated, surveillance testing or no additional imaging at that particular visit.

---

## “If my doctor reduces the number of scans, does that mean they are no longer watching me?”

Not necessarily.

Cancer-specific surveillance often becomes less intensive with time for patients who remain disease-free. At the same time, long-term health care can continue.

---

## “What happens after five years?”

NCCN does not recommend routine gastric-cancer-specific surveillance beyond five years for everyone. However, additional follow-up can be considered according to risk factors and comorbidities.

You may also continue to need care for:

- nutrition;
- treatment effects;
- general health;
- preventive care;
- other medical conditions;
- survivorship needs.

---

# Clinical Importance

Long-term follow-up is the **continuity layer** connecting active treatment with survivorship and routine long-term healthcare.

Its clinical value comes from recognizing that different problems have different timelines:

| Clinical need | Possible timing | Follow-up role |
|---|---|---|
| Early recurrence concern | Often earlier after treatment | Identify concerning changes and trigger appropriate evaluation |
| Treatment recovery | Early/intermediate | Assess recovery and persistent symptoms |
| Nutritional sequelae | Long-term | Monitor and coordinate management |
| Gastrointestinal dysfunction | Early or long-term | Identify persistent problems |
| Bone health | Long-term | Recognize risk and coordinate monitoring |
| Preventive care | Lifelong | Integrate with primary care |
| Survivorship needs | Long-term | Periodically reassess needs |
| Late recurrence | Less common | Consider context-dependent additional evaluation |

This is why “follow-up” should not be reduced to a list of scans.

# Common Misconceptions

## Myth 1 — “Follow-up means scans.”

**Fact:** Follow-up includes clinical assessment, symptom review, laboratory testing when indicated, surveillance, treatment-effect assessment and general health needs.

## Myth 2 — “Follow-up and surveillance are the same thing.”

**Fact:** Surveillance is one component of broader longitudinal follow-up.

## Myth 3 — “Every symptom means recurrence.”

**Fact:** Symptoms can have many causes. They may require assessment but do not automatically establish recurrence.

## Myth 4 — “After five years, I no longer need medical care.”

**Fact:** Routine gastric-cancer-specific surveillance may become unnecessary, but additional follow-up may be appropriate and long-term medical/survivorship care continues.

## Myth 5 — “Everyone gets the same follow-up schedule.”

**Fact:** Follow-up varies according to stage, treatment, surgery, symptoms, risk factors and comorbidities.

## Myth 6 — “A normal scan means no other follow-up is needed.”

**Fact:** Nutritional, functional, preventive and treatment-related needs may persist even when there is no evidence of recurrence.

## Myth 7 — “My oncologist must provide all healthcare forever.”

**Fact:** Long-term care can be shared among oncology, primary care and appropriate specialists.

## Myth 8 — “If I feel well, follow-up has no purpose.”

**Fact:** Follow-up also supports prevention, health maintenance and monitoring for late effects.

# Key Messages

- Long-term follow-up is a continuing process after gastric cancer treatment.
- It is broader than surveillance alone.
- Follow-up changes over time.
- The exact plan depends on treatment history and clinical context.
- Gastrectomy can create long-term nutritional and gastrointestinal needs.
- Additional follow-up after five years may be considered according to risk factors and comorbidities.
- Long-term survivorship and routine medical care remain important.
- New symptoms should be assessed rather than automatically attributed to recurrence.
- Follow-up works best when responsibilities among oncology, primary care and specialists are clear.
- The treating team should determine the patient's individual follow-up plan.

# Clinical Safety Boundary

This package provides general educational information about long-term follow-up.

It does not:

- determine an individual patient's surveillance schedule;
- diagnose recurrence;
- interpret an individual scan or laboratory result;
- decide whether a symptom represents recurrence;
- prescribe nutritional supplementation;
- determine when a patient should transfer from oncology to primary care;
- replace individualized assessment by the treating team.

Any new, persistent or worsening symptom should be discussed with the appropriate healthcare professional rather than being self-classified as recurrence or as a treatment effect.
