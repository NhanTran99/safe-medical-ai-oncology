Knowledge Block 1 — What Is Stomach Cancer Screening?
Patient Explanation

Screening means looking for a disease before a person develops symptoms.

The goal of stomach cancer screening is to detect cancer or abnormal changes at an early stage, when treatment may be easier and more effective.

Screening is different from diagnostic testing. Screening is performed in people who do not have symptoms, while diagnostic tests are used to investigate symptoms that are already present.

Clinical Importance

Understanding the purpose of screening helps patients recognize that screening is intended for early detection rather than confirming an existing diagnosis.

Key Concepts
Screening looks for disease before symptoms appear.
Early detection may improve treatment opportunities.
Screening is different from diagnosis.
Knowledge Block 2 — Should Everyone Be Screened?
Patient Explanation

No.

Not everyone needs stomach cancer screening.

According to current United States recommendations, there is no routine screening program for stomach cancer for people at average risk.

This is different from some countries where stomach cancer is much more common and population-based screening programs exist.

Most people without important risk factors do not require routine screening.

Clinical Importance

Screening recommendations depend on a person's overall level of risk rather than simply their age or preference.

Key Concepts
Routine screening is not recommended for average-risk individuals in the United States.
Screening recommendations depend on individual risk.
Knowledge Block 3 — Who May Benefit from Screening?
Patient Explanation

Some people have a higher chance of developing stomach cancer than others.

Your healthcare team may consider screening if you have certain risk factors, such as:

a family history of stomach cancer;
certain inherited genetic conditions;
chronic atrophic gastritis;
pernicious anemia;
previous partial gastrectomy;
living in or originating from regions where stomach cancer is more common.

Having one or more risk factors does not mean that a person will definitely develop stomach cancer.

It means that screening may be appropriate after discussion with a healthcare professional.

Clinical Importance

Risk-based screening helps identify people who are more likely to benefit while avoiding unnecessary procedures in those at low risk.

Key Concepts
Some people have a higher risk than others.
Higher risk does not mean cancer is inevitable.
Screening decisions should be individualized.
Knowledge Block 4 — How Is Screening Performed?
Patient Explanation

For people who are considered at higher risk, the most common screening test is upper endoscopy.

During this procedure, a doctor uses a thin flexible tube with a light and camera to examine the inside of the esophagus, stomach and the first part of the small intestine.

If an abnormal area is seen, a small tissue sample (biopsy) may be taken for further examination.

This Population Package focuses only on the role of upper endoscopy as a screening tool. The procedure itself is explained in a separate Population Package.

Clinical Importance

Upper endoscopy allows doctors to directly examine the stomach lining and identify suspicious abnormalities that may require further evaluation.

Key Concepts
Upper endoscopy is the main screening test for high-risk individuals.
Abnormal findings may require additional investigation.
Screening does not automatically mean cancer is present.
Knowledge Block 5 — What Are the Benefits and Limitations of Screening?
Patient Explanation

Screening may help detect stomach cancer earlier, when treatment is often more successful.

However, no screening test is perfect.

Sometimes screening may appear normal even though cancer is present (a false-negative result).

In other cases, a screening test may suggest cancer when no cancer is actually present (a false-positive result). Additional tests may then be needed to clarify the diagnosis.

Like any medical procedure, upper endoscopy also carries small risks, although serious complications are uncommon.

Clinical Importance

Patients should understand both the potential benefits and the limitations of screening before deciding whether screening is appropriate for them.

Key Concepts
Screening can improve early detection.
Screening cannot detect every cancer.
Some abnormal results do not represent cancer.
Medical procedures have potential risks.
Knowledge Block 6 — How Should Patients Decide About Screening?
Patient Explanation

The decision to undergo stomach cancer screening should be made together with your healthcare team.

Your doctor will consider factors such as:

your personal medical history;
your family history;
inherited cancer risk;
other medical conditions;
your preferences after understanding the benefits and limitations of screening.

Shared decision-making helps ensure that screening is appropriate for your individual situation.

Clinical Importance

Personalized decision-making supports appropriate use of screening while avoiding unnecessary testing.

Common Misconception

Myth

Everyone should be screened for stomach cancer.

Fact

Routine screening is not recommended for everyone. Screening is generally considered for people with a higher risk of developing stomach cancer.

Key Messages
Screening looks for stomach cancer before symptoms develop.
Routine stomach cancer screening is not recommended for people at average risk in the United States.
Some people with higher risk may benefit from screening.
Upper endoscopy is the main screening method used in higher-risk individuals.
Screening offers potential benefits but also has limitations and risks.
Decisions about screening should be made together with the healthcare team.
