# Educational Objectives

After reading this Population Package, the reader should be able to:

1. Explain what endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR) is and where it fits within endoscopic treatment of early gastric cancer.
2. Understand why EMR can sometimes remove an early gastric cancer while preserving the stomach.
3. Understand why EMR is not appropriate for every gastric cancer and why selection depends on the depth, size, histology, ulcerative features, lymphovascular invasion risk, and likelihood of lymph-node spread.
4. Understand the difference between an endoscopic resection strategy and the specific EMR modality.
5. Understand why the lesion and the patient's pre-treatment staging assessment matter before EMR is considered.
6. Understand why a resection specimen provides more definitive information than a small diagnostic biopsy.
7. Recognize the major pathology features that determine whether an EMR has achieved a potentially curative result, including invasion depth, histologic differentiation, lymphovascular invasion, and lateral/deep margins.
8. Understand the concept of complete or en-bloc resection without learning procedural device mechanics.
9. Understand why a positive margin, lymphovascular invasion, deeper invasion, or unfavorable pathology can make EMR non-curative or incomplete.
10. Understand why some patients may need additional treatment after EMR rather than surveillance alone.
11. Understand the strategic difference between EMR and ESD, including why ESD may be preferred for some lesions.
12. Understand why EMR should be performed and interpreted by an experienced multidisciplinary team.
13. Understand the role of post-treatment endoscopic surveillance after definitive endoscopic treatment.
14. Understand the limitations of the evidence base, including the concentration of historical EMR experience in high-incidence Asian settings.
15. Know which questions to ask the cancer care team before and after EMR.

# Scope

## Atomic Clinical Question

> **What is endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR), when can it be used for early gastric cancer, how is the EMR specimen assessed, and what are the limitations of EMR compared with other endoscopic or surgical treatment?**

## Primary Function

PP-0194 is the **modality-specific, patient-facing knowledge node for endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR)** within the broader endoscopic-resection strategy for early gastric cancer.

It explains the clinical logic connecting:

**selected early gastric cancer**

**EMR as a local endoscopic resection modality**

↓

**resection specimen and pathology assessment**

↓

**curative versus non-curative/incomplete result**

↓

**surveillance or consideration of additional management**

The package deliberately remains below the umbrella strategic question of endoscopic resection and above detailed procedural technique.

# Clinical Knowledge Blocks

## Block 1 — What Is EMR?

**Endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR)** is an endoscopic procedure used to remove selected superficial lesions from the gastrointestinal lining.

In gastric cancer, the supplied NCI patient-facing material describes EMR as a procedure using an endoscope to remove carcinoma in situ and early-stage cancer from the lining of the digestive tract.

The important patient-facing idea is:

> **EMR removes a selected superficial gastric lesion through an endoscope rather than removing part or all of the stomach.**

This makes EMR fundamentally different from gastrectomy.

However, EMR is not simply a less invasive version of surgery that can be used for every gastric cancer. Its role depends on whether the cancer has characteristics that make local endoscopic removal oncologically reasonable.

---

## Block 2 — Where Does EMR Fit in the Treatment of Gastric Cancer?

EMR belongs to the broader family of **endoscopic resection (ER)**.

The two major modalities represented in the supplied gastric-cancer materials are:

- **EMR — endoscopic mucosal resection**
- **ESD — endoscopic submucosal dissection**

The broader ER strategy asks:

> **Can this early gastric cancer be treated adequately by removing it locally through the endoscope?**

The EMR-specific question is narrower:

> **Is EMR an appropriate way to perform that local endoscopic removal?**

This distinction matters because the patient-selection principles for ER and the technical/clinical characteristics of EMR are related but not identical.

---

## Block 3 — Why Can EMR Preserve the Stomach?

A gastrectomy removes part or all of the stomach and usually includes regional lymph-node surgery.

EMR instead aims to remove a selected lesion locally through the endoscope.

When the cancer is truly superficial and has a sufficiently low expected risk of lymph-node spread, local removal may provide an appropriate treatment option.

The stomach-preserving feature is therefore a major potential advantage of EMR.

But the benefit only applies when the biology of the tumor is compatible with local treatment.

A tumor that has a meaningful risk of lymph-node disease may require a treatment approach capable of addressing regional disease rather than only removing the visible mucosal lesion.

---

## Block 4 — Why Is Patient Selection the Central Issue?

The supplied NCI PDQ describes EMR experience in early gastric tumors with good-risk features, including Tis or T1a disease, diameter ≤2 cm, predominantly differentiated histology, and no ulcerative findings.

The same source emphasizes careful patient selection, experienced endoscopists, and close surveillance.

The current NCCN discussion similarly emphasizes that proper patient selection is essential and identifies lesion type, differentiation, and invasion depth as predictors of complete resection.

Therefore:

> **The key question is not simply “Can the lesion be removed?” but “Can it be removed in a way that is adequate for the cancer's biological risk?”**

---

## Block 5 — Why Does Depth of Invasion Matter?

Depth describes how far the tumor has grown through the stomach wall.

For early gastric cancer, the distinction between disease limited to the mucosa and disease extending into the submucosa is clinically important because deeper invasion can increase concern for lymphatic spread.

The supplied NCCN materials emphasize depth of invasion in pre-treatment staging and require depth of submucosal invasion to be assessed in the EMR pathology report where possible.

The current NCCN curative ER framework describes superficial invasion as T1a or T1b with ≤500 μm of submucosal invasion, within its stated curative features.

The important concept for patients is:

> **A lesion that looks small on the surface may still be biologically different if it has grown deeper into the stomach wall.**

---

## Block 6 — Why Does Lymph-Node Risk Matter?

EMR removes the primary lesion.

It does not provide the same regional lymph-node removal as gastrectomy with lymphadenectomy.

Therefore, the safety of an endoscopic-only strategy depends partly on the expected probability that cancer has already spread to regional lymph nodes.

The supplied NCI PDQ notes that intramucosal tumors have a lower risk of nodal metastasis than submucosal tumors.

The Vietnamese gastric-cancer guideline states the same underlying principle in its endoscopic-resection section: lesions considered for endoscopic treatment should have very low likelihood of lymph-node metastasis and be suitable for en-bloc removal.

Thus:

**Low expected nodal risk**

→ local endoscopic treatment may be reasonable.

**Meaningful nodal risk**

→ a local mucosal procedure may be insufficient.

---

## Block 7 — Why Does Tumor Size Matter?

Tumor size is one component of endoscopic-treatment selection.

The supplied NCCN curative framework identifies lesions ≤2 cm among the features supporting adequate endoscopic treatment.

The NCI PDQ also describes the classic favorable-risk EMR population as tumors ≤2 cm.

The Vietnamese guideline likewise uses size ≤2 cm in its absolute-indication framework for differentiated, nonulcerated T1a lesions.

Size, however, should never be interpreted in isolation.

A small tumor with unfavorable histology, deep invasion, LVI, or involved margins may still require additional management.

Conversely, expanded endoscopic indications described in the supplied materials are mainly associated with ESD rather than automatically expanding the role of EMR.

---

## Block 8 — Why Does Histologic Differentiation Matter?

Histology describes the microscopic characteristics of the cancer.

The supplied NCI PDQ identifies predominantly differentiated tumors as part of the classic favorable-risk EMR population.

The NCCN discussion identifies well or moderately differentiated histology as part of its curative ER features.

This is important because histology helps estimate whether the tumor behaves like a lesion that can safely be managed with local endoscopic treatment.

PP-0194 uses histology at this clinical-selection level.

It does not teach the full classification of gastric adenocarcinoma.

---

## Block 9 — Why Does Ulceration Matter?

Ulceration is an endoscopic/pathologic feature that can affect how a lesion is classified for endoscopic treatment.

The classic EMR population described by NCI and the Vietnamese guideline includes selected nonulcerated lesions.

The NCCN materials also identify ulceration among endoscopic features that can suggest deeper submucosal invasion in larger lesions.

Therefore ulceration should be treated as a **selection signal**, not as an automatic yes/no rule in isolation.

Expanded endoscopic indications may differ by modality, and the supplied NCCN material specifically presents broader expanded indications under ESD.

---

## Block 10 — Why Does Lymphovascular Invasion Matter?

**Lymphovascular invasion (LVI)** means that tumor cells are identified within lymphatic or blood-vessel spaces.

The supplied NCCN pathology framework requires vascular/lymphatic invasion to be reported for an EMR specimen.

LVI is important because it can indicate a pathway by which tumor cells may spread beyond the locally resected lesion.

The current NCCN curative framework requires absence of LVI for curative endoscopic treatment.

Therefore:

> **An EMR specimen is not considered fully assessed until relevant invasion and LVI information has been evaluated.**

---

## Block 11 — Why Does the EMR Specimen Matter So Much?

A conventional biopsy samples only part of a lesion.

An EMR specimen can provide a larger piece of tissue that allows more complete assessment of:

- differentiation;
- invasion depth;
- LVI;
- resection margins.

The supplied NCCN endoscopic-staging section specifically states that EMR/ESD of focal nodules ≤2 cm can provide a larger specimen and better assessment by the pathologist, including differentiation, LVI, and depth of infiltration.

This creates an important dual role for EMR:

**Treatment**

and

**definitive pathologic assessment of the removed lesion.**

---

## Block 12 — What Does the Pathologist Need to Look For?

For an EMR specimen, the supplied NCCN pathology table specifies reporting of:

- invasion, if present;
- histologic type;
- grade;
- depth of submucosal invasion in micrometers, if possible;
- vascular/lymphatic invasion;
- mucosal and deep margin status.

These elements answer a clinical question:

> **Was the cancer removed completely, and does the pathology show a sufficiently low-risk pattern for endoscopic treatment to be considered curative?**

PP-0194 explains why these elements matter.

Detailed pathology methodology remains owned by the histopathology package.

---

## Block 13 — What Does “En-Bloc” Mean?

**En-bloc resection** means removing the target lesion as one specimen rather than as multiple separate pieces.

For patient education, the important point is not the technical mechanics.

The important point is that a single intact specimen can make it easier to determine:

- the true lesion boundaries;
- whether the margins are clear;
- how deeply the tumor has invaded;
- whether other high-risk features are present.

The Vietnamese guideline explicitly places suitability for en-bloc removal within the principle of endoscopic-resection selection.

---

## Block 14 — What Are Resection Margins?

A **margin** is the edge of the tissue removed during a resection.

For EMR, the supplied NCCN pathology framework distinguishes:

- mucosal/lateral margin;
- deep margin.

The current NCCN curative ER framework requires negative lateral and deep margins.

A positive margin means tumor extends to the relevant edge of the removed specimen and raises concern that cancer may remain locally.

A positive margin therefore changes the interpretation of the EMR and may lead to consideration of additional treatment.

---

## Block 15 — What Is a Curative EMR?

A “curative” endoscopic resection is not determined simply by the fact that the lesion was successfully removed.

The supplied NCCN framework describes curative features including:

- small lesion, ≤2 cm;
- superficial invasion;
- well or moderately differentiated histology;
- complete excision;
- negative lateral and deep margins;
- no LVI.

The broader principle is:

> **Curative status is a combined pathology and oncologic assessment, not a procedural label.**

The supplied Vietnamese guideline expresses the same principle by linking curative endoscopic resection to complete removal of the primary tumor and absence of meaningful lymph-node metastatic potential.

---

## Block 16 — What Is a Non-Curative or Incomplete EMR?

An EMR may be technically completed but still be considered **non-curative or incomplete** from an oncologic perspective.

The supplied NCCN discussion identifies concerning features including:

- poorly differentiated histology;
- LVI;
- deep submucosal invasion;
- positive lateral or deep margins;
- lymph-node metastases.

When such features are present, additional therapy may need to be considered.

This is why patients should not interpret:

> “The lesion was removed”

as automatically meaning:

> “The cancer is definitely cured.”

---

## Block 17 — What Happens After a Non-Curative EMR?

The next step depends on the reason the EMR is considered non-curative.

Possible pathways may include:

- additional endoscopic treatment in selected situations;
- surgical treatment;
- systemic treatment in selected circumstances;
- surveillance in carefully selected contexts.

The supplied NCCN algorithm shows that post-resection pathology determines whether gastrectomy, systemic therapy, or surveillance should be considered.

PP-0194 does not own the detailed algorithm for choosing among these options.

Its role is to explain the transition:

**EMR**

→ **pathology**

→ **curative or non-curative assessment**

→ **next management decision.**

---

## Block 18 — Why Is ESD Often Preferred?

EMR and ESD are both endoscopic resection modalities, but they are not identical.

The supplied NCCN guideline states that en-bloc excision of small gastric lesions by ESD has been shown to be more effective than EMR in several studies.

The same source states that ESD requires greater skill and instrumentation and has significant risks including perforation.

The current NCCN framework therefore prefers ESD within its ER strategy.

This does not mean:

> **“EMR is obsolete.”**

It means:

> **The choice of endoscopic modality depends on the lesion and the capabilities of the treatment center.**

---

## Block 19 — Why Might EMR Still Be Used?

The supplied NCI PDQ and Vietnamese guideline both retain a role for EMR in selected favorable-risk early gastric cancers.

Classic examples include:

- superficial disease;
- favorable differentiation;
- small size;
- absence of ulceration;
- low expected nodal risk.

The current NCCN discussion also states that EMR or ESD can be considered adequate therapy for selected lesions ≤2 cm with favorable histology, superficial invasion, no LVI, and clear margins, while noting that ESD is preferred.

Therefore:

> **EMR remains a legitimate treatment modality for selected patients even when ESD is preferred in the broader guideline framework.**

---

## Block 20 — Why Is EMR Not Simply “Smaller ESD”?

EMR and ESD are different procedures.

EMR is a mucosal-resection approach.

ESD allows more extensive dissection within the submucosal plane and therefore can provide broader en-bloc removal.

The clinical consequence is that the two modalities have different technical capabilities and different risk/benefit profiles.

PP-0194 should therefore teach:

**EMR versus ESD**

rather than treating:

**EMR = simplified ESD.**

Detailed ESD technique is delegated to the dedicated ESD package.

---

## Block 21 — Why Does the Treatment Center Matter?

The supplied NCCN discussion recommends that endoscopic resection be performed in high-volume medical centers with extensive experience in these techniques.

NCI similarly emphasizes treatment by an experienced endoscopist and close surveillance.

This matters because successful endoscopic treatment requires more than the procedure itself.

The care pathway includes:

**patient selection**

+

**experienced endoscopy**

+

**expert pathology**

+

**appropriate interpretation of the specimen**

+

**ability to provide additional treatment if required**

+

**surveillance.**

---

## Block 22 — What Are the Main Risks?

EMR is less invasive than gastrectomy in the sense that it avoids surgical removal of part or all of the stomach, but it is still an invasive endoscopic treatment.

Patients should understand that endoscopic treatment can have procedure-related risks.

The supplied materials provide more detailed complication discussion for ESD than for EMR, including bleeding and perforation risk.

Therefore this PP should communicate risks at a high level without inventing an EMR-specific complication rate that is not established in the supplied source set.

Detailed complication prevention and management are outside scope.

---

## Block 23 — What Outcomes Have Been Reported?

The supplied NCI PDQ describes a prospective Japanese EMR experience involving 445 patients with 479 intramucosal tumors.

Among patients with intramucosal disease who underwent complete resection, the source reports 2% local recurrence treated with curative intent and 100% disease-free survival at a median follow-up of 38 months.

The same source reports that among patients with incomplete or non-evaluable resections, local recurrence occurred in 18 of 127 patients and these patients underwent curative surgery.

The purpose of these figures is to illustrate how complete resection status can influence outcomes.

They should not be interpreted as an individualized prediction for a particular patient.

---

## Block 24 — What Long-Term Outcomes Are Reported?

The supplied NCCN discussion cites a study of 124 patients with early-stage mucosal gastric cancers in which 5- and 10-year survival rates of 84% and 64%, respectively, were reported for patients receiving EMR.

NCCN also cites a retrospective study of 215 patients with intramucosal gastric cancer in which EMR was associated with shorter hospital stays and had comparable risk of death and recurrence to surgery in that study context.

These findings support the clinical value of EMR in selected early disease.

They do not establish that every patient treated with EMR will have the same outcome.

---

## Block 25 — Why Is the Evidence Base Concentrated in Asia?

The supplied NCCN and NCI materials emphasize that much of the experience with EMR for early gastric cancer comes from Japan and other high-incidence Asian settings.

This is clinically relevant because such settings may have:

- more frequent detection of early gastric cancer;
- established endoscopic expertise;
- experienced GI pathology services;
- structured surveillance systems.

The current NCCN discussion also notes that applicability of endoscopic techniques in the United States is limited by the lower incidence of early-stage disease.

Therefore:

> **The evidence for EMR is strong enough to support its role in selected early gastric cancer, but the infrastructure and expertise available in the treating center remain important.**

---

## Block 26 — Does EMR Replace Gastrectomy?

No.

EMR can sometimes provide definitive treatment for carefully selected early gastric cancers.

Gastrectomy remains an important treatment when:

- the lesion is not suitable for endoscopic resection;
- there is substantial risk of lymph-node disease;
- the EMR specimen shows non-curative features;
- the lesion cannot be adequately removed endoscopically;
- additional oncologic treatment is otherwise required.

The key distinction is:

**EMR treats the selected local lesion.**

**Gastrectomy can remove the involved stomach segment and provide regional lymph-node treatment.**

Therefore EMR should not be presented as universally superior to surgery.

---

## Block 27 — Why Can EMR Be Both Treatment and Staging-Relevant?

A small biopsy answers:

> “Is cancer present in this sampled tissue?”

An EMR specimen can answer additional questions:

> “How deeply has the cancer invaded?”

> “What is its histologic differentiation?”

> “Is lymphovascular invasion present?”

> “Are the resection margins clear?”

These questions may determine whether the endoscopic treatment was adequate.

Thus EMR sits at an important interface:

**diagnosis**

→

**pathologic characterization**

→

**local treatment**

→

**curative assessment.**

---

## Block 28 — Why Is Pre-Treatment Staging Important?

The supplied NCCN guideline states that EUS before treatment is important in initial clinical staging and is especially important when endoscopic resection is being considered.

EUS can provide information about:

- depth of tumor invasion;
- suspicious lymph nodes;
- selected features relevant to distant spread.

PP-0194 does not teach EUS technique.

The patient-facing message is:

> **Before EMR, the team needs enough information to judge whether the cancer is superficial enough and low-risk enough for local endoscopic treatment.**

---

## Block 29 — What If the Biopsy and EMR Pathology Differ?

This can happen because a biopsy and an EMR specimen examine different amounts of tissue.

A biopsy may not capture:

- the deepest point of invasion;
- the entire lesion;
- the complete resection margin;
- all histologic components.

The larger EMR specimen may therefore reveal information that was not available from the original biopsy.

This is not necessarily a contradiction.

It may reflect the greater amount of tissue available for assessment.

---

## Block 30 — What Does “Complete Resection” Mean?

Complete resection has two related meanings that should not be confused:

### Technical completeness

The visible target lesion was removed.

### Oncologic adequacy

The pathology shows that the resection meets the relevant curative criteria.

The second is the clinically more important question.

A technically successful EMR can therefore still be judged non-curative if pathology shows:

- positive margins;
- deeper invasion;
- LVI;
- unfavorable histology;
- other high-risk features.

---

## Block 31 — Why Is a Negative Margin Important?

A negative margin means that the examined edge of the removed specimen does not contain tumor.

For EMR, the supplied NCCN framework specifically considers lateral and deep margins.

Negative margins support complete local removal.

However:

> **Negative margins alone do not establish curative treatment.**

Depth, differentiation, LVI, lesion size, and nodal-risk considerations also matter.

---

## Block 32 — Why Does Deep Margin Matter?

The deep margin is the margin at the deeper tissue boundary of the resection.

If tumor reaches this boundary, residual disease may remain in the stomach wall.

This is particularly important because the depth of invasion itself is a major determinant of whether local endoscopic treatment is sufficient.

Therefore:

**Deep margin**

and

**depth of invasion**

must be interpreted together.

---

## Block 33 — Why Does the Lateral Margin Matter?

The lateral margin describes the side-to-side boundary of the removed lesion.

A positive lateral margin raises concern that tumor extends beyond the area removed.

The supplied NCCN framework includes negative lateral and deep margins among curative features.

A positive margin may therefore lead to additional endoscopic or surgical consideration depending on the full pathology context.

---

## Block 34 — Why Does “No LVI” Matter?

A lesion can be completely removed at the visible site but still show biological features associated with spread.

LVI is one such feature.

The current NCCN curative framework requires no LVI for curative endoscopic treatment.

This illustrates why:

> **The pathology report is not just a confirmation that the lesion was removed.**

It is a risk assessment of whether local removal was enough.

---

## Block 35 — Why Is “≤500 μm” Important in the Current NCCN Framework?

The supplied NCCN version 2.2026 defines superficial pT1b as invasion ≤500 μm into the submucosa and distinguishes this from deeper pT1b invasion.

Within the curative ER framework, NCCN includes superficial invasion up to 500 μm among the favorable features.

This threshold is therefore relevant when explaining current guideline terminology.

It should not be used as an isolated self-assessment rule because the final interpretation also depends on histology, LVI, margins, size, and the complete clinical context.

---

## Block 36 — What Are Expanded Endoscopic Indications?

The supplied materials describe expanded indications for endoscopic treatment, but these should be handled carefully.

The current NCCN discussion describes expanded indications primarily for **ESD**, including selected lesions with:

- favorable differentiation without ulceration;
- superficial submucosal invasion;
- selected ulcerated lesions;
- selected poorly differentiated lesions without ulceration.

The Vietnamese guideline also describes relative/expanded indications and explicitly notes that ESD is often preferred because it can resect more broadly in the submucosal layer.

Therefore:

> **Expanded ESD indications should not be automatically converted into expanded EMR indications.**

This is a key boundary for PP-0194.

---

## Block 37 — What Is the Relationship Between EMR and ESD Selection?

The selection process can be conceptualized as:

**Early gastric cancer**

↓

**Is endoscopic treatment potentially appropriate?**

↓

**How superficial is the lesion?**

↓

**What are size, histology, ulceration and nodal-risk features?**

↓

**Can the lesion be adequately removed with EMR?**

↓

**If not, would ESD provide the required resection capability?**

↓

**If endoscopic treatment is unsuitable or non-curative, should surgery or other treatment be considered?**

This is a clinical reasoning framework, not a self-directed treatment algorithm.

---

## Block 38 — What Questions Should a Patient Ask Before EMR?

Useful questions include:

1. Why is EMR being considered for my lesion?
2. What stage and depth is suspected before the procedure?
3. What features make my lesion suitable for EMR?
4. What is the estimated risk of lymph-node involvement?
5. Is ESD being considered instead, and why?
6. What will the pathology report need to determine?
7. How will the margins be assessed?
8. Will the specimen be reviewed by an experienced GI pathologist?
9. What would make the EMR non-curative?
10. If the pathology is non-curative, what additional options might be considered?
11. What surveillance will be needed after EMR?
12. Is the procedure being performed in a center experienced with early gastric cancer endoscopic resection?

---

## Block 39 — What Questions Should a Patient Ask After the Pathology Report?

Ask the care team:

1. Was the lesion completely removed?
2. Were the lateral and deep margins negative?
3. What was the exact depth of invasion?
4. Was the tumor well, moderately, or poorly differentiated?
5. Was lymphovascular invasion present?
6. Does the pathology meet the criteria for curative endoscopic treatment?
7. Is any additional endoscopic treatment recommended?
8. Is surgery being considered?
9. Why is surveillance sufficient, if surveillance is recommended?
10. What follow-up endoscopy is planned?

These questions help the patient understand the clinical meaning of the pathology without attempting to interpret the report independently.

---

## Block 40 — Patient Explanation: The Whole EMR Pathway in Plain Language

A useful way to understand EMR is:

> **First, the team determines whether the cancer is early and low-risk enough for local endoscopic treatment.**

> **Then, EMR removes the selected lesion through the endoscope.**

> **The removed tissue is examined carefully under the microscope.**

> **The pathology report tells the team how deep the cancer was, what type it was, whether the edges are clear, and whether there are features that increase the risk of spread.**

> **Only after that assessment can the team determine whether the EMR is considered curative or whether additional treatment is needed.**

This sequence is the central patient-facing concept of PP-0194.

# Common Misconceptions

## Myth 1

**“EMR is just a biopsy.”**

**Fact:**  
EMR can provide a larger specimen than a routine biopsy and can be therapeutic for appropriately selected early gastric cancer.

---

## Myth 2

**“If the lesion is small, EMR is automatically appropriate.”**

**Fact:**  
Size is only one factor. Depth, histology, ulceration, LVI, margins, and lymph-node risk also matter.

---

## Myth 3

**“T1a automatically means EMR.”**

**Fact:**  
T1a is an important favorable setting, but the complete lesion and pathology profile still has to be considered.

---

## Myth 4

**“If the endoscopist removed the whole visible lesion, I am cured.”**

**Fact:**  
Curative status depends on the pathology and the complete oncologic assessment.

---

## Myth 5

**“Clear margins mean nothing else matters.”**

**Fact:**  
Margins are important, but depth, histology, LVI, size, and nodal-risk considerations also influence curative assessment.

---

## Myth 6

**“A positive margin means the EMR failed completely.”**

**Fact:**  
A positive margin means the resection may be non-curative and may require additional assessment or treatment. The EMR specimen may still provide essential information.

---

## Myth 7

**“EMR and ESD are the same procedure.”**

**Fact:**  
They are different endoscopic resection modalities with different technical capabilities and risk profiles.

---

## Myth 8

**“ESD being preferred means EMR is no longer useful.”**

**Fact:**  
The supplied guidelines retain a role for EMR in selected favorable-risk early gastric cancers.

---

## Myth 9

**“Expanded ESD indications automatically apply to EMR.”**

**Fact:**  
They should not be automatically transferred. The current NCCN material presents expanded indications primarily in the ESD framework.

---

## Myth 10

**“EMR means I will never need surgery.”**

**Fact:**  
If the lesion is unsuitable or the pathology shows non-curative/high-risk features, additional surgery may still be considered.

---

## Myth 11

**“EMR removes the risk of lymph-node spread.”**

**Fact:**  
EMR does not remove regional lymph nodes. It is appropriate only when the expected risk of nodal disease is sufficiently low.

---

## Myth 12

**“The biopsy already tells the team everything needed for EMR.”**

**Fact:**  
The larger EMR specimen can reveal invasion depth, LVI, margins, and other information that may not be available from a small biopsy.

---

## Myth 13

**“A favorable-looking endoscopy guarantees favorable pathology.”**

**Fact:**  
The final pathology can reveal deeper invasion or other high-risk features that were not fully apparent before resection.

---

## Myth 14

**“EMR is risk-free because it avoids surgery.”**

**Fact:**  
EMR is still an invasive endoscopic treatment and carries procedure-related risks.

---

## Myth 15

**“A historical survival percentage tells me my personal chance of cure.”**

**Fact:**  
Published cohort outcomes describe groups of selected patients and cannot substitute for individualized clinical assessment.

# Key Clinical Concepts

## Concept 1 — EMR is a selective treatment

EMR is not a universal gastric-cancer treatment.

It is a treatment for selected superficial lesions in which local removal is expected to provide adequate oncologic control.

---

## Concept 2 — Local treatment requires low nodal-risk biology

Because EMR does not remove regional lymph nodes, the expected risk of nodal disease is central to patient selection.

---

## Concept 3 — The specimen determines more than the procedure

The final pathology is a major determinant of whether EMR was adequate.

---

## Concept 4 — Curative status is multidimensional

Curative assessment combines:

- complete removal;
- margins;
- depth;
- histology;
- LVI;
- size;
- nodal-risk considerations.

---

## Concept 5 — ESD is not simply “better EMR”

ESD has different capabilities and risks.

The current NCCN framework prefers ESD in endoscopic treatment, but EMR remains relevant for selected favorable lesions.

---

## Concept 6 — Non-curative EMR is a management branch, not an endpoint

A non-curative pathology result triggers a new clinical decision.

It does not mean the information obtained from EMR was useless.

---

## Concept 7 — Center expertise matters

Endoscopic resection requires appropriate endoscopy expertise, pathology expertise, and the ability to provide additional treatment when required.

---

# Patient-Facing Key Messages

1. **EMR is an endoscopic treatment that can remove selected early gastric cancers while preserving the stomach.**
2. **EMR is appropriate only for carefully selected lesions.**
3. **Depth of invasion and lymph-node risk are central to the decision.**
4. **Tumor size, histology, ulceration, LVI, and margins all matter.**
5. **The EMR specimen provides important information that a small biopsy may not provide.**
6. **A successful procedure does not automatically mean the cancer has been cured.**
7. **Curative status is determined from the full pathology and clinical context.**
8. **ESD may be preferred when greater en-bloc resection capability is needed.**
9. **Some patients still need surgery or other treatment after EMR.**
10. **Follow-up remains important after apparently successful endoscopic treatment.**
11. **Experienced centers and expert pathology review are important.**
12. **Ask the care team exactly why EMR is appropriate for your lesion and what the pathology must show for treatment to be considered curative.**
