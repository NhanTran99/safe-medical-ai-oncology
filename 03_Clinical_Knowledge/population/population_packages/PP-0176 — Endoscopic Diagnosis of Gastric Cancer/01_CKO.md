## Educational Objectives

After reading this Population Package, the reader should be able to:

1. Explain what diagnostic endoscopy means in the context of suspected gastric cancer.
2. Distinguish diagnostic endoscopy from screening and surveillance endoscopy.
3. Understand what endoscopists look for when evaluating the stomach for possible neoplasia.
4. Recognize that gastric cancer can have focal, ulcerated, depressed, irregular, or diffuse/infiltrative appearances.
5. Understand why tumor location, size, extent and relationship to the esophagogastric junction are documented.
6. Understand why systematic examination and clear documentation matter.
7. Distinguish endoscopic suspicion from definitive tissue diagnosis.
8. Understand why suspicious lesions are generally followed by tissue sampling.
9. Recognize important limitations of visual detection, including microscopic and diffuse disease.
10. Understand why an initial negative or nondiagnostic biopsy may not always resolve strong endoscopic suspicion.
11. Understand the selected diagnostic/staging interfaces with EUS and EMR/ESD.
12. Understand what diagnostic endoscopy does and does not determine.

# Clinical Knowledge Blocks

## Knowledge Block 1 — What Is Diagnostic Endoscopy for Gastric Cancer?

### Patient Explanation

Diagnostic endoscopy is an examination of the inside of the upper digestive tract that is used to look directly at the stomach when gastric cancer is suspected or when an abnormality needs further evaluation.

### Clinical Importance

It is a central part of the gastric-cancer diagnostic pathway because it can show whether a suspicious abnormality is present and where it is located. It also allows tissue to be obtained for the next diagnostic step.

### Key Concepts

- diagnostic endoscopy
- upper endoscopy
- direct visualization
- gastric neoplasia

## Knowledge Block 2 — How Is Diagnostic Endoscopy Different From Screening Endoscopy?

### Patient Explanation

Screening endoscopy is performed to look for disease in people who are being evaluated because of their risk or population context. Diagnostic endoscopy investigates a specific clinical suspicion or abnormal finding.

### Clinical Importance

Keeping these purposes separate prevents the patient from confusing an examination performed because cancer is suspected with an examination performed to screen an at-risk population.

### Key Concepts

- screening
- diagnostic evaluation
- clinical suspicion

## Knowledge Block 3 — How Is Diagnostic Endoscopy Different From Surveillance Endoscopy?

### Patient Explanation

Surveillance endoscopy is performed after a previous disease, treatment, or defined high-risk state to look for recurrence, progression, or new abnormalities. Diagnostic endoscopy is focused on investigating a current suspicion or newly identified abnormality.

### Clinical Importance

The same instrument may be used, but the clinical question is different.

### Key Concepts

- surveillance
- recurrence
- new abnormality

## Knowledge Block 4 — What Does the Endoscopist Look For?

### Patient Explanation

The endoscopist examines the gastric lining for areas that look different from the surrounding mucosa. A suspicious area may be raised, depressed, ulcerated, irregular, infiltrative, or otherwise abnormal.

### Clinical Importance

Gastric cancer does not always appear as a single obvious mass. Recognizing different patterns is important because subtle or diffuse disease can be harder to detect.

### Key Concepts

- abnormal mucosa
- suspicious lesion
- morphology

## Knowledge Block 5 — Can Every Gastric Cancer Be Seen as a Mass?

### Patient Explanation

No. Some gastric cancers form a visible focal lesion, while others may infiltrate the stomach more diffusely or involve microscopic areas that are difficult to see.

### Clinical Importance

Diffuse or microscopic disease is an important limitation of purely visual diagnosis. A normal-looking or nonspecific area does not have the same meaning in every clinical context.

### Key Concepts

- focal cancer
- diffuse cancer
- microscopic disease

## Knowledge Block 6 — What Does an Ulcerated Gastric Lesion Mean?

### Patient Explanation

An ulcerated lesion is an area where the surface of the stomach lining is broken or eroded. Ulceration can occur with different conditions, including gastric cancer, so an ulcer does not by itself establish a cancer diagnosis.

### Clinical Importance

Ulcerated lesions deserve careful assessment and adequate tissue evaluation when cancer is suspected. NCCN specifically emphasizes adequate biopsy material in ulcerated lesions.

### Key Concepts

- ulcerated lesion
- suspicion
- tissue evaluation

## Knowledge Block 7 — What Does a Raised or Depressed Lesion Mean?

### Patient Explanation

A raised lesion protrudes above the surrounding surface, while a depressed lesion sits below it. These are descriptions of how an abnormal area looks rather than final diagnoses.

### Clinical Importance

Morphologic description helps communicate what was seen and supports decisions about further evaluation, while pathology determines what the tissue actually represents.

### Key Concepts

- raised lesion
- depressed lesion
- morphology

## Knowledge Block 8 — What Does an Irregular or Infiltrative Area Mean?

### Patient Explanation

An irregular or infiltrative area may not have a clear border or may make the stomach look or feel different from normal mucosa. Some diffuse gastric cancers spread within the wall rather than forming a discrete mass.

### Clinical Importance

Recognizing an infiltrative pattern is important because superficial sampling or visual assessment may not fully represent the disease.

### Key Concepts

- infiltrative disease
- diffuse pattern
- linitis plastica

## Knowledge Block 9 — Why Is Tumor Location Recorded?

### Patient Explanation

The endoscopist records where an abnormality is located in the stomach, such as the cardia, fundus, body, antrum, or pylorus. For tumors near the top of the stomach, the relationship to the esophagogastric junction is also important.

### Clinical Importance

Accurate location helps the clinical team communicate about the lesion, compare examinations, and use the information in later staging and treatment planning.

### Key Concepts

- cardia
- fundus
- body
- antrum
- pylorus
- EGJ

## Knowledge Block 10 — Why Is the Relationship to the Esophagogastric Junction Important?

### Patient Explanation

For a tumor near the junction between the esophagus and stomach, the endoscopic examination should document how the lesion relates to that junction.

### Clinical Importance

This provides an anatomical reference for subsequent clinical assessment. Endoscopic localization should not be confused with the later treatment or staging decision based on that location.

### Key Concepts

- esophagogastric junction
- proximal tumor
- localization

## Knowledge Block 11 — Why Are Lesion Size and Extent Documented?

### Patient Explanation

The endoscopist records how large the abnormal area appears and how far it extends. These observations help create a clear description of what was found.

### Clinical Importance

Size and extent are useful for comparison, communication, and planning of subsequent evaluation. Specific size thresholds for endoscopic treatment belong to downstream packages.

### Key Concepts

- lesion size
- lesion extent
- documentation

## Knowledge Block 12 — Why Should the Stomach Be Examined Systematically?

### Patient Explanation

A diagnostic examination should not focus only on the first abnormality that is noticed. The stomach should be examined adequately so that additional or subtle abnormalities are less likely to be overlooked.

### Clinical Importance

Systematic inspection is particularly important because gastric neoplasia can be subtle, multifocal, or diffuse.

### Key Concepts

- systematic examination
- subtle lesion
- completeness

## Knowledge Block 13 — Why Is Endoscopic Documentation Important?

### Patient Explanation

The findings should be recorded clearly enough to communicate what was seen, where it was seen, and what features made the area concerning.

### Clinical Importance

Good documentation supports communication between endoscopy, pathology, radiology, surgery, and other members of the care team and allows comparison with future examinations.

### Key Concepts

- documentation
- communication
- follow-up comparison

## Knowledge Block 14 — Can Endoscopy Tell for Certain That a Lesion Is Cancer?

### Patient Explanation

Endoscopy can make a lesion strongly suspicious for cancer, but visual appearance alone does not establish the final tissue diagnosis.

### Clinical Importance

This distinction is fundamental: endoscopy provides visual and anatomical information, while biopsy and pathology determine what the tissue actually represents.

### Key Concepts

- suspicion versus confirmation
- visual diagnosis
- pathology

## Knowledge Block 15 — Why Is Biopsy the Next Step After a Suspicious Lesion?

### Patient Explanation

When a suspicious lesion is identified, tissue can be removed and examined under a microscope. NCCN identifies biopsy of suspicious lesions as a core component of an adequate diagnostic endoscopic examination.

### Clinical Importance

The biopsy connects visual observation to tissue-based diagnosis. The detailed strategy for obtaining adequate tissue is owned by PP-0177.

### Key Concepts

- biopsy
- tissue diagnosis
- diagnostic transition

## Knowledge Block 16 — Does PP-0176 Decide How Many Biopsies Are Needed?

### Patient Explanation

No. PP-0176 establishes that suspicious lesions require appropriate tissue sampling. Detailed biopsy number, location, technique, and optimization belong to the dedicated biopsy package.

### Clinical Importance

This boundary prevents diagnostic endoscopy from absorbing the substantive clinical ownership of PP-0177.

### Key Concepts

- biopsy interface
- scope boundary
- PP-0177

## Knowledge Block 17 — Why Can a Biopsy Be Negative When Endoscopy Looks Suspicious?

### Patient Explanation

A negative biopsy does not always resolve a strong clinical or endoscopic suspicion. The sample may not have captured the abnormal area adequately, the lesion may be heterogeneous, or disease may be deeper or more diffuse than the sampled surface.

### Clinical Importance

Discordance between what is seen and what the first tissue sample shows should be recognized as a diagnostic problem rather than automatically interpreted as proof that cancer is absent.

### Key Concepts

- discordance
- sampling limitation
- nondiagnostic biopsy

## Knowledge Block 18 — Why Is Diffuse Gastric Cancer a Special Diagnostic Challenge?

### Patient Explanation

Diffuse gastric cancer can spread within the stomach wall rather than forming one clearly visible mass. NCI describes linitis plastica as diffuse infiltration that can make the stomach stiff, and notes that a very superficial biopsy may be negative.

### Clinical Importance

This is a concrete example of why endoscopic appearance and superficial tissue sampling may not always capture the full disease.

### Key Concepts

- diffuse gastric cancer
- linitis plastica
- superficial biopsy

## Knowledge Block 19 — Can Microscopic Cancer Be Invisible During Endoscopy?

### Patient Explanation

Yes. Some gastric cancers, especially in selected hereditary diffuse gastric cancer settings, can exist as microscopic foci that are not obvious on visual examination.

### Clinical Importance

NCI's hereditary diffuse gastric cancer material documents that microscopic signet-ring-cell foci may be difficult to identify endoscopically. This is an important limitation of visual detection.

### Key Concepts

- microscopic signet-ring cells
- visual limitation
- HDGC

## Knowledge Block 20 — Does a Normal-Looking Examination Always Exclude Cancer?

### Patient Explanation

No. A normal-looking stomach is reassuring in many circumstances, but the meaning of a normal examination depends on the clinical context. Microscopic or diffuse disease may be difficult to recognize visually.

### Clinical Importance

This is especially important when clinical suspicion or hereditary risk remains high. The package should not turn a normal visual examination into an absolute exclusion rule.

### Key Concepts

- normal-appearing mucosa
- false reassurance
- clinical context

## Knowledge Block 21 — Can Intestinal Metaplasia Always Be Seen?

### Patient Explanation

No. NCI notes that intestinal metaplasia is not always visible during endoscopy and may be identified through gastric biopsies.

### Clinical Importance

This reinforces the broader principle that endoscopy and tissue examination provide complementary information.

### Key Concepts

- intestinal metaplasia
- endoscopic visibility
- biopsy

## Knowledge Block 22 — What Is the Difference Between Endoscopic and Pathologic Diagnosis?

### Patient Explanation

Endoscopic diagnosis describes what the clinician sees and where it is. Pathologic diagnosis examines sampled tissue under a microscope to determine what the abnormality actually is.

### Clinical Importance

The two steps should not be treated as interchangeable. Endoscopy helps identify and localize what should be investigated; pathology establishes the tissue diagnosis.

### Key Concepts

- endoscopic assessment
- pathology
- tissue diagnosis

## Knowledge Block 23 — What If the Endoscopic Finding and Pathology Result Do Not Agree?

### Patient Explanation

If the endoscopic appearance is strongly concerning but the first pathology result does not explain the finding, the clinical team may need to consider whether the tissue sample was adequate and whether additional evaluation is needed.

### Clinical Importance

Recognizing this discordance helps prevent false reassurance from an isolated nondiagnostic result.

### Key Concepts

- endoscopy-pathology discordance
- clinical reassessment

## Knowledge Block 24 — What Is the Role of EUS After Endoscopic Diagnosis?

### Patient Explanation

Endoscopic ultrasound, or EUS, adds ultrasound imaging to assess the layers of the stomach wall and nearby lymph nodes. NCCN describes it as an important component of initial clinical staging before treatment.

### Clinical Importance

EUS therefore extends beyond the primary visual diagnostic role of PP-0176 and belongs mainly to the staging pathway.

### Key Concepts

- EUS
- local staging
- wall invasion
- lymph nodes

## Knowledge Block 25 — What Is the Role of EMR or ESD in Selected Lesions?

### Patient Explanation

For selected small or early lesions, endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR) or endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) can provide a larger specimen than a routine biopsy. NCCN notes that this can provide more information about differentiation, lymphovascular invasion, and depth of infiltration.

### Clinical Importance

These procedures can have both diagnostic/staging and therapeutic implications, but the procedures themselves are downstream from this diagnostic package.

### Key Concepts

- EMR
- ESD
- larger specimen
- early gastric cancer

## Knowledge Block 26 — Does Endoscopy Provide Complete Cancer Staging?

### Patient Explanation

No. Endoscopy provides important information about the primary lesion, but complete staging may require pathology, EUS, CT, PET/CT, diagnostic laparoscopy, or other assessments depending on the clinical situation.

### Clinical Importance

Keeping endoscopic diagnosis separate from complete staging prevents overestimating what one examination can determine.

### Key Concepts

- diagnosis
- staging
- multimodal assessment

## Knowledge Block 27 — Can Endoscopic Findings Influence Later Treatment Planning?

### Patient Explanation

Yes. NCCN states that accurate documentation of tumor location helps with treatment planning and follow-up examinations.

### Clinical Importance

The important boundary is that PP-0176 provides diagnostic information that later decisions may use; it does not itself choose treatment.

### Key Concepts

- treatment planning interface
- location documentation

## Knowledge Block 28 — Is Every Abnormal Gastric Finding Cancer?

### Patient Explanation

No. Abnormal endoscopic findings can have different causes. A suspicious appearance identifies the need for appropriate evaluation; it is not a final diagnosis by itself.

### Clinical Importance

This is why tissue diagnosis and clinical context remain important.

### Key Concepts

- differential diagnosis
- suspicious finding

## Knowledge Block 29 — Does Every Patient Need the Same Endoscopic Evaluation?

### Patient Explanation

No. The extent and focus of diagnostic evaluation depend on the clinical presentation, the finding being investigated, and what information is already available.

### Clinical Importance

A patient-facing package should explain the principles without creating a rigid universal endoscopy algorithm.

### Key Concepts

- clinical context
- individualized work-up
- no universal algorithm

## Knowledge Block 30 — Does Diagnostic Endoscopy Have a Role After Treatment?

### Patient Explanation

Endoscopy may also be used for surveillance after treatment or in other clinical contexts, but those uses answer different questions from initial diagnostic endoscopy.

### Clinical Importance

Keeping these contexts separate prevents surveillance and treatment-related endoscopy from being absorbed into PP-0176.

### Key Concepts

- surveillance
- post-treatment endoscopy

## Knowledge Block 31 — Can Enhanced Endoscopic Imaging Help?

### Patient Explanation

Enhanced or specialized endoscopic imaging may improve visualization of subtle mucosal abnormalities in selected settings. The supplied sources support the general concept but do not establish one universal enhanced-imaging algorithm for all gastric-cancer diagnosis.

### Clinical Importance

PP-0176 can acknowledge the concept without inventing technical protocols or modality-specific thresholds that are not established in the project source set.

### Key Concepts

- enhanced imaging
- subtle lesions
- context-dependent

## Knowledge Block 32 — What Does a Complete Diagnostic Endoscopy Need to Achieve?

### Patient Explanation

Conceptually, an adequate examination should identify whether suspicious neoplastic disease is present, determine where it is located, characterize the abnormality, document the findings, and enable appropriate tissue evaluation when indicated.

### Clinical Importance

This combines the core NCCN diagnostic goals without turning PP-0176 into a technical procedural manual.

### Key Concepts

- presence
- location
- characterization
- documentation
- biopsy

# Common Misconceptions

### Myth 1

**“If the endoscopist sees cancer, the diagnosis is already final.”**

**Fact:** Endoscopy can strongly suggest cancer, but tissue examination is generally needed for definitive diagnosis.

### Myth 2

**“Every gastric cancer looks like a lump.”**

**Fact:** Gastric cancer can be focal, ulcerated, depressed, irregular, or diffuse/infiltrative.

### Myth 3

**“A normal-looking stomach always rules out cancer.”**

**Fact:** Microscopic or diffuse disease can be difficult to recognize visually in selected contexts.

### Myth 4

**“An ulcer seen during endoscopy means cancer.”**

**Fact:** An ulcer has multiple possible causes. Suspicious lesions may need tissue evaluation.

### Myth 5

**“A negative first biopsy proves that the suspicious lesion is benign.”**

**Fact:** A nondiagnostic sample may not adequately represent the lesion, particularly when disease is diffuse, deep, or heterogeneous.

### Myth 6

**“Endoscopy and biopsy are the same examination.”**

**Fact:** Endoscopy visualizes the stomach; biopsy obtains tissue for microscopic examination.

### Myth 7

**“Endoscopy can determine the Lauren type.”**

**Fact:** Lauren classification requires tissue-based pathologic assessment.

### Myth 8

**“Endoscopy can determine the molecular subtype.”**

**Fact:** Molecular classification and biomarker testing are downstream from endoscopic diagnosis.

### Myth 9

**“EUS is simply another name for ordinary endoscopy.”**

**Fact:** EUS adds ultrasound imaging and is mainly used to answer additional local-staging questions.

### Myth 10

**“EMR and ESD are only treatments.”**

**Fact:** In selected early lesions, they can also provide a larger specimen for diagnostic and staging information.

### Myth 11

**“Finding one lesion means the rest of the stomach does not need attention.”**

**Fact:** The examination should be adequate and systematic so additional or subtle abnormalities are not overlooked.

### Myth 12

**“Screening endoscopy and diagnostic endoscopy have the same purpose.”**

**Fact:** Screening evaluates an at-risk population; diagnostic endoscopy investigates a specific suspicion or abnormality.

### Myth 13

**“A normal endoscopy means no biopsy is ever relevant.”**

**Fact:** In selected high-risk or microscopic-disease settings, visual findings and tissue findings can differ.

### Myth 14

**“Every patient needs the same endoscopic sequence.”**

**Fact:** The diagnostic approach is adapted to the clinical question and available information.

### Myth 15

**“Endoscopy itself determines treatment.”**

**Fact:** Endoscopy supplies diagnostic and anatomical information; treatment decisions integrate pathology, staging and other clinical evidence.

# Key Messages

1. Diagnostic endoscopy is a central method for evaluating suspected gastric cancer.
2. Its core tasks are to determine whether suspicious neoplastic disease is present and where it is located.
3. Gastric cancer does not always appear as one obvious mass.
4. Ulcerated, depressed, irregular, and diffuse/infiltrative patterns may require careful evaluation.
5. Location, size, extent and relationship to the EGJ should be documented clearly.
6. Endoscopic appearance is not the same as tissue diagnosis.
7. Suspicious lesions generally require appropriate tissue sampling.
8. A negative or nondiagnostic first biopsy does not always resolve strong clinical or endoscopic suspicion.
9. Diffuse and microscopic disease can be difficult to detect visually.
10. EUS and endoscopic resection may provide additional diagnostic/staging information in selected situations but are downstream from the core visual-diagnostic role.
11. Endoscopy contributes information to later staging and treatment planning but does not independently determine treatment.
12. The diagnostic approach must remain clinically contextual rather than a rigid universal checklist.
