# B. Educational Objectives

After reading this Population Package, the reader should be able to:

1. Explain what endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) is.
2. Understand why ESD is a distinct endoscopic resection modality rather than simply a larger biopsy.
3. Explain the difference between ESD and endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR) at a conceptual level.
4. Understand why ESD can provide broader and more reliable en-bloc resection than EMR in selected lesions.
5. Understand why ESD is mainly considered for selected early gastric cancers rather than deeply invasive disease.
6. Recognize the major clinical factors used when considering ESD, including tumor size, histologic differentiation, ulceration, depth of invasion, lymphovascular invasion, margins, and expected lymph-node risk.
7. Understand the concept of classic versus expanded ESD indications.
8. Understand why superficial submucosal invasion and deep submucosal invasion have different treatment implications.
9. Understand why pre-treatment staging and, in selected patients, EUS are important before endoscopic resection.
10. Understand what information the ESD specimen can provide to pathology.
11. Understand the difference between technical removal and a pathologically curative ESD.
12. Understand what a non-curative or incomplete ESD result can mean for subsequent treatment.
13. Compare the strategic roles of ESD, EMR, and gastrectomy.
14. Understand the main benefits, limitations, bleeding/perforation risks, and expertise requirements associated with ESD.
15. Understand why high-volume, experienced centers matter.
16. Understand why ESD evidence is especially developed in East Asian settings.
17. Understand why surveillance remains important after curative endoscopic resection.
18. Identify practical questions to discuss with the treating team.
19. Recognize common misconceptions about ESD.
20. Understand that the final suitability of ESD is an individualized clinical decision and is not determined by a single size or pathology rule alone.

# D. Clinical Knowledge Blocks

## Block 1 — What Is ESD?

### Patient Explanation

Endoscopic submucosal dissection, or ESD, is a treatment that removes a selected early gastric cancer through an endoscope passed into the stomach.

Unlike an operation through the abdomen, ESD does not require a skin incision. The endoscope provides access to the lesion, and specialized instruments are used to remove the lesion together with an appropriate layer of tissue beneath it.

The important point is that ESD is a **therapeutic resection procedure**, not simply a biopsy.

### Clinical Importance

ESD is designed for situations in which the cancer is sufficiently superficial and has a sufficiently low expected risk of lymph-node spread that local removal can potentially provide adequate cancer treatment.

The procedure therefore depends on careful patient and lesion selection.

### Key Message

> ESD is a specialized endoscopic treatment for selected early gastric cancers. It is not a general treatment for all stomach cancers.

---

## Block 2 — Why Is ESD Different From a Biopsy?

A conventional biopsy usually removes a small sample to establish or characterize a diagnosis.

ESD removes a substantially larger, defined lesion specimen.

The larger specimen can allow the pathologist to assess features that are important for staging and curative assessment, including:

- histologic differentiation;
- depth of invasion;
- lymphovascular invasion;
- resection margins.

The NCCN source specifically describes EMR/ESD of small focal lesions as providing a larger specimen that can give greater information on differentiation, LVI, and depth of infiltration, with potential therapeutic effect.

### Key Message

> The specimen obtained by ESD is intended to answer both “What is this lesion?” and “Was it removed in a way that can be considered oncologically adequate?”

---

## Block 3 — Why Does ESD Go Into the Submucosal Layer?

The stomach wall has multiple layers.

Early gastric cancers can sometimes extend beyond the surface mucosa into the submucosal layer while remaining within a clinical setting where endoscopic treatment is considered.

ESD is designed to dissect within the submucosal plane so that the lesion can be removed more extensively and, when appropriate, in one piece.

This greater depth is one reason ESD can be more capable than EMR for selected lesions.

### Safety Distinction

“Deeper than EMR” does **not** mean that ESD is appropriate for deeply invasive cancer.

Deep submucosal invasion can indicate a higher risk of disease beyond the locally removed lesion and may make endoscopic resection inadequate.

---

## Block 4 — Why Is En-Bloc Resection Important?

En-bloc resection means removing the lesion as one specimen rather than fragmenting it into multiple pieces.

A single specimen makes it easier to evaluate:

- the full lesion;
- the depth of invasion;
- the relationship of tumor to the margins;
- other pathologic risk features.

NCCN describes ESD as more effective than EMR for en-bloc excision in several cited studies.

### Patient Meaning

A technically successful procedure is not judged only by whether the visible lesion was removed.

The specimen must also be suitable for a reliable pathology assessment.

---

## Block 5 — Who May Be Considered for ESD?

ESD is mainly considered for selected early gastric cancers.

The selection process considers multiple dimensions together:

1. Stage / depth of invasion.
2. Tumor size.
3. Histologic differentiation.
4. Ulceration.
5. Lymphovascular invasion risk.
6. Expected risk of lymph-node metastasis.
7. Ability to achieve complete resection.
8. Clinical staging information.
9. Expertise available at the treating center.

No single factor should be presented as an automatic self-selection rule.

### Key Message

> ESD suitability is a multidimensional clinical decision.

---

## Block 6 — What Are the Classic Endoscopic-Resection Features?

The project sources describe a classic favorable pattern centered on very early disease.

The Vietnamese gastric-cancer guidance identifies an absolute endoscopic-resection indication for a differentiated, non-ulcerated, clinically T1a tumor smaller than 2 cm.

NCCN similarly describes a favorable framework involving small lesions, favorable differentiation, superficial invasion, absence of LVI, and clear margins.

These criteria are best understood as a **clinical pattern**, not as a standalone checklist for self-diagnosis.

---

## Block 7 — What Are Expanded ESD Indications?

The project NCCN source identifies expanded ESD indications including selected:

- well/moderately differentiated cancers without ulceration, including larger lesions;
- well/moderately differentiated lesions with superficial submucosal invasion;
- well/moderately differentiated lesions smaller than 3 cm with ulceration;
- poorly differentiated lesions smaller than 2 cm without ulceration.

The Vietnamese guideline describes a similar expanded framework and specifically notes that ESD is used more often than EMR because it can remove a broader portion of the submucosa.

### Important Qualification

Expanded indications do not mean that every lesion meeting a numerical criterion is automatically cured by ESD.

Final pathology, LVI, margins, depth, and overall nodal-risk assessment remain important.

---

## Block 8 — Why Does Tumor Size Matter?

Size is one of the factors used to estimate whether an endoscopic resection can be performed appropriately.

Smaller lesions generally fit more readily into classic endoscopic-treatment frameworks.

Larger lesions may still be considered for ESD in selected settings, especially within expanded indications, because ESD has greater en-bloc and resection capability.

However:

> Size alone does not determine whether ESD is appropriate.

A larger lesion with favorable pathology may be considered in a different way from a smaller lesion with deep invasion or other high-risk features.

---

## Block 9 — Why Does Histologic Differentiation Matter?

Histologic differentiation describes how closely the cancer cells resemble the tissue pattern from which they arose.

The project guidance distinguishes well/moderately differentiated and poorly differentiated tumors in the endoscopic-resection framework.

This matters because histology contributes to estimating the likelihood that the cancer can be adequately treated by local resection.

The package does not teach the full pathology classification system. Instead, it explains why the pathology label matters to ESD decisions.

---

## Block 10 — Why Does Ulceration Matter?

Ulceration changes the clinical assessment of an early gastric lesion.

Classic favorable endoscopic-treatment patterns often involve lesions without ulceration.

However, the expanded ESD framework allows selected ulcerated lesions, including well/moderately differentiated lesions below a specified size threshold in the source guidance.

Therefore:

> Ulceration does not have one universal meaning across every ESD decision.

It must be interpreted together with histology, size, depth, and other risk features.

---

## Block 11 — Why Does Depth of Invasion Matter?

Depth is one of the most important determinants of whether local endoscopic treatment may be adequate.

A superficial lesion may have a sufficiently low risk of lymph-node spread for endoscopic treatment.

A deeper lesion can have a higher probability of lymphatic or nodal involvement.

The project NCCN framework distinguishes superficial submucosal invasion, including sm1-type depth, from deeper submucosal invasion.

### Core Principle

> ESD can reach the submucosal layer, but it does not replace lymph-node surgery when the biology suggests a meaningful risk of disease beyond the locally removed lesion.

---

## Block 12 — Why Does Lymph-Node Risk Matter?

ESD removes the primary gastric lesion.

It does not remove regional lymph nodes.

Therefore, ESD can be oncologically appropriate only when the expected risk of clinically meaningful nodal disease is sufficiently low.

This is the underlying principle behind endoscopic treatment of early gastric cancer.

### Patient Explanation

A doctor is not asking only:

> “Can we remove this tumor through an endoscope?”

The more important question is:

> “If we remove this tumor locally, is that likely to provide adequate cancer treatment without needing lymph-node surgery?”

---

## Block 13 — Why Is Lymphovascular Invasion Important?

Lymphovascular invasion, or LVI, means that cancer cells are identified within lymphatic or blood-vessel spaces in the specimen.

The project NCCN guidance includes absence of LVI among features supporting a curative endoscopic-resection assessment.

LVI matters because it can indicate that the cancer has a pathway for spread beyond the locally removed lesion.

### Key Message

> LVI is one of the pathology findings that can change an ESD from potentially curative to requiring additional treatment consideration.

---

## Block 14 — What Happens Before ESD?

Before treatment, the team needs enough information to judge whether an endoscopic approach is reasonable.

The project NCCN source states that EUS performed before treatment can provide information about:

- depth of tumor invasion;
- regional lymph nodes;
- selected surrounding-organ findings.

This is particularly relevant when a patient is being considered for EMR or ESD.

### Boundary

This package explains **why** pre-treatment staging matters.

It does not teach the detailed EUS procedure or complete staging methodology.

---

## Block 15 — What Does the ESD Specimen Tell the Pathologist?

The ESD specimen can provide a more complete assessment than a small biopsy.

The relevant assessment includes:

- tumor histology;
- differentiation;
- depth of invasion;
- lymphovascular invasion;
- lateral margins;
- deep margins;
- completeness of removal.

NCCN specifically requires expert gastrointestinal pathology assessment of the endoscopic resection specimen.

### Patient Meaning

The procedure is not the final step.

The pathology report is what helps determine whether the ESD achieved an oncologically adequate result.

---

## Block 16 — What Makes an ESD Potentially Curative?

A curative assessment requires more than technical removal.

The project source framework includes features such as:

- appropriate lesion characteristics;
- superficial invasion;
- favorable differentiation where applicable;
- complete excision;
- negative lateral and deep margins;
- absence of LVI.

The exact combination depends on the clinical framework and indication category.

### Core Principle

> Technical success and oncologic cure are not the same thing.

---

## Block 17 — What Is a Non-Curative ESD?

An ESD can remove the visible lesion but still reveal pathology features suggesting that local endoscopic treatment was not sufficient.

Examples include:

- deep submucosal invasion;
- LVI;
- positive lateral or deep margins;
- unfavorable pathology features;
- evidence of lymph-node disease.

NCCN describes these findings as reasons an endoscopic resection may be considered incomplete.

### Patient Meaning

A non-curative ESD does not necessarily mean that the procedure was a failure.

The specimen may have provided the information needed to recognize that additional treatment is required.

---

## Block 18 — When Might Surgery Be Needed After ESD?

If the final pathology indicates a meaningful risk that cancer remains or may have spread beyond the endoscopically treated area, additional treatment can be necessary.

The project NCCN guidance states that additional gastrectomy with lymphadenectomy should be considered when endoscopic resection is incomplete because of relevant high-risk features.

The project ACS source similarly explains that surgery may be recommended when it is unclear whether all of the cancer was removed after an endoscopic procedure.

### Boundary

PP-0195 explains **why additional surgery may become necessary**.

Detailed gastrectomy and lymphadenectomy belong to surgical Population Packages.

---

## Block 19 — ESD Versus EMR

| Dimension | EMR | ESD |
|---|---|---|
| Main concept | Endoscopic mucosal resection | Endoscopic submucosal dissection |
| Resection depth | More superficial | Extends into submucosal plane |
| En-bloc capability | More limited in selected lesions | Greater |
| Technical complexity | Lower | Higher |
| Expanded lesion capability | More limited | Broader in selected indications |
| Bleeding/perforation context | Endoscopic risk | Greater technical and complication burden |
| Main value | Selected favorable lesions | Selected lesions needing more extensive/en-bloc resection |

NCCN reports greater en-bloc effectiveness with ESD in several studies and identifies ESD as preferred within its current endoscopic-resection framework.

### Key Message

> ESD is not simply “better EMR.” It is a different modality with greater resection capability and greater technical complexity.

---

## Block 20 — Why Is ESD Often Preferred in the Current NCCN Framework?

The project NCCN source states that ESD is preferred in the endoscopic-resection framework.

The rationale is related to its ability to achieve more effective en-bloc resection and to manage lesions for which a more extensive endoscopic resection is desirable.

However, ESD also requires greater skill and has higher bleeding/perforation risk.

Therefore:

> A preferred modality in a guideline framework is not the same as an automatic recommendation for every patient.

---

## Block 21 — ESD Versus Gastrectomy

| Dimension | ESD | Gastrectomy |
|---|---|---|
| Access | Through an endoscope | Abdominal surgery |
| Stomach preservation | Yes, when successful | Partial or total stomach removal depending on operation |
| Primary target | Selected local early lesion | Primary tumor plus regional oncologic treatment |
| Lymph-node dissection | No | Yes, when indicated |
| Main advantage | Less invasive local treatment | More comprehensive treatment when nodal/deeper disease is a concern |
| Main limitation | Cannot adequately treat significant nodal/deep disease | Greater operative burden |

ESD is therefore best understood as a **stomach-preserving oncologic strategy for selected early disease**, not as a replacement for surgery in all gastric cancer.

---

## Block 22 — What Are the Main Benefits of ESD?

Potential benefits include:

- no abdominal skin incision;
- preservation of the stomach;
- local treatment of selected early disease;
- ability to obtain a large specimen;
- improved en-bloc resection capability;
- potential definitive treatment when pathology supports curative resection.

These benefits are meaningful only when the procedure is oncologically appropriate.

---

## Block 23 — What Are the Main Risks and Limitations?

ESD is technically demanding.

The project sources specifically identify:

- bleeding;
- perforation;
- greater procedural complexity;
- need for specialized expertise.

Other limitations include:

- not every lesion is appropriate;
- deep or high-risk disease may require surgery;
- pathology may reveal non-curative features;
- availability and expertise vary between centers.

This package does not provide detailed complication-management instructions.

---

## Block 24 — Why Does the Treatment Center Matter?

The project NCCN guidance recommends experienced centers.

ACS also explains that not all physicians are trained in ESD and that it may not be available in some centers.

This is especially relevant because the technical complexity of ESD affects:

- ability to achieve complete/en-bloc resection;
- management of procedural complications;
- quality of specimen assessment;
- appropriate patient selection.

### Patient Question

> “How often does this center and this endoscopist perform ESD for early gastric cancer?”

---

## Block 25 — Why Is ESD Especially Developed in East Asia?

The project ACS and NCCN sources explain that EMR and ESD have been used more extensively in East Asian countries such as Japan.

One reason is that gastric cancer is more common there and is more often detected at an early stage.

This creates:

- larger clinical experience;
- more specialized operators;
- more high-volume centers;
- more established endoscopic-treatment infrastructure.

This contextualizes the evidence without implying that results from every Asian cohort automatically apply to every healthcare system.

---

## Block 26 — What Outcomes Have Been Reported?

The project NCCN source summarizes comparative evidence showing that ESD can improve en-bloc/complete resection in selected settings.

It cites a multicenter retrospective study reporting a 3-year recurrence-free rate of approximately:

- 98% after ESD;
- 93% after EMR.

The same source reports better complete-resection rates with ESD for lesions larger than 5 cm in cited studies.

These are **study-level observations**, not individualized predictions.

### Key Message

> Evidence supports ESD as an effective treatment for appropriately selected early gastric cancer, but individual outcome depends on lesion characteristics, pathology, expertise, and clinical context.

---

## Block 27 — Why Is Follow-Up Still Needed?

Curative endoscopic treatment does not mean that the patient never needs another endoscopy.

The project NCCN source recommends endoscopic surveillance after endoscopic resection for early-stage Tis/T1a disease, with the schedule depending on disease depth and treatment context.

Surveillance can help detect:

- recurrent disease;
- new gastric lesions;
- other clinically important findings.

### Key Message

> Curative ESD changes the treatment pathway; it does not end the need for follow-up.

---

## Block 28 — What Questions Should I Ask Before ESD?

Useful questions include:

1. Why is ESD being considered instead of EMR or surgery?
2. How deep does the team believe the tumor extends?
3. What is the tumor size?
4. What is the histologic differentiation?
5. Is there ulceration?
6. What is the estimated risk of lymph-node spread?
7. Is EUS or another staging assessment needed before ESD?
8. Does this lesion fit a classic or expanded ESD indication?
9. How often does the center perform ESD?
10. How experienced is the endoscopist with gastric ESD?
11. What pathology findings will determine whether the ESD is considered curative?
12. What happens if the margins are positive?
13. What happens if deeper invasion or LVI is found?
14. Could surgery still be needed after ESD?
15. What follow-up endoscopy will be needed?

# E. Common Misconceptions

## Myth 1

**“ESD is just a larger biopsy.”**

**Fact:** ESD is a therapeutic endoscopic resection procedure for selected early gastric cancer. The specimen can also provide important staging and pathology information.

## Myth 2

**“ESD is simply a deeper EMR.”**

**Fact:** ESD is a distinct modality designed to dissect through the submucosal plane and facilitate more extensive/en-bloc resection.

## Myth 3

**“Because ESD goes deeper, it can treat deeply invasive cancer.”**

**Fact:** Deep submucosal invasion can indicate that endoscopic treatment is inadequate and that additional surgery may be needed.

## Myth 4

**“ESD is always better than EMR.”**

**Fact:** ESD has greater resection capability and is preferred in the current NCCN framework, but it is more technically demanding and has greater bleeding/perforation risk.

## Myth 5

**“If the endoscopist removed the visible tumor, I am cured.”**

**Fact:** Final pathology is needed to determine whether the resection meets curative criteria.

## Myth 6

**“Negative margins alone prove cure.”**

**Fact:** Depth, differentiation, LVI, and other pathology findings also influence curative assessment.

## Myth 7

**“ESD is only for tumors smaller than 2 cm.”**

**Fact:** Expanded ESD indications can include selected larger lesions.

## Myth 8

**“Ulceration always rules out ESD.”**

**Fact:** Selected ulcerated lesions are included in expanded ESD indications.

## Myth 9

**“Poorly differentiated cancer can never be treated with ESD.”**

**Fact:** Selected poorly differentiated lesions are included in the expanded ESD framework, subject to the full clinical-pathologic assessment.

## Myth 10

**“ESD eliminates the need to think about lymph nodes.”**

**Fact:** Low expected lymph-node risk is fundamental to the rationale for endoscopic treatment.

## Myth 11

**“ESD is a simple endoscopy procedure.”**

**Fact:** ESD requires substantial technical expertise.

## Myth 12

**“Any endoscopy center can perform ESD equally well.”**

**Fact:** Guidelines emphasize experienced, high-volume centers.

## Myth 13

**“A successful ESD means I will definitely avoid surgery.”**

**Fact:** Surgery may still be needed if pathology demonstrates non-curative features.

## Myth 14

**“After curative ESD, no further follow-up is necessary.”**

**Fact:** Endoscopic surveillance remains part of care.

# F. Key Concepts

## Concept 1 — Local treatment versus regional disease

ESD removes the local lesion.

Regional lymph-node disease is a separate oncologic problem.

This distinction explains why ESD is restricted to carefully selected early cancers.

## Concept 2 — Technical success versus oncologic adequacy

A visible lesion can be removed successfully while pathology still shows features requiring additional treatment.

## Concept 3 — En-bloc resection

Removing a lesion in one piece can improve pathologic assessment and margin evaluation.

## Concept 4 — Expanded indication does not equal automatic cure

Expanded indications identify situations in which ESD may be considered. Curative status still depends on the final specimen and overall clinical context.

## Concept 5 — Pathology is part of treatment assessment

The ESD specimen is not merely a record of what was removed. It is evidence used to determine whether the treatment was adequate.

## Concept 6 — Expertise is part of the treatment environment

ESD is a specialized procedure. Center and operator experience matter.

# H. Patient Safety Messages

1. Do not assume that a tumor size alone determines whether ESD is appropriate.
2. Do not assume that an early stage automatically means endoscopic treatment is appropriate.
3. Ask how tumor depth was assessed.
4. Ask whether lymph-node risk has been considered.
5. Ask whether the lesion fits classic or expanded ESD criteria.
6. Ask about center and operator experience.
7. Understand that final pathology can change the treatment plan.
8. Understand that surgery can still be needed after ESD.
9. Do not interpret a pathology feature in isolation without the treating team.
10. Follow the recommended surveillance plan after treatment.
