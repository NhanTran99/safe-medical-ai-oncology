# Educational Question

> **What does MSI-H/dMMR mean in gastric cancer, and why does it matter for immunotherapy?**

This Population Package explains MSI-H/dMMR as a clinically meaningful gastric-cancer phenotype and explains why this result can materially alter the relevance of immune checkpoint inhibition.

It is deliberately positioned downstream of MSI/MMR testing and alongside, but distinct from, the broader immunotherapy and immune-checkpoint-inhibitor packages.

---

# Educational Objectives

After reading this Population Package, the reader should be able to:

1. Explain in plain language what MSI-H and dMMR mean.
2. Understand why MSI-H/dMMR tumors are biologically and clinically distinct from MSS/pMMR tumors.
3. Explain why MSI-H/dMMR status is particularly relevant to immune checkpoint inhibition.
4. Understand that selected MSI-H/dMMR treatment options may be used independently of PD-L1 expression.
5. Recognize the major evidence supporting checkpoint inhibition in MSI-H/dMMR gastric/EGJ adenocarcinoma.
6. Understand the difference between advanced/metastatic and selected neoadjuvant/perioperative evidence.
7. Understand that MSI-H/dMMR is not synonymous with PD-L1 positivity, TMB-H, or Lynch syndrome.
8. Recognize the major limitations and uncertainties of MSI-H/dMMR immunotherapy evidence.
9. Know which detailed questions belong to testing, PD-L1, TMB, genetics, response-assessment, and toxicity Population Packages.

# Clinical Knowledge Blocks

## 1. What are MSI-H and dMMR?

**Microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H)** describes a tumor with a high level of instability in short repetitive DNA sequences called microsatellites.

**Mismatch repair deficiency (dMMR)** describes a tumor in which the DNA mismatch-repair system is deficient.

These are closely related biological findings, but they are not simply two names for one identical laboratory test.

At the clinical level, both identify a tumor state in which abnormal DNA repair is associated with microsatellite instability and a distinct pattern of tumor biology.

For this Population Package, the important point is not how the laboratory performs the test. The important point is what the established MSI-H/dMMR result means for gastric-cancer biology and treatment.

---

## 2. Why can MSI-H/dMMR matter so much for immunotherapy?

A mismatch-repair-deficient tumor can accumulate DNA replication errors. When these alterations produce abnormal tumor-derived signals, the immune system may have more opportunities to recognize the tumor.

Checkpoint inhibitors do not directly repair the mismatch-repair defect.

Instead, they act on inhibitory immune pathways. By releasing checkpoint-mediated inhibition, they can allow an existing antitumor immune response to become more effective.

The clinically important chain is therefore:

**MMR deficiency**
→ **microsatellite instability**
→ **distinct tumor genomic/antigenic features**
→ **greater potential immune recognition**
→ **greater relevance of immune checkpoint blockade**

This is a biological rationale, not a guarantee of response in an individual patient.

---

## 3. MSI-H/dMMR is a clinical phenotype, not merely a laboratory label

NCCN describes MSI-H/dMMR gastric tumors as having distinct clinicopathologic characteristics and notes an association with more favorable prognosis compared with MSS/MMR-proficient tumors.

Reported associations include factors such as older age, intestinal-type histology, and more distal gastric location.

These are associations rather than diagnostic rules.

A patient should not infer MSI-H/dMMR status from age, tumor location, or histologic appearance alone.

---

## 4. Prognostic and predictive meaning are different

MSI-H/dMMR can have **prognostic** meaning because MSI-H/dMMR tumors have been associated with a different natural history and, in some datasets, more favorable outcomes.

It also has **predictive/treatment-relevant** meaning because checkpoint inhibitors can have substantial activity in MSI-H/dMMR tumors.

These are different concepts.

A prognostic association describes what tends to happen because of tumor biology.

A predictive association describes whether a biomarker helps identify a population more likely to benefit from a particular treatment.

For PP-0215, the predictive relevance to immunotherapy is the central clinical question.

---

## 5. MSI-H/dMMR is not the same as MSS/pMMR

**MSS/pMMR** refers to the contrasting tumor state in which high microsatellite instability or mismatch-repair deficiency is not demonstrated.

The distinction matters because the evidence for checkpoint-inhibitor benefit is not uniform across these populations.

The particularly strong treatment signal seen in MSI-H/dMMR disease should not be generalized automatically to MSS/pMMR tumors.

For example, in CheckMate-649, the MSI-H subgroup showed a strong signal with nivolumab plus ipilimumab, while the microsatellite-stable cohort did not show the same survival benefit.

---

## 6. MSI-H/dMMR is not the same as PD-L1 positivity

PD-L1 is a separate biomarker.

A tumor can be:

- MSI-H/dMMR and PD-L1 positive;
- MSI-H/dMMR and PD-L1 negative;
- or have other combinations of biomarker findings.

NCCN specifically recommends selected MSI-H/dMMR checkpoint-inhibitor strategies independently of PD-L1 status.

Therefore:

**MSI-H/dMMR pathway**
and
**PD-L1-guided pathway**

must remain conceptually distinct.

This does not mean that PD-L1 is irrelevant in every clinical decision. It means that MSI-H/dMMR can itself define an important immunotherapy context.

---

## 7. MSI-H/dMMR is related to TMB-H, but they are not interchangeable

MSI-H/dMMR tumors can have increased mutation burden, but:

> **MSI-H/dMMR is not simply another name for TMB-H.**

TMB is a separate biomarker concept with its own testing and interpretation.

NCCN discusses TMB-H separately and notes an important gastric-cancer evidence limitation in the KEYNOTE-158 TMB analysis: no gastroesophageal cancer patients were included in that particular TMB analysis.

Detailed TMB belongs to the dedicated TMB Population Package.

---

## 8. Why the advanced/metastatic evidence is important

The most mature MSI-H/dMMR immunotherapy evidence in gastric/EGJ adenocarcinoma comes from advanced or metastatic disease.

The supplied NCCN v2.2026 source identifies:

- pembrolizumab monotherapy;
- pembrolizumab plus fluoropyrimidine/oxaliplatin;
- nivolumab plus fluoropyrimidine/oxaliplatin;
- nivolumab plus ipilimumab;

as relevant MSI-H/dMMR treatment strategies in guideline-defined contexts.

The exact role depends on treatment line, disease setting, prior therapy, and the rest of the patient's clinical information.

This Population Package explains the MSI-H/dMMR treatment context rather than issuing an individualized treatment prescription.

---

## 9. Pembrolizumab evidence

The MSI-H signal is visible across several pembrolizumab studies.

### KEYNOTE-059

Among seven previously treated gastric/EGJ patients with MSI-H tumors, four had an objective response, giving an ORR of 57.1%. Median OS and PFS were not reached in this small subgroup.

### KEYNOTE-061

Among the MSI-H subgroup, median OS was not reached with pembrolizumab versus 8.1 months with paclitaxel.

Median PFS was 17.8 versus 3.5 months, and ORR was 46.7% versus 16.7%.

### KEYNOTE-062

Among treatment-naive MSI-H patients with locally advanced/unresectable or metastatic disease, median OS was not reached with pembrolizumab or pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy versus 8.5 months with chemotherapy.

ORR was 57.1% with pembrolizumab, 64.7% with pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy, and 36.8% with chemotherapy.

These subgroup findings are clinically important but should not be interpreted as direct head-to-head comparisons between treatment strategies.

---

## 10. Tumor-agnostic pembrolizumab evidence

KEYNOTE-158 provides a tumor-agnostic context for pembrolizumab in MSI-H/dMMR solid tumors.

The updated analysis included 351 patients across 28 tumor types. The overall ORR was 30.8%, with a median duration of response of 47.5 months.

Among 42 patients with gastric cancer, ORR was 31%.

The correct interpretation is:

**tumor-agnostic evidence supports the biological and clinical relevance of MSI-H/dMMR,**

but

**tumor-agnostic evidence should not be presented as identical to gastric-specific randomized evidence.**

---

## 11. Nivolumab plus chemotherapy

CheckMate-649 provides important gastric/EGJ evidence.

In the MSI-H subgroup, the reported survival benefit was particularly pronounced.

At the reported follow-up:

- among MSI-H tumors with PD-L1 CPS ≥5, median OS was 44.8 versus 8.8 months, HR 0.29;
- across randomized MSI-H patients, median OS was 38.7 versus 12.3 months, HR 0.34.

NCCN v2.2026 therefore recommends nivolumab plus fluoropyrimidine/oxaliplatin as a category 2A preferred first-line option for MSI-H/dMMR tumors independently of PD-L1 status.

These numbers are subgroup results and should not be interpreted as an individualized prediction of survival.

---

## 12. Nivolumab plus ipilimumab

CheckMate-649 also provides a particularly important MSI-H observation for dual checkpoint blockade.

In MSI-H tumors:

- median OS was not reached versus 10 months with chemotherapy;
- HR was 0.28;
- ORR was 70% versus 57%.

The same survival benefit was not observed in the microsatellite-stable cohort.

This illustrates why MSI-H/dMMR is clinically important as a biomarker-defined treatment context.

NCCN v2.2026 lists nivolumab plus ipilimumab as a category 2A preferred first-line option and a category 2A option useful in certain circumstances in second/subsequent-line treatment for MSI-H/dMMR tumors.

---

## 13. Subsequent-line treatment context

For recurrent or metastatic MSI-H/dMMR disease after previous treatment, NCCN includes selected checkpoint-inhibitor options such as:

- pembrolizumab;
- nivolumab plus ipilimumab;
- dostarlimab-gxly.

The exact choice depends on the treatment history and current clinical context.

This package therefore explains that MSI-H/dMMR can continue to influence treatment options beyond first-line therapy, but it does not construct a universal sequencing algorithm.

---

## 14. Dostarlimab

Dostarlimab is relevant to MSI-H/dMMR disease in selected guideline-defined contexts.

The package should present it as a treatment option supported by the project source set, while preserving the distinction between:

- evidence in mismatch-repair-deficient solid tumors broadly;
- gastric/EGJ-specific evidence;
- the precise guideline-defined clinical setting.

It should not be presented as interchangeable with every other checkpoint inhibitor in every treatment setting.

---

## 15. Neoadjuvant and perioperative immunotherapy

MSI-H/dMMR has importance beyond metastatic disease.

NCCN v2.2026 states that, for certain patients with MSI-H/dMMR gastric tumors, immunotherapy in the neoadjuvant or perioperative setting may be an option.

This includes selected use of:

- nivolumab plus ipilimumab followed by nivolumab;
- pembrolizumab;
- selected other checkpoint-inhibitor strategies in guideline-defined contexts.

This is an evolving area and should be described as **selected / useful in certain circumstances**, not as a universal standard for every resectable MSI-H/dMMR tumor.

---

## 16. NEONIPIGA

The GERCOR NEONIPIGA phase II trial is a major evidence landmark.

It enrolled 32 patients with locally advanced gastric or EGJ adenocarcinoma and confirmed MSI-H/dMMR.

Patients received neoadjuvant nivolumab plus ipilimumab followed by adjuvant nivolumab.

Twenty-nine patients underwent R0 resection, and approximately 59% achieved a pathologic complete response.

Among 31 patients evaluated for survival, 97% had a survival benefit at the reported follow-up.

These findings provide an important proof-of-concept for deep response to neoadjuvant checkpoint inhibition in MSI-H/dMMR disease.

However, NEONIPIGA was a phase II study with a small population. Its results should not be represented as proof that every MSI-H/dMMR patient should receive the same perioperative regimen.

---

## 17. Complete response does not automatically resolve the surgical question

A deep or complete response after neoadjuvant immunotherapy creates a clinically important question:

> If no residual disease can be demonstrated, is surgery still required?

The answer is not universally established.

NCCN identifies uncertainty around the role of surgery after biopsy-proven and radiologic/metabolic complete response following neoadjuvant immunotherapy.

Therefore this package should communicate uncertainty rather than create a surgical algorithm.

---

## 18. Other perioperative evidence

Additional phase II and evolving studies have evaluated checkpoint inhibition in MSI-H/dMMR tumors in the neoadjuvant/perioperative setting.

For example, a phase II study of neoadjuvant pembrolizumab in locally advanced MSI-H/dMMR solid tumors reported an ORR of 82% among assessed patients and a pathologic complete response rate of 65% among the surgically resected group.

However, most participants had colorectal cancer rather than gastric cancer.

Therefore this evidence should be treated as supportive biological evidence rather than equivalent to gastric-specific evidence.

The package should always distinguish:

**gastric/EGJ-specific evidence**

from

**broader MSI-H/dMMR solid-tumor evidence.**

---

## 19. Response depth and durability

One of the clinically important features of MSI-H/dMMR immunotherapy is that responses can be deep and durable.

Examples include:

- durable responses in pembrolizumab studies;
- high response rates in MSI-H subgroup analyses;
- pathologic complete responses in neoadjuvant studies.

However:

> **A population-level high response rate is not an individual guarantee.**

Some MSI-H/dMMR tumors do not respond, and acquired resistance can occur.

---

## 20. What if immunotherapy does not work?

MSI-H/dMMR increases the relevance of checkpoint inhibition but does not eliminate biological heterogeneity.

Possible clinical situations include:

- primary non-response;
- partial response followed by progression;
- durable control followed by acquired resistance.

The detailed molecular mechanisms of resistance are outside this package.

At the patient-facing level, the important message is:

> MSI-H/dMMR identifies a treatment-relevant tumor context, not a guarantee of treatment success.

---

## 21. MSI-H/dMMR and hereditary cancer

An MSI-H/dMMR tumor can raise a separate question:

> Could the mismatch-repair abnormality be related to an inherited condition?

The answer is:

> **Possibly, but an MSI-H/dMMR tumor result does not by itself establish Lynch syndrome or another inherited disorder.**

Appropriate clinical circumstances may lead to genetic counseling or further hereditary assessment.

The detailed hereditary pathway is delegated to the genetics Population Packages.

---

## 22. Patient-facing interpretation of an MSI-H/dMMR result

A useful way to understand the result is:

**Your tumor has a biological feature called MSI-H and/or dMMR.**

↓

**This feature can make the tumor particularly relevant to immune checkpoint treatment.**

↓

**Your oncology team combines this result with stage, resectability, prior treatment, other biomarkers, overall health, and treatment goals.**

↓

**The MSI-H/dMMR result can therefore change which treatment options are considered.**

This is the intended clinical meaning of the result.

It is not a treatment order.

# Common Misconceptions

## Myth 1 — “MSI-H is the same as PD-L1 positive.”

**Fact:** They are different biomarkers.

---

## Myth 2 — “MSI-H means TMB-H.”

**Fact:** They can be biologically related but are distinct biomarkers.

---

## Myth 3 — “MSI-H means immunotherapy will definitely work.”

**Fact:** MSI-H/dMMR is associated with a strong population-level immunotherapy signal, but individual responses vary.

---

## Myth 4 — “If a tumor is MSI-H, PD-L1 never matters.”

**Fact:** Selected MSI-H/dMMR treatment options can be independent of PD-L1, but the complete treatment context can still include other biomarkers and clinical factors.

---

## Myth 5 — “MSI-H automatically means Lynch syndrome.”

**Fact:** Tumor MSI-H/dMMR can prompt hereditary assessment in appropriate circumstances but does not by itself prove inherited disease.

---

## Myth 6 — “All MSI-H immunotherapy evidence is equally strong.”

**Fact:** Evidence varies by drug, trial design, treatment setting, population, and treatment line.

---

## Myth 7 — “If MSI-H responds well, MSS disease should respond the same way.”

**Fact:** The evidence pattern is different, and strong MSI-H subgroup findings should not simply be generalized to MSS/pMMR tumors.

---

## Myth 8 — “A complete response before surgery means surgery is unnecessary.”

**Fact:** The role of surgery after complete response following neoadjuvant immunotherapy remains uncertain in some clinical contexts.

---

# Key Messages

1. **MSI-H and dMMR identify a clinically important tumor phenotype in gastric cancer.**
2. **MSI-H/dMMR is particularly relevant to immune checkpoint inhibition.**
3. **MSI-H/dMMR and PD-L1 are different biomarkers.**
4. **MSI-H/dMMR and TMB-H are related but not interchangeable.**
5. **Selected MSI-H/dMMR immunotherapy strategies can be independent of PD-L1 expression.**
6. **Advanced/metastatic MSI-H/dMMR disease has substantial checkpoint-inhibitor evidence.**
7. **Selected MSI-H/dMMR tumors may also be considered for neoadjuvant/perioperative immunotherapy.**
8. **The evidence is strong but not uniform across drugs and settings.**
9. **MSI-H/dMMR does not guarantee an individual response.**
10. **An MSI-H/dMMR tumor finding does not automatically mean Lynch syndrome.**
11. **Detailed testing, PD-L1, TMB, genetics, toxicity, response assessment, and treatment sequencing belong to dedicated Population Packages.**
12. **The MSI-H/dMMR result should be interpreted together with the complete clinical context.**
