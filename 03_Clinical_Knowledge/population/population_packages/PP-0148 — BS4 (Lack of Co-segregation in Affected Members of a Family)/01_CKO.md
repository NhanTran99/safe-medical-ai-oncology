# Knowledge Block 1 — What Is BS4?

## Patient Explanation

**BS4** is an ACMG/AMP **Strong Benign Evidence** criterion.

It concerns a situation in which a genetic variant does **not segregate with disease** in affected members of a family.

In simple terms:

> If a genetic variant truly causes a familial disorder, it would generally be expected to occur in family members who have that disorder.

If an appropriately characterized affected family member **does not carry the variant**, this lack of co-segregation can provide evidence that the variant may not be responsible for the disease.

---

## Clinical Importance

BS4 uses family information to ask whether the genetic variant behaves as expected for a disease-causing variant.

# Knowledge Block 2 — What Is Co-segregation?

## Patient Explanation

**Co-segregation** describes whether a genetic variant tends to occur together with a disease within a family.

If affected family members consistently carry a particular variant while unaffected family members do not, the variant may be said to co-segregate with the disease.

This type of family evidence can support a pathogenic interpretation.

BS4 addresses the opposite pattern.

---

# Knowledge Block 3 — What Is Lack of Co-segregation?

## Patient Explanation

**Lack of co-segregation** means that the disease and the variant do not consistently occur together in an informative family.

For BS4, an important example is:

> **An appropriately affected family member has the disease but does not carry the variant being evaluated.**

If the disease model predicts that the variant should be present in that affected relative, the absence of the variant can provide evidence against pathogenicity.

---

# Knowledge Block 4 — Why Does the Affected Person Need to Be Truly Affected?

## Patient Explanation

A family member should only be considered "affected" for BS4 if their condition genuinely matches the disease or hereditary syndrome being evaluated.

This is especially important in cancer.

For example:

> A relative has gastric cancer

does not automatically mean:

> The relative has the hereditary gastric-cancer syndrome being evaluated.

The cancer may have another explanation.

---

## Clinical Importance

Incorrectly assigning a relative as affected can create apparent non-segregation that is not actually informative.

---

# Knowledge Block 5 — Why Does the Inheritance Pattern Matter?

## Patient Explanation

Different genetic disorders are inherited in different ways.

A variant may be expected to track with disease differently depending on whether the disorder is:

- dominant;
- recessive;
- X-linked;
- or associated with another inheritance mechanism.

Therefore, a family member who does not carry a variant is informative only when the disease model predicts that the person should have carried it if the variant were truly responsible for the disease.

---

# Knowledge Block 6 — Why Does Penetrance and Age Matter?

## Patient Explanation

**Penetrance** describes how likely people carrying a disease-causing variant are to actually develop the associated condition.

Some disorders also develop only after a particular age.

Therefore, family interpretation must consider:

- whether the disease is fully or partially penetrant;
- whether the family member is old enough to assess their disease status;
- whether the person's phenotype is sufficiently established.

These factors can affect whether a family member is truly informative for segregation analysis.

---

# Knowledge Block 7 — What Is a Phenocopy?

## Patient Explanation

A **phenocopy** is a condition that resembles the disease associated with a genetic variant but is caused by something else.

This is particularly important in cancer.

For example, a family member may develop gastric cancer without carrying a hereditary gastric-cancer variant because their cancer arose through another mechanism.

Therefore:

> **Gastric cancer without the familial variant does not automatically prove that the familial variant is benign.**

---

# Knowledge Block 8 — Can a Family Have More Than One Genetic Cause?

## Patient Explanation

Yes.

Different genetic causes can occasionally contribute to disease within the same family.

If an affected relative has another disease-causing variant, their lack of the variant being evaluated may not represent true evidence against that variant.

Alternative genetic explanations therefore need to be considered.

---

# Knowledge Block 9 — Does One Affected Relative Without the Variant Prove Benignity?

## Patient Explanation

No.

A genotype-negative affected relative may provide useful evidence, but its meaning depends on:

- the certainty of the phenotype;
- the reliability of the genetic result;
- the inheritance pattern;
- penetrance;
- age;
- possible phenocopies;
- possible alternative genetic causes;
- disease-specific interpretation rules.

BS4 is evidence within a broader variant-classification framework.

---

# Knowledge Block 10 — Why Is BS4 Important in Gastric Cancer?

## Patient Explanation

Some people with gastric cancer undergo genetic testing because their cancer may be associated with an inherited cancer-predisposition syndrome.

When interpreting a variant in such a setting, family information may help determine whether the variant tracks with the hereditary cancer phenotype.

However:

> **A family member having gastric cancer is not automatically equivalent to having the specific hereditary gastric-cancer syndrome under evaluation.**

The relevant phenotype and disease model must be established first.

---

# Knowledge Block 11 — BS4 and Disease-Specific Rules

## Patient Explanation

The general ACMG/AMP framework provides the concept of BS4, but individual diseases and genes may have additional rules.

A disease-specific expert panel may:

- define which relatives are informative;
- specify phenotype requirements;
- consider age and penetrance;
- modify the evidence strength;
- restrict the situations in which BS4 can be used;
- or determine that BS4 should not be used for a particular gene or disease.

Therefore, BS4 should not be applied as a universal family-counting rule.

# Common Misconceptions

**Myth 1**

An affected relative does not have the variant, so the variant is benign.

**Fact**

Not necessarily. The relative may have a phenocopy or another genetic cause.

---

**Myth 2**

Anyone with gastric cancer in the family can be counted as an affected relative.

**Fact**

The phenotype must correspond to the hereditary gastric-cancer disorder being evaluated.

---

**Myth 3**

BS4 applies identically to every hereditary gastric-cancer gene.

**Fact**

Disease- and gene-specific ClinGen specifications may modify or exclude BS4.

---

**Myth 4**

BS4 and PP1 mean the same thing.

**Fact**

PP1 concerns co-segregation supporting pathogenicity, whereas BS4 concerns lack of segregation supporting benign interpretation.

# Key Messages

- **BS4** is a Strong Benign Evidence criterion.
- BS4 concerns **lack of co-segregation** between a variant and disease in an informative family.
- An appropriately affected family member who does not carry the variant can provide evidence against pathogenicity.
- The family member must genuinely have the relevant disease phenotype.
- Inheritance pattern, penetrance, age, and phenotype definition matter.
- Phenocopies are particularly important in cancer genetics.
- Alternative genetic causes can create misleading apparent non-segregation.
- A single affected relative without the variant does not automatically prove benignity.
- Disease- and gene-specific rules must be followed.
- In gastric-cancer genetics, generic BS4 should not be applied without considering the specific hereditary gastric-cancer disorder.

# Governance Status

**Current ACMG/AMP Evidence Criterion — CONTEXT-DEPENDENT APPLICATION**

BS4 should not be interpreted as:

> "Any affected family member without the variant = Strong Benign Evidence."

Application depends on:

- phenotype validity;
- genotype validity;
- inheritance pattern;
- penetrance;
- age;
- possibility of phenocopy;
- alternative molecular explanations;
- and applicable disease/gene-specific ClinGen guidance.

For hereditary gastric-cancer interpretation, disease-specific governance takes precedence over generic application.

---

# Runtime Safety Rule

The Safe Medical AI System must not generate:

> "The variant is benign because an affected relative does not carry it."

Instead, it should communicate:

> **Lack of segregation in an appropriately characterized affected family member may provide benign evidence, but the observation must be interpreted in the context of the specific disease, inheritance pattern, penetrance, phenotype, and possible alternative causes.**
