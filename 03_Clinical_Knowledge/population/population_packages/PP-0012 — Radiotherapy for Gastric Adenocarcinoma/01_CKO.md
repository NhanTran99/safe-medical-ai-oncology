Knowledge Block 1 — What Is Radiotherapy?
Patient Explanation

Radiotherapy is a cancer treatment that uses carefully planned high-energy radiation to damage cancer cells.

The goal is to destroy cancer cells or stop them from growing while protecting as much healthy tissue as possible.

Unlike chemotherapy, which works throughout the body, radiotherapy mainly treats the specific area where the radiation is directed.

Clinical Importance

Radiotherapy is a local treatment that may be used as part of multidisciplinary care for selected patients with gastric adenocarcinoma.

Key Concepts
Radiotherapy uses high-energy radiation.
It is a local treatment.
Treatment is carefully planned before it begins.
Knowledge Block 2 — Why Is Radiotherapy Used?
Patient Explanation

Radiotherapy may be recommended for different reasons depending on the stage of the cancer and the overall treatment plan.

In selected patients, radiotherapy may:

help reduce the risk of cancer returning after treatment;
be combined with chemotherapy;
relieve symptoms caused by advanced cancer, such as pain or bleeding.

Not every patient with gastric cancer requires radiotherapy.

Clinical Importance

The role of radiotherapy depends on the individual's disease characteristics and multidisciplinary treatment strategy.

Key Concepts
Radiotherapy is used only in selected situations.
Treatment goals differ among patients.
Decisions are individualized.
Knowledge Block 3 — How Does Radiotherapy Work?
Patient Explanation

Radiation damages the genetic material inside cancer cells.

After enough damage occurs, cancer cells lose the ability to continue growing and dividing.

Healthy cells in the treated area may also be affected, but they usually recover better than cancer cells.

Clinical Importance

Radiotherapy works by damaging DNA within the treatment field, leading to cancer cell death or loss of reproductive capacity.

Key Concepts
Radiation damages cancer cells.
Healthy tissues are protected as much as possible.
Treatment is carefully planned to maximize benefit and reduce unnecessary exposure.
Knowledge Block 4 — When May Radiotherapy Be Used?
Patient Explanation

Radiotherapy is not used for every patient with gastric adenocarcinoma.

Depending on the clinical situation, it may be recommended:

Together With Chemotherapy

Some patients receive radiotherapy together with chemotherapy because the two treatments can work better when combined.

After Surgery

In selected situations, radiotherapy may be considered after surgery as part of the overall treatment plan.

Palliative Treatment

When cancer cannot be cured, radiotherapy may help control symptoms such as bleeding, pain or blockage, improving quality of life.

Clinical Importance

Radiotherapy has different roles depending on treatment intent, disease stage and multidisciplinary recommendations.

Key Concepts
Combined treatment
Selected postoperative use
Symptom relief
Knowledge Block 5 — What Happens During Radiotherapy?
Patient Explanation

Before treatment begins, the radiation team carefully plans the treatment.

Most patients receive treatment over several visits rather than all at once.

Each treatment session is usually short, and the radiation itself cannot be seen or felt.

The treatment team monitors patients throughout the course of therapy.

Clinical Importance

Modern radiotherapy uses careful planning and repeated treatments to maximize effectiveness while minimizing harm to surrounding tissues.

Key Concepts
Careful planning
Multiple treatment sessions
Continuous monitoring
Knowledge Block 6 — How Is Radiotherapy Different From Chemotherapy?
Patient Explanation

Although both treatments help fight cancer, they work differently.

Radiotherapy mainly treats a specific part of the body.

Chemotherapy travels through the bloodstream and can reach cancer cells throughout the body.

Some patients receive both treatments because they work in different ways.

Clinical Importance

Understanding the distinction between local and systemic therapy helps patients understand multidisciplinary treatment planning.

Key Concepts
Radiotherapy is local.
Chemotherapy is systemic.
Treatments may be combined.
Knowledge Block 7 — Benefits and Possible Side Effects
Expected Benefits

Radiotherapy may:

reduce the chance of cancer returning in selected patients;
improve local cancer control;
relieve symptoms caused by advanced cancer;
improve quality of life in appropriate situations.

Benefits vary among individuals.

Common Side Effects

Possible side effects may include:

fatigue;
nausea;
reduced appetite;
irritation of nearby normal tissues;
temporary digestive symptoms.

Many side effects improve after treatment finishes.

The healthcare team can often provide treatments to help manage these problems.

Common Misconception

Myth

Radiotherapy makes the body radioactive after treatment.

Fact

External beam radiotherapy does not make patients radioactive. It is safe to be around family members after treatment unless the healthcare team provides specific instructions.

Knowledge Block 8 — What Should Patients Expect?

Patients are regularly assessed before, during and after radiotherapy.

The treatment team monitors:

treatment progress;
side effects;
overall health;
recovery after treatment.

Patients should tell their healthcare team about new or worsening symptoms during treatment.

Clinical Importance

Regular assessment helps ensure treatment remains safe and effective.

Key Messages
Radiotherapy uses high-energy radiation to treat cancer.
It is a local treatment rather than a whole-body treatment.
Radiotherapy is recommended only for selected patients.
It may be combined with chemotherapy or surgery as part of multidisciplinary care.
Radiotherapy can also help relieve symptoms in advanced disease.
Side effects vary and are often manageable.
Patients should communicate openly with their healthcare team throughout treatment.
