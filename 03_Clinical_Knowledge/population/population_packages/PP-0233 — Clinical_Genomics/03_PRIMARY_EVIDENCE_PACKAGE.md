# Clinical Question

**Primary Educational Question**

> **What is Clinical Genomics and how is genomic information used in cancer care?**

---

# Educational Intent

Provide patients and caregivers with an accurate, evidence-based explanation of **clinical genomics** as the clinical framework for using genomic information in cancer care.

The package establishes the conceptual pathway:

**clinical question**
→ **appropriate genomic/molecular test**
→ **appropriate specimen**
→ **genomic finding**
→ **interpretation/classification**
→ **integration with pathology and clinical context**
→ **clinical decision-making**

The package intentionally stops before detailed sequencing methodology, variant interpretation/classification, genetic-testing workflows, disease-specific biomarker testing, and treatment algorithms.

# Evidence Synthesis

## 1. Clinical Genomics Is a Clinical Framework

The central evidence-supported concept is that genomic information becomes useful when connected to a clinical question.

ESMO/ASCO places genetic and genomic testing within the competencies required for modern oncology. These competencies include selecting an appropriate molecular test, understanding assay scope, recognizing different genomic alterations, interpreting molecular pathology reports, and contextualizing cases in a molecular tumor board.

Therefore, clinical genomics is broader than the technology used to generate the result.

The conceptual framework is:

**Patient/tumor context**
→ **clinical question**
→ **test selection**
→ **specimen**
→ **genomic finding**
→ **interpretation/classification**
→ **clinical integration**
→ **clinical decision**

PP-0233 introduces the whole framework while delegating detailed steps to specialized packages.

---

## 2. Why Test Selection Is Part of Clinical Genomics

ESMO/ASCO explicitly identifies selection of the appropriate molecular test according to patient needs and tumor histology as a clinical skill.

This supports an important educational principle:

> **The clinical question should determine the test.**

Testing is not selected simply because a technology is available.

A focused test may be appropriate for a focused question.

A broader panel or genome-wide approach may be useful when a broader question is clinically justified.

The package therefore discusses targeted versus broader testing only conceptually and does not teach the methodology of NGS, WGS, WES, or gene panels.

---

## 3. Specimen and Assay Requirements

ESMO/ASCO requires understanding whether an assay needs DNA, RNA, or protein and how this affects tissue requirements.

This is clinically important because the quality and adequacy of the specimen can influence whether testing can be performed and what can be detected.

Assays also differ in:

- alteration types detected;
- target scope;
- analytical sensitivity;
- analytical specificity;
- intended application;
- limitations.

Therefore, a genomic test should always be understood together with its analytical scope.

---

## 4. Types of Genomic Alterations

ESMO/ASCO identifies multiple genomic alteration categories, including:

- point mutations;
- rearrangements;
- copy-number alterations.

Clinical genomics introduces these categories because the type of alteration influences:

- assay selection;
- interpretation;
- potential clinical significance.

Detailed variant interpretation and classification are explicitly delegated.

---

## 5. Pathology and Clinical Integration

The evidence supports treating genomic findings as one component of a larger clinical assessment.

ESMO/ASCO specifically describes interpretation of molecular pathology reports in conjunction with pathologists and clinical geneticists when needed.

This reinforces the principle:

**Genomic result ≠ standalone diagnosis.**

A clinically meaningful interpretation may require integration of:

- tumor morphology;
- histology;
- immunophenotype;
- clinical history;
- staging;
- genomic findings;
- other laboratory or imaging information.

---

## 6. Germline and Somatic Genomic Information

Clinical genomics must distinguish two broad contexts.

### Somatic

Genetic changes acquired by tumor cells.

### Germline

Inherited genomic information that may be present throughout the body.

The distinction matters because the clinical implications are different.

ESMO/ASCO includes recognition of circumstances when germline testing and genetic counseling may be required.

NCI emphasizes that genomic testing and interpretation are complex and supports involvement of qualified professionals.

PP-0233 introduces this distinction only.

Detailed germline testing is delegated to **PP-0109**.

Detailed somatic testing is delegated to **PP-0110**.

---

## 7. Diagnostic and Classification Roles

Genomic information can contribute to understanding what a tumor is.

In some cancers, molecular findings can help distinguish disease categories or refine classification.

PP-0233 introduces this concept without providing disease-specific diagnostic algorithms.

Detailed molecular classification and variant interpretation are downstream.

---

## 8. Prognostic Role

Some genomic characteristics may provide information associated with expected disease behavior or outcomes.

However, a genomic finding should not be presented as a complete prediction of an individual patient's future.

Prognostic meaning depends on:

- the specific genomic feature;
- cancer type;
- evidence;
- disease stage;
- patient characteristics;
- other clinical factors.

---

## 9. Predictive and Treatment-Related Role

Genomic information can sometimes contribute to treatment selection or prediction of treatment response.

NCCN's gastric-cancer guidance places molecular/biomarker testing within the clinical management pathway and describes molecular testing approaches for selected biomarkers.

However, PP-0233 does not provide treatment algorithms.

The educational boundary is:

> **Genomic information may inform treatment decisions; it does not automatically dictate treatment.**

Drug-specific treatment recommendations belong to downstream treatment and biomarker packages.

---

## 10. Gastric-Cancer Clinical Context

NCCN Gastric Cancer Version 2.2026 places pathologic review and biomarker testing within the gastric-cancer clinical framework.

The guideline describes:

- IHC/ISH/targeted PCR as important initial approaches for several biomarkers;
- molecular testing using PCR or NGS for selected applications;
- broader NGS, including TMB assessment, in selected clinical circumstances when sufficient tumor tissue is available;
- ctDNA-based approaches in selected settings.

This supports PP-0233's conceptual point that clinical genomics is embedded in a broader clinical workflow rather than being a standalone laboratory activity.

PP-0233 intentionally does not reproduce biomarker-specific testing algorithms.

---

## 11. Molecular Pathology Reports

A molecular pathology report communicates findings generated by molecular testing.

The clinical meaning of the report depends on:

- what assay was performed;
- what specimen was tested;
- which findings were identified;
- what the assay can and cannot detect;
- the patient's pathology and clinical context.

ESMO/ASCO explicitly identifies discussion with pathologists and clinical geneticists as part of appropriate interpretation.

---

## 12. Molecular Tumor Boards

A molecular tumor board provides a multidisciplinary setting for discussing complex genomic cases.

The evidence supports its role in contextualizing genomic information within clinical care.

PP-0233 explains the concept rather than prescribing a universal composition or workflow.

Possible participants may include:

- oncologists;
- pathologists;
- molecular/genomic specialists;
- clinical geneticists;
- other relevant specialists.

---

## 13. Limitations and Uncertainty

Clinical genomics cannot answer every clinical question.

Limitations can arise from:

- assay scope;
- specimen quality;
- tissue quantity;
- alteration type;
- analytical sensitivity;
- analytical specificity;
- tumor heterogeneity;
- incomplete scientific knowledge;
- uncertain clinical significance.

Therefore, genomic results should be interpreted with awareness of the test's capabilities.

---

## 14. Meaning of a Negative Result

A negative result means that the relevant finding was not detected within the assay's scope and capabilities.

It does not necessarily establish that:

- no genomic alteration exists;
- no clinically important alteration exists;
- another assay would also be negative.

NCCN's gastric-cancer guidance illustrates this principle in the context of ctDNA by cautioning that a negative result does not exclude the presence of tumor.

PP-0233 generalizes the educational principle without turning it into a disease-specific testing rule.

---

## 15. Professional Interpretation

NCI emphasizes the complexity of genomic testing and interpretation and the importance of qualified healthcare professionals in ordering and interpreting genomic tests.

This supports PP-0233 's safety boundary:

> **A genomic result should not be interpreted in isolation by the patient.**

The patient-facing role of PP-0233 is to explain what genomic information is and why clinical context matters.

---

# Clinical Claims Summary

The evidence consistently supports the following educational messages:

- Clinical genomics is the clinical use of genomic information.
- It is broader than a laboratory sequencing procedure.
- Clinical questions should guide molecular-test selection.
- Tumor histology and clinical context can influence test choice.
- Specimen type and adequacy matter.
- Different assays detect different types of genomic information.
- Genomic findings can contribute to diagnosis/classification, prognosis, prediction, and other clinical questions.
- Genomic information should be integrated with pathology and clinical information.
- Germline and somatic genomic information are distinct.
- Molecular pathology reports may require multidisciplinary interpretation.
- Molecular tumor boards can help contextualize complex genomic cases.
- Assays have important scope and analytical limitations.
- A negative genomic result does not prove that no genomic abnormality exists.
- A genomic finding does not automatically identify the correct treatment.
- Qualified professionals play an important role in genomic testing and interpretation.

# Evidence Consistency Review

No clinically meaningful disagreement was identified within the approved evidence framework.

The sources are complementary:

### ESMO/ASCO Global Curriculum

Provides the broad oncology competency framework for genetic and genomic testing, including test selection, genomic alteration types, assay requirements, limitations, molecular pathology reports, germline recognition, and molecular tumor boards.

### NCCN Gastric Cancer Version 2.2026

Provides the gastric-cancer clinical context showing how biomarker testing and molecular testing are incorporated into clinical management.

### NCI Cancer Genetics Risk Assessment and Counseling

Provides the broader context of genomic-test complexity, qualified professional involvement, informed consent, and germline/genetic-testing considerations.

The package uses these sources at different levels and does not treat them as interchangeable.

# Clinical Safety Boundaries

## Safety Message 1

**A genomic result is not a standalone diagnosis.**

It should be interpreted with pathology and clinical context.

## Safety Message 2

**A genomic finding does not automatically identify the correct treatment.**

Clinical utility depends on evidence and patient context.

## Safety Message 3

**A negative genomic result does not prove that no genomic abnormality exists.**

The meaning of a negative result depends on assay scope and capability.

## Safety Message 4

**Tumor genomic information is not the same as inherited genomic information.**

Somatic and germline testing answer different questions.

## Safety Message 5

**Detailed genomic interpretation requires appropriate professional expertise.**

Patients should discuss genomic results with their healthcare team.
