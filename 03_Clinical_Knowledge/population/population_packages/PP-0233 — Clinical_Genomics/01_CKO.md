# Educational Objectives

After reading this Population Package, the reader should be able to:

- Understand what clinical genomics means in cancer care.
- Understand that clinical genomics is about using genomic information in a clinical context, not simply performing a genetic test.
- Understand the conceptual pathway from a clinical question to a genomic test, genomic finding, clinical interpretation, and clinical decision-making.
- Recognize why genomic information may contribute to cancer diagnosis, classification, prognosis, or treatment planning.
- Understand that the appropriate molecular/genomic test depends on the clinical question and tumor context.
- Recognize that different tests examine different types or scopes of biological information.
- Understand at a conceptual level why specimen type and tissue adequacy matter.
- Recognize the distinction between somatic/tumor genomic information and germline/inherited genomic information.
- Understand that genomic findings should be interpreted together with pathology and the broader clinical picture.
- Understand that variant interpretation and classification are downstream processes rather than the same thing as clinical genomics.
- Understand what a molecular pathology report is used for at a foundational level.
- Understand the conceptual role of a molecular tumor board.
- Recognize important limitations and uncertainty in genomic testing.
- Understand that a genomic finding does not automatically mean that a treatment will work.
- Understand that a negative genomic result does not necessarily mean that no genomic abnormality exists.
- Feel more confident discussing genomic testing and genomic results with the healthcare team.

# Knowledge Block 1 — What Is Clinical Genomics?

## Patient Explanation

**Clinical genomics** means using information about a person's genes and other genomic features as part of medical care.

In cancer care, this may involve examining genetic changes or other molecular features in a tumor or in a person's inherited genetic material.

The important point is that clinical genomics is not simply about performing a genomic test.

It is about connecting genomic information to a **clinical question** and to the rest of the patient's medical information.

A simple conceptual pathway is:

**Clinical question**
→ **choose an appropriate genomic or molecular test**
→ **obtain an appropriate specimen**
→ **generate genomic findings**
→ **interpret and classify the findings**
→ **integrate them with pathology and clinical context**
→ **inform clinical decisions**

The individual steps may be covered in greater detail by other Population Packages.

---

## Clinical Importance

A genomic result has meaning only when it is considered in the context in which it was obtained.

The same type of genomic alteration can have different clinical significance depending on:

- the gene involved;
- the type of alteration;
- the cancer type;
- tumor histology;
- the clinical situation;
- the assay used;
- the available evidence.

Clinical genomics therefore connects laboratory information with patient care.

# Knowledge Block 2 — Why Is Genomic Information Relevant to Cancer Care?

## Patient Explanation

Cancer develops through biological changes that can affect how cancer cells grow, survive, spread, or respond to treatment.

Genomic information can help healthcare professionals understand some of these biological features.

Depending on the clinical situation, genomic information may contribute to:

- cancer classification;
- diagnosis;
- prognosis;
- prediction of treatment response;
- identification of potentially relevant molecular characteristics;
- understanding of treatment resistance or tumor evolution.

The role of a genomic test is therefore determined by the clinical question.

---

## Clinical Importance

Genomic information is one source of clinical evidence.

It does not replace:

- medical history;
- physical examination;
- imaging;
- pathology;
- staging;
- laboratory information;
- patient preferences;
- other clinical factors.

A genomic finding becomes clinically useful when it is integrated with these other sources of information.

---

## Key Concepts

- Diagnosis
- Classification
- Prognosis
- Prediction
- Tumor biology
- Clinical integration

# Knowledge Block 3 — What Is the Clinical Genomics Workflow?

## Patient Explanation

Clinical genomics can be understood as a sequence of linked questions.

### Step 1 — What is the clinical question?

The healthcare team first considers what information is needed.

For example, the question may concern tumor classification, prognosis, a possible treatment-related biomarker, or another molecular feature.

### Step 2 — What test can answer that question?

The team chooses a test whose scope matches the clinical need.

### Step 3 — What specimen is needed?

Different tests may require different biological materials.

### Step 4 — What genomic findings are identified?

The test produces information about genetic or molecular features.

### Step 5 — What do the findings mean?

Interpretation and classification are performed using the relevant scientific and clinical evidence.

### Step 6 — How does the finding fit the patient?

The result is considered together with pathology, tumor type, clinical history, and other information.

### Step 7 — Does the information affect clinical care?

The genomic information may contribute to diagnosis, prognosis, treatment planning, or another clinical decision, depending on the evidence.

---

## Clinical Importance

The workflow shows why clinical genomics is broader than a laboratory test.

The test generates information.

Clinical genomics is the framework that connects that information to patient care.

---

# Knowledge Block 4 — How Is the Right Genomic Test Chosen?

## Patient Explanation

There is no single genomic test that is appropriate for every patient.

The choice depends on the clinical question and the characteristics of the cancer.

Relevant considerations may include:

- tumor type and histology;
- suspected molecular feature;
- whether a focused or broader question is being asked;
- available tissue or other specimen;
- the type of information the assay can detect;
- the clinical purpose of testing.

At a conceptual level, testing approaches may range from a focused assay to a broader multigene or genome-wide approach.

The detailed technologies are covered by separate Population Packages.

---

## Clinical Importance

ESMO/ASCO identifies selection of the appropriate molecular test according to patient needs and tumor histology as a core oncology competency.

The practical principle is:

> **Choose the test to answer the clinical question.**

A broader test is not automatically a better test.

A focused test may be appropriate when the clinical question is narrow.

A broader test may be useful when multiple possible genomic features need to be considered.

---

## Key Concepts

- Test selection
- Clinical question
- Tumor histology
- Test scope
- Focused assay
- Broader genomic assay

# Knowledge Block 5 — Why Do Specimen and Assay Characteristics Matter?

## Patient Explanation

A genomic test can only examine what its assay is designed and able to detect.

The required specimen depends on the test.

For example, an assay may require:

- DNA;
- RNA;
- protein;
- tumor tissue;
- another appropriate biological specimen.

The amount and quality of available material can also affect whether testing is possible and how confidently the result can be interpreted.

---

## Clinical Importance

ESMO/ASCO emphasizes understanding whether an assay requires DNA, RNA, or protein and how this affects tissue requirements.

Assays also differ in:

- the types of alterations they can detect;
- the number of targets they examine;
- their analytical performance;
- their sensitivity;
- their limitations.

Therefore:

> **A test result is always limited by what the test was designed and able to measure.**

---

## Key Concepts

- Specimen
- DNA
- RNA
- Protein
- Tissue adequacy
- Assay scope
- Analytical performance

# Knowledge Block 6 — What Types of Genomic Findings Can Be Relevant?

## Patient Explanation

Genomic testing can identify different kinds of changes.

At a conceptual level, these may include:

- small DNA sequence changes;
- changes involving larger sections of DNA;
- copy-number changes;
- gene rearrangements or fusions;
- other molecular alterations.

Different assays are better suited to detecting different types of changes.

---

## Clinical Importance

The type of alteration matters because its clinical meaning depends on:

- which gene is affected;
- what kind of alteration occurred;
- the cancer in which it is found;
- the available evidence.

PP-0233 introduces these concepts but does not teach detailed variant interpretation or classification.

Those are downstream knowledge layers.

---

## Key Concepts

- Genomic alteration
- Point mutation
- Copy-number alteration
- Rearrangement
- Gene fusion
- Variant

# Knowledge Block 7 — How Do Genomic Findings Fit With Pathology?

## Patient Explanation

A genomic result should not usually be viewed as a diagnosis by itself.

Cancer diagnosis and classification may also depend on:

- tumor morphology;
- histology;
- immunophenotype;
- pathology findings;
- clinical history;
- imaging and staging.

Genomic information adds another layer of information.

A simplified model is:

**Clinical history + pathology + imaging/staging + genomic information**
→ **integrated clinical understanding**

---

## Clinical Importance

ESMO/ASCO describes interpretation of molecular pathology reports together with active discussion with pathologists and clinical geneticists when needed.

This reflects an important principle:

> **Genomic information should be interpreted in context rather than in isolation.**

A genomic alteration may be important, but its significance depends on the tumor and the clinical situation.

---

## Key Concepts

- Pathology
- Histology
- Immunophenotype
- Molecular pathology report
- Integrated interpretation

# Knowledge Block 8 — Germline vs Somatic Genomic Information

## Patient Explanation

Genomic information can come from different biological contexts.

### Somatic / tumor genomic information

This refers to genetic changes acquired by tumor cells.

These changes are part of the biology of the cancer.

### Germline / inherited genomic information

This refers to genetic information that is inherited or otherwise present throughout the body.

Some germline variants can increase susceptibility to certain cancers.

These two concepts answer different clinical questions.

---

## Clinical Importance

A tumor genomic finding should not automatically be assumed to be inherited.

Similarly, germline testing is not simply another name for tumor sequencing.

Clinical genomics should recognize when a finding or clinical history raises the possibility that germline evaluation may be appropriate.

Detailed germline-testing indications, counseling, and family-testing workflows belong to dedicated packages.

---

## Key Concepts

- Somatic
- Tumor genomic information
- Germline
- Inherited genomic information
- Hereditary cancer
- Confirmatory testing

# Knowledge Block 9 — What Can Genomic Information Tell Us About Diagnosis, Prognosis, or Treatment?

## Patient Explanation

Depending on the cancer and the clinical question, genomic information may contribute to:

### Diagnosis or classification

Certain molecular findings can help characterize a tumor or distinguish among disease categories.

### Prognosis

Some genomic characteristics may be associated with how a cancer behaves or with expected outcomes.

### Treatment prediction

Some genomic findings may help identify whether a particular treatment strategy could be relevant.

### Resistance and tumor evolution

Genomic information may also help understand changes in tumor biology over time, including mechanisms associated with treatment resistance.

---

## Clinical Importance

These roles are conceptual.

A genomic finding does **not** automatically mean:

> “This is the treatment that will work.”

Clinical utility depends on the strength of evidence, the cancer context, the assay, and the complete clinical picture.

Treatment algorithms and biomarker-specific treatment decisions belong to downstream packages.

---

## Key Concepts

- Diagnostic role
- Classification
- Prognostic role
- Predictive role
- Resistance
- Clinical utility

# Knowledge Block 10 — What Is a Molecular Pathology Report?

## Patient Explanation

A molecular pathology report summarizes findings generated by a molecular or genomic test.

Depending on the test, it may describe:

- what was tested;
- what genomic findings were identified;
- relevant assay information;
- limitations;
- other information needed for clinical interpretation.

The report should be considered together with the patient's pathology and clinical information.

---

## Clinical Importance

ESMO/ASCO identifies the ability to interpret molecular pathology reports together with pathologists and clinical geneticists when needed as an important clinical competency.

The report is therefore not simply a list of mutations.

Its information must be understood within the clinical context.

---

# Knowledge Block 11 — What Is a Molecular Tumor Board?

## Patient Explanation

A **molecular tumor board** is a multidisciplinary group that can review complex cases involving genomic or molecular information.

Depending on the clinical setting, participants may include:

- oncologists;
- pathologists;
- molecular or genomic specialists;
- clinical geneticists;
- other relevant healthcare professionals.

The group considers the genomic findings together with the patient's disease and clinical context.

---

## Clinical Importance

ESMO/ASCO specifically includes the ability to report and contextualize a clinical case within a molecular tumor board.

The purpose is not simply to identify a mutation.

It is to help integrate complex molecular information with clinical and pathology information.

PP-0233 explains the concept only.

It does not define a universal molecular tumor-board workflow.

---

## Key Concepts

- Molecular tumor board

# Knowledge Block 12 — What Are the Limitations of Clinical Genomics?

## Patient Explanation

Genomic testing is powerful, but it does not provide complete information about every aspect of a cancer.

Important limitations include:

- the assay may examine only selected genes or regions;
- some alteration types may not be detectable by a particular assay;
- the specimen may not contain enough suitable material;
- tumor heterogeneity can affect what is detected;
- analytical sensitivity and specificity vary between assays;
- scientific knowledge about some genomic findings remains incomplete;
- a finding may have uncertain clinical significance.

---

## Clinical Importance

ESMO/ASCO requires understanding assay scope and limitations, including differences between focused assays, targeted panels, and genome-wide approaches.

A negative result should therefore be interpreted in relation to:

- what was tested;
- which specimen was used;
- which alteration types the assay could detect;
- the clinical question.

---

## Key Concepts

- Test limitation
- Assay scope
- Sensitivity
- Specificity
- Tissue adequacy
- Tumor heterogeneity

# Knowledge Block 13 — What Does a Negative Genomic Result Mean?

## Patient Explanation

A negative genomic result means that the test did not identify the relevant finding within the scope and capabilities of that assay.

It does not necessarily mean that:

- the cancer has no genetic changes;
- no clinically relevant alteration exists;
- another assay would also be negative.

A result must be interpreted according to what the test was designed to detect.

---

## Clinical Importance

This is especially important when the test has limited scope or when the specimen is limited.

A negative result is therefore not the same as proof that no genomic abnormality exists.

---

## Key Concepts

- Negative result
- Test scope
- Detection limits
- Residual uncertainty

# Knowledge Block 14 — What Can a Genomic Result Not Tell Me?

## Patient Explanation

A genomic result does not necessarily tell you:

- exactly how your cancer will behave;
- exactly which treatment will work;
- whether a cancer will definitely return;
- whether a genetic finding is inherited;
- whether a single genomic finding explains every feature of the cancer.

These questions often require additional information.

---

## Clinical Importance

Genomic information is one component of clinical care.

Its usefulness depends on:

**genomic finding + evidence + tumor context + clinical context + other medical information**

This is why genomic testing should be interpreted by appropriately qualified professionals and integrated into clinical decision-making.

---

- Clinical utility
- Context
- Evidence
- Uncertainty

# Common Misconceptions

## Myth 1

**“Clinical genomics means everyone needs genome sequencing.”**

### Fact

Clinical genomics is the use of genomic information in clinical care. The appropriate test depends on the clinical question, patient needs, tumor context, and test scope.

---

## Myth 2

**“A genomic test replaces pathology.”**

### Fact

Genomic information complements pathology and other clinical information. It does not automatically replace histology, immunophenotype, imaging, or clinical assessment.

---

## Myth 3

**“A genomic finding automatically tells the doctor which treatment will work.”**

### Fact

A genomic finding may provide clinically useful information, but treatment relevance depends on the evidence and the patient's complete clinical context.

---

## Myth 4

**“A negative genomic test means the cancer has no genetic abnormalities.”**

### Fact

A negative result means that the relevant finding was not detected within the scope and capabilities of the assay used.

---

## Myth 5

**“Tumor genomic testing and inherited genetic testing are the same.”**

### Fact

Tumor testing primarily evaluates acquired tumor changes, whereas germline testing evaluates inherited genomic information.

---

## Myth 6

**“If a tumor test finds a variant, it is automatically a germline variant.”**

### Fact

A tumor finding may require additional evaluation before it can be considered an inherited finding.

---

## Myth 7

**“A larger genomic test is always better.”**

### Fact

The best test is the one that appropriately addresses the clinical question. Broader testing is not automatically more useful in every situation.

---

# Key Messages

- Clinical genomics means using genomic information as part of patient care.
- It is broader than simply performing a genomic test.
- The clinical question should guide test selection.
- Tumor type and histology can influence which test is appropriate.
- Specimen type, tissue adequacy, and assay scope matter.
- Genomic findings may contribute to diagnosis, classification, prognosis, prediction, or understanding resistance, depending on the context.
- Genomic information should be integrated with pathology and other clinical information.
- Somatic and germline genomic information answer different clinical questions.
- Variant interpretation and classification are downstream steps, not synonyms for clinical genomics.
- Molecular pathology reports may require multidisciplinary discussion.
- Molecular tumor boards can help contextualize complex genomic cases.
- A negative result does not prove that no genomic abnormality exists.
- A genomic finding does not automatically identify the correct treatment.
- Clinical genomics involves uncertainty and should be interpreted by appropriately qualified professionals.
