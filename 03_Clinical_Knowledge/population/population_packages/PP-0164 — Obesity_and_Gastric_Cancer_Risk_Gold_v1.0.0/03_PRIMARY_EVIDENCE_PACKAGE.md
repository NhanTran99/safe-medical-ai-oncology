## Clinical Question

> How does excess body weight affect gastric-cancer risk, which gastric-cancer patterns are relevant, and what does maintaining a healthy weight mean for gastric-cancer prevention?

## Educational Intent

Provide a source-grounded, patient-facing explanation of excess body weight as a gastric-cancer risk factor while preserving the uncertainty and limitations of the supplied evidence.

## Scope

### Included
- Excess body weight / overweight / obesity.
- Gastric-cancer risk association.
- Some-type/subtype limitation.
- GERD-associated tissue damage as contextual evidence.
- Healthy-weight maintenance.
- Association versus causation.
- Individual-risk limitations.

## Primary Evidence Sources

### NCI — Genetics of Gastric Cancer (PDQ®)

NCI states that:

> “Emerging evidence suggests an association between increased gastric cancer risk, excess body weight, and tissue damage associated with GERD.”

NCI also places obesity within the environmental-risk context and describes analyses in which gastric-cancer risk associations were adjusted for environmental factors including obesity.

This source supports the cautious evidence classification used in PP-0164.

### ACS — Stomach Cancer

ACS states that:

> “Having excess body weight (overweight or obesity) increases the risk of some types of stomach cancer, so getting to and staying at a healthy weight might lower your risk.”

This is the clearest patient-facing source supporting both:
1. the risk association; and
2. the prevention implication.

### NCCN Gastric Cancer

NCCN provides the broader gastric-cancer risk framework and distinguishes environmental and anatomic/histologic patterns. The source set does not provide sufficient obesity-specific quantitative detail to create a gastric-cancer-specific BMI model.

## Evidence Notes

### 1. Excess Body Weight

The central evidence supports an association between excess body weight and gastric-cancer risk.

### 2. “Some Types” Matters

ACS does not state that excess body weight increases the risk of every gastric-cancer subtype. The package therefore preserves this wording rather than generalizing across all gastric cancers.

### 3. GERD Context

NCI describes emerging evidence involving excess body weight, gastric-cancer risk, and tissue damage associated with GERD. This is contextual and should not be transformed into a GERD-specific risk model.

### 4. Healthy Weight

ACS states that getting to and staying at a healthy weight might lower stomach-cancer risk.

This is a reasonable prevention direction but not evidence of a quantified cancer-prevention intervention.

### 5. Individualized Risk

The supplied materials do not provide:
- a validated BMI-based absolute-risk model;
- a gastric-cancer-specific BMI threshold;
- a dose-response equation;
- a quantified risk reduction from intentional weight loss.

Therefore none should be invented.

## Clinical Claims Summary

### Established / Supported
- Excess body weight is associated with increased risk of some types of stomach cancer.
- Maintaining a healthy weight might lower stomach-cancer risk.

### Emerging / Context-dependent
- The relationship involving excess body weight, gastric-cancer risk, and GERD-associated tissue damage.
- Exact subtype-specific risk patterns.
- Exact magnitude of risk by BMI.

### Not Established
- Obesity as a universal direct cause of gastric cancer.
- A specific BMI threshold for gastric-cancer risk.
- A specific kilogram weight-loss target that prevents gastric cancer.
- Bariatric surgery as proven gastric-cancer prevention.
- Anti-obesity medication as proven gastric-cancer prevention.
- Individual absolute gastric-cancer risk based on BMI alone.

## Evidence Consistency Review

The supplied sources are directionally consistent:

- ACS gives the clearest patient-facing statement.
- NCI uses more cautious “emerging evidence” wording.
- NCCN provides the broader risk framework.

No material contradiction requiring reconciliation was identified.

## Evidence Gaps

1. No gastric-cancer-specific absolute-risk model by BMI.
2. No validated gastric-cancer-specific BMI threshold.
3. No quantified benefit of a defined amount of weight loss.
4. Limited obesity-specific subtype detail in the supplied materials.
5. No source-supported conclusion that anti-obesity medication or bariatric surgery prevents gastric cancer.
