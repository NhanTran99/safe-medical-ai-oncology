# 1. Educational Objectives

After reading this Population Package, the reader should be able to:

1. Explain what an immune checkpoint is and why cancer can exploit checkpoint signaling.
2. Explain, at a conceptual level, how PD-1/PD-L1 blockade can restore anti-tumor immune activity.
3. Distinguish immune checkpoint inhibitors from immunotherapy as the broader treatment category.
4. Recognize the major checkpoint-inhibitor classes relevant to gastric cancer: PD-1, PD-L1, and CTLA-4 inhibition.
5. Recognize the major checkpoint inhibitors represented in the supplied gastric-cancer evidence: nivolumab, pembrolizumab, tislelizumab, durvalumab, dostarlimab, and the selected combination role of ipilimumab.
6. Understand why checkpoint inhibitors may be used alone or combined with chemotherapy or targeted therapy.
7. Understand why treatment setting and tumor biomarkers can materially change the role of a checkpoint inhibitor.
8. Understand the clinical bridge between checkpoint inhibition and PD-L1 or MSI-H/dMMR results without confusing treatment context with testing methodology.
9. Recognize the major evidence contexts represented by CheckMate-649, KEYNOTE-859, RATIONALE-305, KEYNOTE-811, MATTERHORN, and selected MSI-H/dMMR studies.
10. Understand that checkpoint inhibitors can produce durable benefit in selected patients but do not work for everyone.
11. Recognize that immune checkpoint inhibitors have a characteristic toxicity profile that differs from conventional chemotherapy.
12. Recognize common and potentially serious immune-related adverse events and understand why new symptoms should be reported promptly.
13. Understand that response assessment after checkpoint inhibition can sometimes be more complex than a simple early increase/decrease in tumor size.
14. Understand the concepts of primary and acquired resistance without interpreting them as individualized treatment predictions.
15. Understand why no single checkpoint inhibitor can be considered universally “best” for every person with gastric cancer.

# 3. Clinical Knowledge Blocks

## Block 1 — What is an immune checkpoint?

The immune system needs mechanisms that prevent immune cells from attacking healthy tissues unnecessarily. Immune checkpoints are regulatory pathways that act as brakes on immune activation.

Cancer can exploit these brakes. A tumor may use checkpoint signaling to reduce the ability of immune cells to recognize and attack malignant cells.

An immune checkpoint inhibitor is a medicine designed to block a checkpoint pathway so that the inhibitory signal is reduced and anti-tumor immune activity can be restored or strengthened.

**Patient message:** A checkpoint inhibitor does not directly “poison” the cancer cell in the same way as many chemotherapy drugs. It changes an immune-regulatory interaction so that the patient's immune system can attack cancer more effectively.

---

## Block 2 — Why is checkpoint signaling important?

Immune activation is useful against abnormal cells, but uncontrolled immune activation can damage normal tissues.

Checkpoint pathways therefore have a normal physiologic role.

The clinical problem in cancer is that the same regulatory system can become part of tumor immune escape.

This creates a therapeutic opportunity:

**normal checkpoint function**
→ prevents excessive immune attack

but

**tumor exploitation of checkpoint signaling**
→ weakens anti-tumor immunity

and

**checkpoint inhibition**
→ can release part of that inhibitory effect.

The balance is clinically important because the benefit of stronger immune activity can coexist with immune-mediated toxicity.

---

## Block 3 — PD-1 and PD-L1: the core checkpoint pathway

PD-1 is a checkpoint receptor expressed on immune cells including T cells.

PD-L1 is one of its ligands.

When PD-1 binds PD-L1, signaling can reduce T-cell activity. Some cancer cells can express PD-L1 and use this interaction as part of immune evasion.

Blocking PD-1 or PD-L1 can interfere with that inhibitory interaction.

The conceptual sequence is:

**T cell**
→ **PD-1**

**tumor / surrounding cells**
→ **PD-L1**

**PD-1 ↔ PD-L1 interaction**
→ inhibitory signaling

**PD-1 or PD-L1 inhibitor**
→ blocks the interaction

**result**
→ immune activity against cancer may increase.

This mechanism explains why PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors are a major checkpoint-inhibitor class in gastric cancer.

---

## Block 4 — CTLA-4

CTLA-4 is another immune checkpoint.

It regulates T-cell activation through a pathway that differs from PD-1/PD-L1.

CTLA-4 inhibition can therefore produce a different pattern of immune activation from PD-1 blockade.

In gastric cancer, the most relevant project-supported use is the combination of nivolumab, a PD-1 inhibitor, with ipilimumab, a CTLA-4 inhibitor, in selected MSI-H/dMMR settings.

The package does not treat CTLA-4 inhibition as a universally applicable standalone gastric-cancer treatment.

**Patient message:** Different checkpoint inhibitors target different immune brakes. Combining two checkpoints can change the intensity and pattern of immune activation, but it also changes the evidence and toxicity context.

---

## Block 5 — Immune checkpoint inhibitors are a subset of immunotherapy

Immunotherapy is the broader category.

Immune checkpoint inhibition is one major immunotherapy strategy.

This distinction matters because other immunotherapy approaches exist, including cellular therapies and other immune-directed strategies.

PP-0214 owns checkpoint inhibition.

It does not own the entire immunotherapy field.

The hierarchy is:

**Immunotherapy**
→ **Immune checkpoint inhibition**
→ **specific checkpoint / specific drug / specific treatment context**

---

## Block 6 — Major checkpoint-inhibitor classes in gastric cancer

### PD-1 inhibitors

Major project-supported examples include:

- nivolumab;
- pembrolizumab;
- tislelizumab;
- dostarlimab.

### PD-L1 inhibitor

- durvalumab.

### CTLA-4 inhibitor

- ipilimumab, primarily represented in combination with nivolumab for selected MSI-H/dMMR contexts.

The supplied ACS material identifies durvalumab, nivolumab, pembrolizumab, tislelizumab and dostarlimab as PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors used in different gastric-cancer contexts. NCI's approved-drug material independently lists nivolumab, pembrolizumab and tislelizumab among drugs approved for stomach cancer.

---

## Block 7 — Nivolumab

Nivolumab is a PD-1 inhibitor.

In advanced gastric/EGJ adenocarcinoma, the project evidence supports nivolumab in combination with chemotherapy for selected HER2-negative patients according to PD-L1 and treatment-setting criteria.

The major evidence base includes CheckMate-649.

The same clinical-development program also evaluated nivolumab plus ipilimumab.

An important evidence nuance is that nivolumab plus ipilimumab did not demonstrate a broad survival advantage over chemotherapy in the unselected populations reported from CheckMate-649, whereas MSI-H tumors showed a substantially more favorable signal.

Therefore the package must not generalize the MSI-H result to all gastric cancers.

---

## Block 8 — Pembrolizumab

Pembrolizumab is a PD-1 inhibitor.

The project evidence supports several distinct gastric-cancer contexts, including:

- combination with chemotherapy in selected HER2-negative advanced disease;
- combination with trastuzumab plus chemotherapy in selected HER2-positive advanced disease;
- monotherapy or combination treatment in selected MSI-H/dMMR disease;
- selected TMB-H tumor-agnostic context with important evidence limitations.

These contexts should not be collapsed into one universal indication.

KEYNOTE-859 supports pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy in treatment-naïve advanced HER2-negative disease.

KEYNOTE-811 supports adding pembrolizumab to trastuzumab plus chemotherapy in selected HER2-positive disease.

---

## Block 9 — Tislelizumab

Tislelizumab is a PD-1 inhibitor.

The project evidence supports its combination with fluoropyrimidine- and platinum-based chemotherapy for selected unresectable or metastatic HER2-negative gastric/EGJ adenocarcinoma with PD-L1 expression.

RATIONALE-305 provides the major trial evidence represented in the project source set.

The trial illustrates an important principle:

**the biomarker framework and scoring system used in one ICI trial may differ from another.**

Therefore PD-L1 TAP should not automatically be treated as identical to CPS.

Detailed PD-L1 scoring is delegated to the PD-L1 testing and PD-L1-guided therapy packages.

---

## Block 10 — Durvalumab

Durvalumab is a PD-L1 inhibitor.

The project evidence includes durvalumab in a perioperative treatment context for resectable gastric/EGJ cancer, particularly as part of a regimen incorporating FLOT.

MATTERHORN provides the major evidence context.

This demonstrates that checkpoint inhibition is not restricted to metastatic treatment.

The package should explain the concept of perioperative integration but should not reproduce detailed FLOT management.

---

## Block 11 — Dostarlimab

Dostarlimab is a PD-1 inhibitor.

The supplied clinical sources support its use in selected MSI-H/dMMR contexts.

Its role is therefore closely connected to biomarker-defined immunotherapy.

The package should teach:

**MSI-H/dMMR result**
→ may identify a treatment context in which checkpoint inhibition is particularly relevant.

The detailed biology and testing of MSI/MMR belong to PP-0215 and PP-0182.

---

## Block 12 — Ipilimumab

Ipilimumab is a CTLA-4 inhibitor.

Within the gastric-cancer source set, its most important role is in combination with nivolumab for selected MSI-H/dMMR disease.

The evidence is context-dependent.

The package should not present ipilimumab as a universal first-line gastric-cancer monotherapy.

# 4. Clinical Importance

## 4.1 Why checkpoint inhibitors matter in gastric cancer

Checkpoint inhibition has changed the treatment landscape for selected gastric/EGJ adenocarcinomas.

Its importance comes from several properties:

- it can produce meaningful tumor responses in selected patients;
- some responses can be durable;
- it can be combined with chemotherapy;
- it can be combined with targeted therapy;
- it can be incorporated into perioperative treatment;
- biomarker-defined populations can have particularly strong benefit;
- the toxicity profile is distinct from conventional cytotoxic chemotherapy.

At the same time, benefit is not universal.

---

## 4.2 Why the same drug may have different roles

A checkpoint inhibitor's role depends on:

- disease setting;
- HER2 status;
- PD-L1 status;
- MSI-H/dMMR status;
- previous checkpoint-inhibitor exposure;
- prior treatment;
- performance status;
- comorbidities;
- treatment goals;
- toxicity considerations.

Therefore:

> “This is an immunotherapy drug” is not enough information to determine how it should be used.

---

# 5. Major Combination Paradigms

## 5.1 Checkpoint inhibitor + chemotherapy

This is a central advanced-disease paradigm.

Examples in the project evidence include:

- nivolumab + fluoropyrimidine/oxaliplatin;
- pembrolizumab + fluoropyrimidine/oxaliplatin;
- tislelizumab + fluoropyrimidine/platinum chemotherapy.

The conceptual rationale is that chemotherapy and immune checkpoint blockade can act through different mechanisms.

The package does not attempt to determine the optimal chemotherapy regimen for an individual patient.

---

## 5.2 Checkpoint inhibitor + targeted therapy + chemotherapy

KEYNOTE-811 represents a particularly important example:

**pembrolizumab**
+
**trastuzumab**
+
**chemotherapy**

in selected HER2-positive advanced gastric/EGJ adenocarcinoma.

The key educational message is that immunotherapy can be layered onto a biomarker-directed targeted-treatment strategy.

Detailed HER2 treatment belongs to PP-0210.

---

## 5.3 Dual checkpoint inhibition

The project evidence includes:

**nivolumab + ipilimumab**

This targets:

- PD-1;
- CTLA-4.

The important evidence boundary is that dual-checkpoint therapy is not universally superior to chemotherapy in gastric cancer.

Its strongest project-supported role is in selected MSI-H/dMMR disease.

---

## 5.4 Perioperative checkpoint inhibition

The project source set includes:

- durvalumab + perioperative chemotherapy in MATTERHORN;
- selected MSI-H/dMMR neoadjuvant/perioperative checkpoint strategies.

This broadens the concept of immunotherapy beyond metastatic disease.

The package does not own the detailed surgical or perioperative chemotherapy algorithm.

# 6. Biomarker Context

## 6.1 PD-L1

PD-L1 is relevant to several checkpoint-inhibitor treatment pathways.

In current NCCN v2.2026, selected first-line HER2-negative advanced-disease regimens use PD-L1 CPS thresholds, including nivolumab, pembrolizumab and tislelizumab combinations.

Selected HER2-positive pembrolizumab + trastuzumab + chemotherapy treatment also incorporates PD-L1 CPS.

The package should explain the clinical principle:

> A biomarker result can influence the treatment context in which a checkpoint inhibitor is considered.

It should not teach:

- how CPS is calculated;
- how an IHC assay is performed;
- how to interpret every threshold;
- companion-diagnostic laboratory requirements.

Those are owned elsewhere.

---

## 6.2 MSI-H/dMMR

MSI-H/dMMR is one of the most important biomarker-defined checkpoint-inhibitor contexts.

Current NCCN v2.2026 supports checkpoint-inhibitor options in MSI-H/dMMR tumors independent of PD-L1 status in selected settings.

The project evidence also reports particularly strong benefit signals for MSI-H tumors in CheckMate-649.

This package therefore uses MSI-H/dMMR as a treatment-context bridge.

The detailed MSI/MMR testing and biology remain delegated.

---

## 6.3 TMB-H

TMB-H can support a tumor-agnostic pembrolizumab treatment context.

However, the project NCCN source explicitly notes a major evidence limitation: no gastroesophageal cancer patients were included in the TMB analysis from KEYNOTE-158 that underpinned the relevant evidence.

Therefore TMB-H must not be presented as equivalent to the stronger gastric-cancer-specific PD-L1 or MSI-H/dMMR evidence.

---

# 7. Landmark Evidence

## 7.1 CheckMate-649

CheckMate-649 is a major first-line trial of nivolumab plus chemotherapy in advanced HER2-negative gastric/EGJ/esophageal adenocarcinoma.

The project NCCN evidence reports, among other results, a 3-year OS of 14.4 versus 11.1 months in the PD-L1 CPS ≥5 population, with HR 0.70, and a PFS benefit.

The same evidence reports a particularly strong MSI-H subgroup signal.

**Interpretation:** nivolumab plus chemotherapy has become an important first-line checkpoint-inhibitor strategy in appropriately selected HER2-negative advanced disease.

**Limitation:** trial results apply to the studied population and treatment context; they do not mean every patient with gastric cancer should receive nivolumab.

---

## 7.2 KEYNOTE-859

KEYNOTE-859 evaluated pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy in treatment-naïve advanced HER2-negative gastric/EGJ adenocarcinoma.

The project evidence reports benefit in PD-L1 CPS ≥10 and CPS ≥1 populations.

The higher PD-L1 population had a larger observed treatment effect.

**Interpretation:** pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy is supported in selected advanced HER2-negative disease and demonstrates the importance of biomarker-defined treatment context.

---

## 7.3 RATIONALE-305

RATIONALE-305 evaluated tislelizumab plus chemotherapy.

The project evidence reports median OS of 15.0 versus 12.9 months in all randomized patients and a stronger effect in the PD-L1 TAP ≥5% subgroup.

**Interpretation:** tislelizumab adds another evidence-supported PD-1 inhibitor strategy, while also demonstrating that PD-L1 scoring systems can differ between trials.

---

## 7.4 KEYNOTE-811

KEYNOTE-811 evaluated pembrolizumab with trastuzumab and chemotherapy in HER2-positive advanced gastric/EGJ adenocarcinoma.

The project source reports improvements in PFS and response outcomes, particularly in the PD-L1 CPS ≥1 population.

**Interpretation:** checkpoint blockade can complement targeted HER2 therapy.

**Boundary:** detailed HER2 treatment remains PP-0210.

---

## 7.5 MATTERHORN

MATTERHORN provides the important perioperative checkpoint-inhibitor context for durvalumab with FLOT.

Its significance is architectural as well as clinical:

> checkpoint inhibition can be integrated into treatment around surgery, not only used for metastatic disease.

The package should preserve trial-specific population and endpoint context rather than implying that perioperative ICI is universally appropriate.

---

## 7.6 MSI-H/dMMR evidence

The project NCCN evidence reports strong activity of checkpoint strategies in MSI-H/dMMR disease.

Examples include:

- nivolumab + chemotherapy;
- nivolumab + ipilimumab;
- pembrolizumab;
- dostarlimab;
- selected perioperative strategies.

The key principle is:

> MSI-H/dMMR is a biologically distinct treatment context in which checkpoint inhibition can have particular relevance.

---

# 8. Benefits

Potential benefits of checkpoint inhibition include:

- tumor shrinkage in responsive disease;
- disease control;
- prolonged survival in appropriate populations;
- durable responses in some patients;
- potential benefit in biomarker-defined tumors;
- treatment options when conventional treatment choices are limited.

However:

> response is not guaranteed.

A biomarker can increase the likelihood of benefit in a population without guaranteeing benefit for an individual patient.

---

# 9. Limitations

Checkpoint inhibitors have important limitations:

1. Many patients do not respond.
2. Some responses may be temporary.
3. Resistance can develop.
4. Immune-related adverse events can be serious.
5. Biomarker testing can be imperfect or context-dependent.
6. Trial results may not apply to every patient.
7. Different agents have different evidence bases.
8. Combination therapy can increase treatment complexity.
9. A favorable biomarker does not guarantee response.
10. Apparent radiologic progression can occasionally be difficult to interpret.

---

# 10. Response and Resistance

## 10.1 Durable response

Unlike many cytotoxic treatments, checkpoint inhibition can produce durable responses in selected patients.

This does not mean that all responses are durable.

The correct patient-facing formulation is:

> Some people can have long-lasting benefit, but clinicians cannot assume that this will happen for every patient.

---

## 10.2 Primary resistance

Primary resistance means the tumor does not meaningfully respond from the beginning.

Possible biological contributors include:

- insufficient tumor immunogenicity;
- immune-excluded or immune-suppressed tumor microenvironment;
- ineffective immune-cell recruitment;
- alternative immune-suppressive pathways;
- inadequate target expression or treatment context.

These mechanisms are presented conceptually only.

---

## 10.3 Acquired resistance

Acquired resistance occurs when a tumor initially benefits but later progresses.

Tumor evolution and changes in immune interaction can contribute.

The package does not provide a detailed resistance-treatment algorithm.

---

## 10.4 Pseudoprogression

Some immunotherapies can produce an unusual response pattern in which lesions appear worse before subsequent improvement.

This is called pseudoprogression.

It is not the same as true progression.

However, apparent progression should not automatically be dismissed as pseudoprogression.

Clinical status, imaging, timing and the full treatment context must be considered.

Formal response criteria belong to PP-0217/PP-0218.

---

## 10.5 Mixed response

Different tumor sites can behave differently.

One lesion may shrink while another remains stable or grows.

This creates a mixed response.

Mixed response requires clinical interpretation and should not automatically be classified as either success or failure from one measurement alone.

---

## 10.6 Hyperprogression

Hyperprogression refers to an unusually rapid pattern of disease worsening temporally associated with immune checkpoint treatment.

The phenomenon is clinically recognized but remains difficult to define uniformly.

PP-0214 should recognize the concept without teaching a diagnostic algorithm.

---

# 11. Safety

## 11.1 Why checkpoint inhibitors cause distinctive toxicity

Checkpoint inhibitors reduce immune inhibitory signaling.

That can increase anti-tumor immune activity.

In some patients, the immune system can also attack normal tissues.

This produces immune-related adverse events.

This is a fundamental difference from many chemotherapy toxicities.

---

## 11.2 Common symptoms

The supplied ACS source identifies symptoms such as:

- fatigue or weakness;
- fever;
- cough;
- nausea;
- itching;
- skin rash;
- appetite loss;
- muscle or joint pain;
- constipation or diarrhea.

These symptoms are not automatically immune-related.

They require clinical interpretation.

---

## 11.3 Potentially serious immune-related toxicity

Important organ systems include:

- skin;
- gastrointestinal tract;
- liver;
- lungs;
- endocrine glands;
- kidneys;
- other organs.

Potential serious manifestations include inflammatory or autoimmune-like injury.

The patient-facing safety message is simple:

> New or unusual symptoms during checkpoint-inhibitor treatment should be reported promptly to the cancer care team.

---

## 11.4 Infusion reactions

Checkpoint inhibitors administered intravenously can cause infusion reactions.

Possible symptoms include:

- fever;
- chills;
- flushing;
- rash;
- itching;
- dizziness;
- wheezing;
- breathing difficulty.

Symptoms occurring during infusion should be reported immediately to the treating team.

---

## 11.5 Detailed toxicity management is delegated

PP-0214 does not provide:

- CTCAE grading;
- corticosteroid dosing;
- steroid tapering;
- treatment interruption algorithms;
- organ-specific immunosuppression;
- rechallenge protocols.

Those belong to PP-0231 Treatment-related Toxicity and Supportive Care and the relevant detailed toxicity packages.

# 12. Patient Explanation

## “What is an immune checkpoint inhibitor?”

It is a cancer treatment that blocks one of the immune system's “brakes” so that immune cells can attack cancer more effectively.

---

## “Is it the same as chemotherapy?”

No.

Chemotherapy directly affects rapidly dividing cells through cytotoxic mechanisms.

Checkpoint inhibitors work mainly by changing immune regulation.

They are often combined with chemotherapy rather than always replacing it.

---

## “Why do I need biomarker testing?”

Some checkpoint-inhibitor strategies depend on characteristics of the tumor such as PD-L1 expression or MSI-H/dMMR status.

Testing can therefore help determine which treatment contexts are supported by evidence.

---

## “Does a positive biomarker mean the treatment will work?”

No.

A biomarker can identify a group in which a treatment has greater likelihood of benefit.

It does not guarantee response for one individual.

---

## “Can immunotherapy be used before surgery?”

In selected settings, yes.

The project evidence includes perioperative checkpoint-inhibitor strategies.

Whether this is appropriate depends on the tumor, biomarkers, stage, overall health and treatment plan.

---

## “Can immunotherapy be combined with another targeted drug?”

Yes.

KEYNOTE-811 is an important example in which pembrolizumab was combined with trastuzumab and chemotherapy for selected HER2-positive advanced disease.

---

## “Why can side effects be different from chemotherapy?”

Checkpoint inhibitors activate or restore immune activity.

That can sometimes cause the immune system to attack normal tissues.

These are called immune-related adverse events.

---

## “If my scan looks worse, does that always mean the treatment failed?”

Not necessarily.

Checkpoint inhibitors can occasionally produce unconventional response patterns.

However, apparent progression still needs proper clinical assessment.

---

## “Can I have a long-lasting response?”

Some patients experience durable responses.

The duration varies considerably and cannot be predicted with certainty for every individual.

---

# 13. Common Misconceptions

### Myth 1 — “Immunotherapy and checkpoint inhibition are the same thing.”

**Fact:** Checkpoint inhibition is one major type of immunotherapy.

### Myth 2 — “Checkpoint inhibitors directly kill every cancer cell.”

**Fact:** Their primary therapeutic mechanism is to modify immune regulation so immune cells can attack cancer more effectively.

### Myth 3 — “PD-1 and PD-L1 are the same molecule.”

**Fact:** PD-1 is a receptor and PD-L1 is a ligand involved in the checkpoint interaction.

### Myth 4 — “A high PD-L1 result guarantees response.”

**Fact:** PD-L1 can help define treatment contexts, but it does not guarantee response.

### Myth 5 — “MSI-H means the patient will definitely respond.”

**Fact:** MSI-H/dMMR is an important checkpoint-inhibitor context, but individual response is not guaranteed.

### Myth 6 — “All checkpoint inhibitors are interchangeable.”

**Fact:** Different drugs target different checkpoints and have different evidence, combinations and treatment contexts.

### Myth 7 — “Checkpoint inhibitors are always safer than chemotherapy.”

**Fact:** Their toxicity is different and can include serious immune-mediated injury.

### Myth 8 — “Any scan worsening during immunotherapy is pseudoprogression.”

**Fact:** Pseudoprogression is possible but should not be assumed without clinical assessment.

### Myth 9 — “If one checkpoint inhibitor fails, all immunotherapy is useless.”

**Fact:** Resistance is biologically complex and depends on tumor and treatment context.

### Myth 10 — “A checkpoint inhibitor can be selected without knowing the tumor's other characteristics.”

**Fact:** Treatment context can depend on biomarkers, disease setting, previous treatment, health and toxicity considerations.

# 16. Clinical Safety Boundary

This CKO is educational and does not provide individualized treatment recommendations.

A checkpoint inhibitor should not be started, stopped, continued, or changed solely on the basis of this package.

Individual treatment depends on clinical assessment, disease extent, pathology, biomarkers, previous treatment, performance status, comorbidities, treatment goals, potential benefit and toxicity.

New or worsening symptoms during checkpoint-inhibitor treatment should be communicated promptly to the treating cancer team.

Formal treatment selection, toxicity management and response assessment are delegated to their dedicated Population Packages.

**Supporting =** TMB-H context; EBV/tumor-immune context where clinically relevant; selected trial nuance; combination principles; patient questions and misconceptions.
