# Knowledge Block 1 — What Is Genetic Counseling?

Genetic counseling is a clinical process that helps people understand and adapt to the medical, psychological and family implications of genetic information.

It is broader than simply explaining how a genetic test works.

Counseling may include:

- understanding why hereditary risk is being considered;
- discussing testing options;
- reviewing possible benefits and limitations;
- preparing for possible results;
- assessing psychosocial concerns;
- considering family implications;
- supporting informed decisions;
- interpreting results after testing.

---

# Knowledge Block 2 — Why Is Genetic Counseling Important in Hereditary Gastric Cancer?

Genetic counseling can help a person or family understand:

- why hereditary gastric-cancer risk is being considered;
- what information is already known;
- whether genetic testing may be useful;
- what testing could and could not tell them;
- how results could affect the patient and relatives;
- what decisions may follow a result.

The purpose is not simply to increase testing.

The purpose is to support an informed clinical decision.

---

# Knowledge Block 3 — What Happens Before Genetic Testing?

Pretest counseling commonly addresses:

1. The reason testing is being considered.
2. The genes or hereditary syndromes that may be relevant.
3. Available testing options.
4. Potential benefits.
5. Limitations and uncertainties.
6. Possible results.
7. Medical implications.
8. Family implications.
9. Psychosocial considerations.
10. Privacy and confidentiality.
11. The patient's preferences and ability to make an informed decision.

The exact hereditary-risk assessment and syndrome-specific testing criteria are handled by other Population Packages.

---

# Knowledge Block 4 — Does Counseling Mean the Patient Must Have Genetic Testing?

No.

Genetic counseling supports voluntary and informed decision-making.

Depending on the situation, a patient may:

- proceed with testing;
- delay testing;
- request more information;
- decide not to test.

Counseling should help the patient understand the consequences of these choices rather than pressure the patient toward one decision.

---

# Knowledge Block 5 — What Are the Benefits of Genetic Testing?

Potential benefits can include:

- clarifying whether an inherited pathogenic variant has been identified;
- improving understanding of hereditary risk;
- informing appropriate risk-management decisions;
- identifying relatives who may also be at risk;
- explaining a family's cancer pattern;
- in selected settings, contributing to treatment decisions.

Benefits depend on the clinical context and the type of result obtained.

---

# Knowledge Block 6 — What Are the Limitations of Genetic Testing?

Genetic testing does not answer every hereditary-cancer question.

Limitations may include:

- not every hereditary mechanism is known;
- not every pathogenic variant is detectable;
- a negative result may not eliminate hereditary risk;
- some variants remain uncertain;
- interpretation can change as evidence evolves;
- a test may not explain the entire cancer pattern in a family.

Therefore, a genetic result should be interpreted together with the clinical and family history.

---

# Knowledge Block 7 — What Does Informed Consent Mean?

Informed consent means that the person understands enough about the proposed testing to make a voluntary decision.

Important elements include:

- why testing is being considered;
- what may be tested;
- possible results;
- potential benefits;
- potential limitations;
- medical and family implications;
- psychosocial implications;
- relevant privacy/confidentiality considerations.

The goal is not to eliminate uncertainty.

The goal is to make the decision informed.

---

# Knowledge Block 8 — What Results Might a Genetic Test Produce?

Common result categories include:

### Pathogenic / likely pathogenic variant

May establish a clinically relevant hereditary susceptibility.

### Negative result

No relevant pathogenic variant was identified by the test performed.

A negative result does not always eliminate hereditary risk.

### Variant of uncertain significance (VUS)

A genetic change was identified, but current evidence is insufficient to determine whether it is clinically important.

A VUS is **not equivalent to a pathogenic variant**.

### Other uncertain or discordant findings

Some results may require additional interpretation because evidence is limited or because the molecular finding does not fully match the clinical picture.

---

# Knowledge Block 9 — What Happens After a Genetic Test?

Posttest counseling helps the patient understand:

- what the result means;
- what it does not mean;
- how it changes or does not change hereditary-risk assessment;
- whether additional evaluation is appropriate;
- whether relatives may be affected;
- what downstream clinical pathways may be relevant.

Result-specific interpretation belongs to dedicated molecular/genetic Population Packages.

---

# Knowledge Block 10 — Why Are Psychosocial Issues Part of Genetic Counseling?

Genetic information can affect:

- fear and anxiety;
- perception of cancer risk;
- coping;
- family relationships;
- communication;
- reproductive or family-planning decisions;
- expectations about the future.

A good counseling process considers these issues rather than treating genetic testing as a purely technical procedure.

---

# Knowledge Block 11 — How Does Counseling Address Family Implications?

A hereditary finding can have implications for biological relatives.

Counseling can help patients think through:

- which relatives may need to know;
- how to communicate the information;
- what information should be shared;
- how to handle uncertainty;
- how family relationships may affect communication.

Detailed testing of relatives is a separate downstream process.

---

# Knowledge Block 12 — What About Privacy and Confidentiality?

Genetic information is sensitive health information.

Counseling may address:

- confidentiality;
- who may have access to genetic information;
- sharing information with relatives;
- possible insurance or employment concerns;
- jurisdiction-specific protections and limitations.

Legal protections differ between countries and jurisdictions.

Therefore, general educational information should not be treated as jurisdiction-specific legal advice.

---

# Knowledge Block 13 — What If the Patient Is Especially Vulnerable?

Additional care may be needed when factors affect informed decision-making, such as:

- communication or language barriers;
- cognitive limitations;
- significant psychological distress;
- acute illness;
- financial or social vulnerability;
- family pressure or coercion.

The counseling process should support an autonomous and informed decision.

---

# Knowledge Block 14 — What About Multigene Panels?

Multigene testing can examine several genes at the same time.

This can increase the opportunity to identify a pathogenic variant, but it can also increase the number and complexity of findings that require interpretation.

Counseling should therefore prepare patients for:

- potentially informative findings;
- negative findings;
- VUS;
- findings in genes with limited evidence or uncertain clinical utility.

Detailed panel methodology belongs to the genetic-testing domain.

---

# Knowledge Block 15 — What Happens If a Pathogenic Variant Is Found?

A pathogenic germline result may have implications for:

- the patient's hereditary risk;
- syndrome-specific surveillance or risk reduction;
- biological relatives;
- family communication;
- cascade testing.

The counseling package explains these implications at a high level.

The actual management pathway is delegated to syndrome-, gene-, surveillance- and surgery-specific Population Packages.

---

# Knowledge Block 16 — What If Testing Is Negative but the Family History Is Strong?

A negative test does not necessarily make the hereditary concern disappear.

The meaning of a negative result depends on:

- what was tested;
- whether a familial pathogenic variant was already known;
- the strength and pattern of the clinical phenotype;
- limitations of the test;
- current knowledge.

Persistent strong HDGC-like patterns are handled by the dedicated HDGC-like Population Package.

---

# Knowledge Block 17 — What Is the Role of Shared Decision-Making?

Genetic counseling should help patients make choices that reflect:

- their clinical situation;
- their values;
- their preferences;
- their understanding of benefits and limitations;
- their family circumstances.

Counseling is therefore a decision-support process, not an instruction to undergo testing.

# Common Misconceptions

## Myth 1
**Genetic counseling means the patient will definitely have genetic testing.**

**Fact:** Counseling supports an informed decision; testing may be accepted, deferred or declined.

## Myth 2
**Genetic counseling is just explaining the laboratory test.**

**Fact:** It also addresses medical, psychological, family, ethical and decision-making implications.

## Myth 3
**A negative genetic test means hereditary risk is completely ruled out.**

**Fact:** The meaning of a negative result depends on the test, the family history and the clinical context.

## Myth 4
**A VUS means the patient has a cancer-causing mutation.**

**Fact:** A VUS is uncertain and is not equivalent to a pathogenic variant.

## Myth 5
**A positive genetic result automatically tells the patient what treatment or surgery to have.**

**Fact:** A result may inform management, but specific management belongs to the relevant clinical pathway.

## Myth 6
**Genetic results only affect the person tested.**

**Fact:** A pathogenic germline finding can have implications for biological relatives.

# Key Messages

- Genetic counseling is broader than genetic testing education.
- Pretest counseling prepares patients for the decision and possible outcomes.
- Posttest counseling helps patients understand the result and its implications.
- Genetic testing is voluntary and should be informed.
- Benefits, limitations and uncertainties should be discussed before testing.
- A negative result does not always eliminate hereditary risk.
- A VUS is not the same as a pathogenic variant.
- Psychosocial and family implications are part of genetic counseling.
- Genetic information can have implications for biological relatives.
- Detailed testing, gene interpretation, cascade testing and management belong to dedicated downstream packages.
