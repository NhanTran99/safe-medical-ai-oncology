# Clinical Question

> **What is palliative care in gastric cancer, when can it be integrated, what goals does it serve, how does it coexist with active anticancer treatment, and how does it support patients and families across the cancer trajectory?**

---

# Educational Intent

The package should enable patients and caregivers to understand palliative care without interpreting it as:

- abandonment;
- treatment cessation;
- hospice-only care;
- pain medication only;
- “nothing more can be done.”

The evidence package therefore prioritizes:

1. gastric-cancer-specific guideline framing;
2. longitudinal supportive/palliative-care principles;
3. patient-facing communication;
4. goals-of-care architecture;
5. end-of-life interface;
6. clear separation from detailed Best Supportive Care.

# Primary Evidence Sources

## 1. NCCN Gastric Cancer Version 2.2026

Primary gastric-cancer-specific authority.

The supplied NCCN section GAST-J identifies:

**PRINCIPLES OF PALLIATIVE CARE/BEST SUPPORTIVE CARE**

It states that the goal of best supportive care is to prevent and relieve suffering and support the best possible quality of life for patients and their families, regardless of disease stage or the need for other therapies.

It also states that interventions undertaken to relieve major symptoms may result in prolongation of life and that a multimodality interdisciplinary approach is encouraged.

The source therefore directly supports the central PP-0227 framework.

# Detailed Evidence Notes / Appraisal

## Evidence 1 — NCCN Gastric Cancer v2.2026

### Core statement

NCCN states that the goal of best supportive care is to prevent and relieve suffering and support the best possible quality of life for patients and their families, regardless of disease stage or need for other therapies.

### Why it matters

This single statement establishes several PP-0227 principles:

1. quality of life;
2. relief of suffering;
3. patient/family focus;
4. longitudinal applicability;
5. compatibility with other therapies.

### Additional NCCN point

NCCN states that symptom-relieving interventions can sometimes prolong life and encourages a multimodality interdisciplinary approach.

### Interpretation

Palliative care should not be presented as a passive alternative to cancer treatment.

### Evidence maturity

**Established / guideline-supported.**

---

## Evidence 2 — ESMO-ASCO Supportive Care

The ESMO-ASCO curriculum states that supportive-care needs can exist from diagnosis throughout the cancer trajectory, including:

- prehabilitation;
- treatment;
- survivorship/rehabilitation;
- end of life.

It also identifies:

- anxiety;
- depression;
- bleeding;
- cachexia;
- fatigue;
- malignant effusions;
- nausea;
- nutritional support;
- pain;
- psychosocial effects;
- other physical and psychosocial needs.

### Clinical interpretation

Palliative care requires broad assessment.

It cannot be reduced to one symptom or one stage.

### Evidence maturity

**Established / consensus-supported.**

---

## Evidence 3 — Individual Assessment

ESMO-ASCO emphasizes that correct management of symptoms requires clinical and individual assessment.

It also emphasizes overlap between cancer symptoms and treatment toxicities.

### PP-0227 implication

The package should teach:

> “A symptom should be assessed before its treatment is assumed.”

It should not provide an algorithm that treats every patient identically.

### Evidence maturity

**Established.**

---

## Evidence 4 — Multidisciplinary Care

NCCN encourages multimodality interdisciplinary palliative care.

ESMO-ASCO states that supportive and symptom management is often multidisciplinary and identifies the integration of physicians, nurses, pharmacists and allied health professionals.

### PP-0227 implication

Palliative care is a team-based clinical approach.

Detailed operational team design belongs to PP-0232.

---

## Evidence 5 — Early Integration

ESMO-ASCO explicitly includes:

- principles and benefits of early integration of palliative care;
- supportive care throughout the cancer trajectory.

### Interpretation

PP-0227 should not define a late-stage gate for palliative care.

The package should instead teach needs-based integration.

### Important evidence boundary

The supplied materials support early integration as a principle.

They do not, within the extracted project evidence, establish one universal referral day or threshold.

Therefore no rigid timing rule should be invented.

---

## Evidence 6 — Advance Care Planning

ESMO-ASCO identifies advance care planning as a core supportive/palliative-care concept.

It also includes:

- shared goals of life;
- goals of care;
- physical/emotional/social/cultural/spiritual dimensions;
- family involvement;
- preferred place of end-of-life care.

### Interpretation

Advance care planning is a process, not merely a legal form.

### Scope

PP-0227 owns the conceptual framework.

Jurisdiction-specific documentation is excluded.

---

## Evidence 7 — End-of-Life Care

ESMO-ASCO includes:

- recognition of dying;
- safe and comfortable dying;
- family distress;
- bereavement;
- early referral;
- continuity;
- life goals;
- comfort.

### Interpretation

End-of-life care is a core component of palliative care.

However, it is not the definition of all palliative care.

---

## Evidence 8 — Continuity / Non-Abandonment

ESMO-ASCO explicitly emphasizes continuity of compassionate care throughout the cancer experience, including end of life, without abandonment when treatment to control cancer is no longer effective.

### Patient-facing implication

This is an important safety message:

> A change from cancer-directed treatment to comfort-focused care is a change in goals, not abandonment.

---

## Evidence 9 — ACS: Treatment Continuation or Cessation

ACS states that people may still receive supportive or palliative care whether they continue cancer treatment or not.

It also explains that people who choose not to receive cancer treatment can still receive care for pain and other symptoms.

### Interpretation

This directly supports the PP-0227 misconception-control model.

---

## Evidence 10 — ACS: Hospice

ACS describes hospice for people with advanced cancer approaching the end of life and frames hospice around quality of life.

### Boundary

This supports conceptual hospice education.

It does not establish universal hospice eligibility because eligibility depends on jurisdiction and care system.

---

## Evidence 11 — NCI PDQ: Palliative Treatment

NCI PDQ describes advanced/inoperable/recurrent gastric-cancer treatment as including:

- systemic therapies;
- immunotherapies;
- targeted therapies;
- palliative locoregional therapies.

It also describes palliative radiation, endoluminal interventions, and palliative resection in selected circumstances.

### PP-0227 interpretation

These examples establish that palliative care can involve active disease-directed or procedural interventions.

Detailed intervention ownership remains elsewhere.

# Evidence Limitations

1. The NCCN section combines palliative care and best supportive care, so project-level separation requires an explicit architectural boundary.
2. ESMO-ASCO is a curriculum/consensus framework rather than a gastric-cancer-specific trial evidence base.
3. ACS is patient-facing and should not be treated as the primary professional guideline.
4. Hospice eligibility varies across health systems and jurisdictions.
5. The supplied sources support early integration conceptually but do not establish one universal referral threshold for every gastric-cancer patient.
6. Palliative-care interventions are heterogeneous.
7. Individual symptoms may have multiple causes.
8. Treatment toxicity may overlap with cancer-related symptoms.
9. The package is not intended to quantify an individual's survival.
10. Detailed symptom-management evidence is delegated to PP-0228 and other packages.

---

# Evidence Gaps

## Gap 1 — Universal timing

There is no project-supported single timing rule that should be applied to every patient.

## Gap 2 — Delivery model

The exact composition and intensity of palliative-care services varies by healthcare system.

## Gap 3 — Resource availability

Access to palliative specialists, home care, psychosocial services and hospice differs between settings.

## Gap 4 — Symptom heterogeneity

Patients may have different combinations of disease-related and treatment-related symptoms.

## Gap 5 — Goals

Patient goals are dynamic and cannot be represented by one universal hierarchy.

## Gap 6 — Hospice

Operational hospice criteria are jurisdiction-dependent.
