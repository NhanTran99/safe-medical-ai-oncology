# Educational Objectives

After reading this Population Package, the reader should be able to:

1. Explain what palliative care means in gastric cancer.
2. Understand that palliative care is not limited to the final days or weeks of life.
3. Understand that palliative care can be provided alongside active anticancer treatment.
4. Understand the difference between palliative care and best supportive care.
5. Understand the major goals of palliative care: relief of suffering, quality of life, function, communication, and support for patients and families.
6. Recognize that palliative needs can occur from diagnosis through treatment, advanced disease, survivorship/rehabilitation, and end of life.
7. Understand that symptoms may arise from cancer, treatment, comorbidities, or several causes at the same time.
8. Understand why clinical and individual assessment is necessary before choosing symptom-management strategies.
9. Recognize physical, emotional, social, cultural, spiritual, nutritional, functional, and caregiver needs as legitimate components of palliative care.
10. Understand the role of goals-of-care discussions and shared decision-making.
11. Understand advance care planning at a conceptual level.
12. Understand the role of multidisciplinary palliative care.
13. Understand that disease-directed interventions may sometimes have a palliative purpose.
14. Understand the relationship between palliative care, supportive care, and hospice.
15. Understand that choosing to stop anticancer treatment does not mean stopping medical care.
16. Understand how palliative priorities may change as disease trajectory and patient goals change.
17. Recognize the importance of communication, continuity, and non-abandonment.
18. Understand why end-of-life care is one component of palliative care rather than its entire definition.
19. Recognize common misconceptions about palliative care.
20. Identify practical questions to discuss with the cancer-care team.

# Clinical Knowledge Blocks

## 1. What Is Palliative Care?

Palliative care is a clinical approach focused on preventing and relieving suffering and supporting the best possible quality of life for patients and their families.

For gastric cancer, the supplied NCCN guideline places palliative care and best supportive care within the same clinical framework and states that the goal is to prevent and relieve suffering and support the best possible quality of life regardless of disease stage or the need for other therapies.

The same source emphasizes a multimodality interdisciplinary approach to palliative care in gastric cancer.

The central concept is therefore:

**Palliative care is care focused on the person’s experience of cancer and its consequences, not only on whether the tumor is responding.**

---

## 2. Palliative Care Is Not the Same as “Giving Up”

One of the most important patient-facing messages is:

> Palliative care does not mean that doctors have stopped caring for the patient.

Palliative care can be provided while a patient is:

- receiving chemotherapy;
- receiving immunotherapy;
- receiving targeted therapy;
- undergoing radiation;
- recovering from surgery;
- receiving treatment for complications;
- considering another line of treatment.

The purpose is to address suffering and quality-of-life needs while cancer treatment decisions continue.

The American Cancer Society explicitly explains that supportive or palliative care can continue whether or not a person receives cancer treatment.

---

## 3. Palliative Care Can Begin Early

Palliative needs are not restricted to advanced cancer.

The supplied ESMO-ASCO curriculum states that supportive-care needs can be present:

- from diagnosis;
- during treatment;
- during prehabilitation;
- during survivorship and rehabilitation;
- at end of life.

It also identifies early integration of palliative care as an important principle.

For PP-0227, this means that the package should not define eligibility using a single disease stage.

Instead:

> **The need for palliative care is driven by needs, symptoms, suffering, treatment burden, goals, and trajectory.**

---

## 4. Palliative Care Across the Cancer Trajectory

A useful conceptual model is:

**Diagnosis**

↓

**Treatment**

↓

**Response / stable disease**

↓

**Recurrence or progression**

↓

**Advanced disease**

↓

**End of life, when applicable**

At every stage, the patient's needs can change.

Examples:

### During treatment

A patient may need:

- symptom control;
- emotional support;
- nutritional support;
- treatment-burden assessment;
- communication support.

### During advanced disease

Needs may include:

- symptom relief;
- functional support;
- goals-of-care discussions;
- treatment-benefit versus burden discussions;
- family support.

### Near end of life

The priorities may increasingly emphasize:

- comfort;
- dignity;
- important life goals;
- preferred place of care;
- family support;
- continuity;
- safe and compassionate dying.

The trajectory is not necessarily linear.

---

## 5. Why Quality of Life Is Central

Quality of life is broader than absence of pain.

It may include:

- physical comfort;
- ability to eat or drink when possible;
- sleep;
- mobility;
- independence;
- ability to communicate;
- emotional well-being;
- social relationships;
- meaningful activities;
- family roles;
- cultural and spiritual priorities;
- ability to remain in a preferred setting.

Therefore, palliative care asks not only:

> “How is the cancer?”

but also:

> “How is the person living with the cancer?”

---

## 6. Palliative Care Addresses Suffering

Suffering can arise from multiple sources.

### Physical

- pain;
- nausea;
- vomiting;
- bleeding;
- obstruction;
- fatigue;
- breathlessness;
- nutritional problems;
- other disease-related symptoms.

### Treatment-related

- treatment toxicity;
- functional impairment;
- treatment burden;
- repeated hospital visits;
- procedure-related effects.

### Emotional

- anxiety;
- depression;
- fear;
- uncertainty;
- distress about progression;
- distress about treatment decisions.

### Social

- caregiver burden;
- work and financial consequences;
- transportation;
- family responsibilities;
- social isolation.

### Spiritual/cultural

- meaning;
- values;
- beliefs;
- rituals;
- preferred place of care;
- end-of-life preferences.

The supplied ESMO-ASCO curriculum explicitly recognizes physical and psychosocial needs and emphasizes individual assessment.

---

## 7. Symptoms Can Have More Than One Cause

A symptom does not automatically have one explanation.

For example, fatigue could reflect:

- cancer;
- anemia;
- treatment toxicity;
- poor nutrition;
- infection;
- sleep disturbance;
- psychological distress;
- several factors together.

Similarly, nausea may relate to:

- gastric obstruction;
- treatment;
- medication;
- metabolic factors;
- anxiety;
- other illness.

Therefore:

> **Palliative care begins with assessment rather than assuming the cause.**

This is why detailed symptom-specific treatment is outside PP-0227 and delegated to PP-0228 and relevant clinical packages.

---

## 8. Palliative Care and Best Supportive Care

The terms overlap but are not identical in the architecture of this project.

### Palliative Care — PP-0227

Owns the:

- clinical approach;
- goals;
- timing;
- integration;
- communication;
- patient/family-centered framework;
- quality-of-life orientation;
- end-of-life framework.

### Best Supportive Care — PP-0228

Owns the:

- specific supportive interventions;
- symptom-directed care;
- practical management of complications;
- detailed supportive strategies.

The distinction is architectural rather than an assertion that clinicians always use these terms identically.

The NCCN source itself places them together in “Principles of Palliative Care/Best Supportive Care.”

---

## 9. Palliative Care Can Include Active Interventions

Palliative care does not mean “doing nothing.”

An intervention may have a palliative purpose even when it is an active medical or procedural treatment.

In gastric cancer, the NCCN source includes examples of interventions used to relieve major symptoms, including approaches for:

- bleeding;
- obstruction;
- pain;
- nausea/vomiting;
- selected local or procedural palliation.

These details belong to the relevant supportive/treatment packages.

The PP-0227 principle is:

> **An active treatment can be palliative when its main clinical purpose is symptom relief, function preservation, or quality-of-life improvement.**

---

## 10. Palliative Systemic Therapy and Palliative Care

PP-0208 owns palliative systemic therapy.

That package asks:

> Which systemic anticancer therapies may be used in unresectable, recurrent, or metastatic disease?

PP-0227 asks:

> How does palliative care support the patient while treatment decisions are being made and delivered?

These can coexist.

For example:

**Palliative systemic treatment**

and

**palliative care**

may occur simultaneously.

The presence of one does not exclude the other.

---

## 11. Goals of Care

Goals of care describe what the patient and healthcare team are trying to achieve at a particular point in the illness.

Possible goals can include:

- controlling cancer;
- slowing progression;
- reducing symptoms;
- maintaining function;
- remaining at home;
- maintaining independence;
- attending important family events;
- preserving the ability to eat or communicate;
- minimizing hospital time;
- maximizing comfort.

Goals can change.

A patient may initially prioritize disease control and later place greater emphasis on comfort or time at home.

Neither goal is inherently “wrong.”

---

## 12. Shared Decision-Making

Shared decision-making means that treatment and care decisions are developed through communication among:

- the patient;
- appropriate family/caregivers;
- the healthcare team.

The process should consider:

- clinical information;
- likely benefits;
- burdens;
- uncertainty;
- alternatives;
- patient values;
- preferences;
- practical circumstances.

ESMO-ASCO explicitly includes shared goals of life and goals of care across physical, emotional, social, cultural, and spiritual dimensions.

---

## 13. Advance Care Planning

Advance care planning is a process of discussing and documenting preferences for future care in case the patient later becomes unable to communicate or make decisions.

At the conceptual level, this can include:

- values;
- goals;
- acceptable or unacceptable treatment burdens;
- preferred place of care;
- people the patient wants involved;
- preferences for end-of-life care.

PP-0227 does not provide jurisdiction-specific legal forms or rules.

---

## 14. Communication Is Part of Care

Palliative care is not only a collection of medical interventions.

Communication itself is clinically important.

Important conversations may address:

- what the patient understands;
- what matters most;
- what is worrying the patient;
- what outcomes are realistic;
- what benefits and burdens are expected;
- whether goals have changed;
- what support the family needs.

ESMO-ASCO emphasizes sensitive discussion of prognosis, goals of care, family involvement, continuity, and compassionate communication.

---

## 15. Prognosis: What Palliative Care Needs to Know

Prognosis means the expected course or outlook of an illness.

Palliative care may require discussion of prognosis because prognosis can affect:

- treatment decisions;
- advance care planning;
- family planning;
- practical arrangements;
- goals of care;
- preferred place of care.

However:

> **A prognosis is not a guaranteed timeline.**

PP-0227 does not calculate individual survival.

---

## 16. Palliative Care and Treatment Burden

Treatment can impose burdens even when it has potential benefits.

Burden can include:

- side effects;
- hospitalization;
- procedures;
- travel;
- financial stress;
- time away from home;
- nutritional or functional effects;
- emotional burden.

Palliative care helps place those burdens in the context of the patient's goals.

This does not mean that palliative care decides whether treatment should continue.

Rather:

> **It helps make the benefits, burdens, needs, and goals visible in the decision.**

---

## 17. When Anticancer Treatment Is No Longer Helpful

Cancer treatment may eventually become unable to provide enough benefit relative to its burdens.

This is a clinical decision that should be discussed carefully.

The ACS source states that some people may decide to stop treatment or choose no treatment when treatments are no longer controlling the cancer, while still receiving supportive or palliative care.

Therefore:

**Stopping anticancer treatment**

does not mean:

**stopping medical care.**

Palliative care may become even more important.

---

## 18. Comfort-Focused Care

When a patient's goals shift toward comfort, care may focus increasingly on:

- relief of distressing symptoms;
- dignity;
- meaningful activities;
- preferred environment;
- family presence;
- communication;
- minimizing burdensome interventions that no longer match goals.

This does not imply that every patient will follow the same pathway.

Goals should remain individualized.

---

## 19. End-of-Life Care Is Part of Palliative Care

End-of-life care is a major component of palliative care but not its entire definition.

ESMO-ASCO includes:

- advance care planning;
- shared goals;
- family support;
- place of care;
- recognition of dying;
- comfort;
- continuity;
- bereavement support.

The package therefore includes end-of-life concepts but does not redefine all palliative care as end-of-life care.

---

## 20. Hospice

Hospice is a care/service model associated with people approaching the end of life.

The ACS patient-facing material describes hospice as focusing on the best possible quality of life near the end of life and notes that care may occur at home or in other settings.

Important distinction:

> **Hospice is not synonymous with palliative care.**

Palliative care is broader and can occur earlier and alongside anticancer treatment.

Hospice rules and eligibility can differ by jurisdiction and payer and are outside PP-0227.

---

## 21. Family and Caregiver Support

Cancer affects families as well as patients.

Caregivers may experience:

- emotional distress;
- physical workload;
- sleep disruption;
- financial pressure;
- uncertainty;
- difficulty understanding symptoms;
- grief.

Palliative care can include assessment and support of caregiver needs.

ESMO-ASCO explicitly includes family involvement and bereavement support.

---

## 22. Psychosocial Care

Psychosocial care recognizes that cancer affects emotional and social well-being.

Examples include:

- anxiety;
- depression;
- distress;
- relationship strain;
- social isolation;
- financial stress;
- work disruption;
- family-role changes.

The supplied ESMO-ASCO source emphasizes psychosocial assessment and multidisciplinary supportive care.

Detailed psychological treatment is not owned by PP-0227.

---

## 23. Spiritual and Cultural Needs

Patients may have different beliefs and values concerning:

- illness;
- suffering;
- treatment;
- death;
- family roles;
- decision-making;
- place of care;
- rituals.

ESMO-ASCO specifically includes cultural and spiritual dimensions in goals-of-care discussions.

Palliative care should therefore be respectful and individualized.

---

## 24. Nutrition and Function

Gastric cancer can create nutritional and functional challenges.

Palliative care should recognize:

- reduced intake;
- weight loss;
- cachexia;
- obstruction;
- treatment effects;
- weakness;
- functional decline.

ESMO-ASCO identifies nutritional support as part of supportive care and emphasizes individual and multidisciplinary assessment.

Detailed nutrition treatment is delegated.

---

## 25. Rehabilitation and Function

Maintaining function can itself be a meaningful palliative goal.

Depending on the patient's condition, rehabilitation may address:

- mobility;
- strength;
- independence;
- daily activities;
- recovery after treatment.

Palliative care therefore overlaps with rehabilitation but does not replace the rehabilitation package.

---

## 26. Multidisciplinary Palliative Care

The NCCN guideline encourages a multimodality interdisciplinary approach.

The ESMO-ASCO curriculum similarly emphasizes multidisciplinary symptom/supportive care.

Potential contributors can include:

- oncologists;
- palliative-care clinicians;
- nurses;
- pharmacists;
- dietitians;
- rehabilitation professionals;
- social workers;
- psychologists/counsellors;
- spiritual-care professionals;
- other specialists.

PP-0232 owns the operational multidisciplinary-management framework.

---

## 27. Continuity and Non-Abandonment

A particularly important end-of-life principle in ESMO-ASCO is continuity of compassionate care and avoidance of abandonment when treatment to control cancer is no longer effective.

The patient's care does not end when anticancer treatment ends.

The focus may change.

This principle should remain explicit in PP-0227.

---

# Patient Explanation

## 28. A Simple Explanation

Palliative care is an approach that helps people with cancer live as well as possible by reducing suffering and addressing symptoms, emotions, practical concerns, family needs, and personal goals.

It can be given while cancer treatment is continuing.

It can also continue if cancer treatment is stopped.

It is not limited to the last days of life.

---

## 29. What Palliative Care Can Help With

Depending on the person's needs, palliative care can help address:

- pain;
- nausea;
- vomiting;
- bleeding;
- difficulty eating;
- fatigue;
- emotional distress;
- anxiety;
- family/caregiver needs;
- practical concerns;
- treatment burden;
- decisions about goals of care;
- planning for future care.

The detailed treatment of individual symptoms belongs to supportive-care packages.

---

## 30. What a Patient Should Not Infer

A patient should not infer:

- “Palliative care means my cancer treatment has stopped.”
- “Palliative care means I am dying now.”
- “Hospice and palliative care are exactly the same.”
- “There is nothing more doctors can do.”
- “Palliative care is only pain medication.”
- “The palliative-care team decides whether I should continue cancer treatment.”
- “Every patient needs the same palliative-care plan.”

# Common Misconceptions

## Myth 1 — “Palliative care means giving up.”

**Fact:** It is active care focused on reducing suffering and improving quality of life.

## Myth 2 — “Palliative care is only for terminal cancer.”

**Fact:** It can be integrated throughout the cancer trajectory.

## Myth 3 — “Palliative care means stopping chemotherapy.”

**Fact:** Palliative care can coexist with anticancer treatment.

## Myth 4 — “If treatment stops, care stops.”

**Fact:** Supportive and palliative care continue.

## Myth 5 — “Hospice equals palliative care.”

**Fact:** Hospice is one end-of-life care model; palliative care is broader.

## Myth 6 — “Palliative care is only about pain.”

**Fact:** It addresses physical, emotional, social, cultural, spiritual, functional and family needs.

## Myth 7 — “Palliative care means only medications.”

**Fact:** Care can include communication, procedures, rehabilitation, nutrition, psychosocial support and other interventions.

## Myth 8 — “Only the oncologist decides goals of care.”

**Fact:** Goals should be discussed with the patient and appropriate family/caregivers and care team.

## Myth 9 — “A palliative procedure means cancer treatment has failed.”

**Fact:** Some active interventions are specifically used to relieve symptoms or preserve function.

## Myth 10 — “Every patient should receive the same palliative pathway.”

**Fact:** Palliative care is individualized according to needs and goals.

# Key Messages

1. Palliative care aims to reduce suffering and support quality of life.
2. It can begin early and continue throughout the cancer trajectory.
3. It can coexist with active anticancer treatment.
4. It is not synonymous with hospice or end-of-life care.
5. It addresses patients and families, not only tumors.
6. Physical symptoms are only one part of palliative care.
7. Emotional, social, cultural, spiritual, nutritional and functional needs matter.
8. Goals of care can change over time.
9. Advance care planning can help patients communicate future preferences.
10. Palliative care does not mean “doing nothing.”
11. Active procedures or treatments can sometimes have a palliative purpose.
12. When anticancer treatment stops, care continues.
13. End-of-life care emphasizes comfort, dignity, important life goals and family support when appropriate.
14. Detailed symptom management belongs to Best Supportive Care and relevant treatment-specific packages.
15. Individual palliative decisions should be made with the treating team.

# Patient Questions

Patients and caregivers may ask:

1. What symptoms or problems can the palliative-care team help with?
2. Can I receive palliative care while continuing cancer treatment?
3. What are my most important goals right now?
4. What can be done to improve my quality of life?
5. Which symptoms need urgent medical assessment?
6. Could my symptoms be caused by the cancer, treatment, or both?
7. What treatment benefits should I expect, and what burdens might it cause?
8. How can my family or caregiver be supported?
9. Should we discuss advance care planning now?
10. What should we do if I become unable to make decisions myself?
11. When might hospice become appropriate?
12. Where could I receive care if my goals become more comfort-focused?
13. What services are available for nutrition, rehabilitation, emotional support or social needs?
14. Who is responsible for coordinating my palliative and cancer treatments?
15. If anticancer treatment stops, what care will continue?

# Clinical Safety Boundary

This package is an educational framework.

It does not:

- decide whether an individual should receive palliative care;
- determine whether anticancer treatment should stop;
- determine hospice eligibility;
- predict an individual's survival;
- prescribe symptom medication;
- recommend palliative procedures;
- provide terminal-care dosing;
- replace clinical assessment.

Urgent or severe symptoms require appropriate medical assessment.

Detailed symptom-specific management belongs to the relevant supportive-care and treatment packages.

# Evidence Maturity

## Established / Guideline-Supported

The supplied sources support:

- palliative/supportive care as an integral component of cancer care;
- relief of suffering and quality-of-life support;
- care for patients and families;
- palliative needs across the cancer trajectory;
- multidisciplinary care;
- symptom and psychosocial assessment;
- early integration;
- goals-of-care discussions;
- advance care planning;
- end-of-life care;
- continuity and family support.

## Context-Dependent

- timing and intensity of palliative-care involvement;
- specific symptom priorities;
- caregiver involvement;
- place of care;
- transition toward comfort-focused goals;
- use of disease-directed interventions for palliation.

## Emerging / Context-Dependent

The supplied project sources support early integration as a core educational principle, but this package does not add unsupported numerical claims about survival benefit.

## Not Established / Excluded

- palliative care as a universal replacement for anticancer treatment;
- palliative care as synonymous with hospice;
- one universal referral time for every patient;
- individualized survival prediction from this framework;
- universal symptom-management algorithms.
