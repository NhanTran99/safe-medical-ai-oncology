# 3. What Is Lymphadenectomy?

Lymphadenectomy means surgical removal of lymph nodes.

In gastric cancer, the relevant concept is **regional lymphadenectomy**: removal of lymph nodes that are considered part of the regional drainage and oncologic field of the stomach.

The term therefore does not mean removing every lymph node in the body.

It also does not mean that lymph-node removal is an independent procedure that is unrelated to the stomach operation.

In curative gastric-cancer surgery, regional lymphadenectomy is integrated with gastric resection.

NCI describes surgical resection with regional lymphadenectomy as the treatment of choice for appropriate stage II and III gastric cancer and describes regional lymphadenectomy alongside the relevant gastrectomy options in stage I disease.

The central patient-facing idea is:

> **The stomach is removed to treat the primary tumor, and appropriate regional lymph nodes are removed and examined because gastric cancer can spread through the lymphatic system.**

---

# 4. Why Are Regional Lymph Nodes Important?

## 4.1 Lymph nodes are one pathway of gastric-cancer spread

Gastric cancer can spread from the primary tumor to regional lymph nodes.

The supplied Vietnamese gastric-cancer guideline explicitly identifies lymphatic spread as a major route of gastric-cancer metastasis and gives several reasons for lymphadenectomy:

1. lymph nodes are a major route of metastasis;
2. lymphadenectomy can make surgery more oncologically complete;
3. lymphadenectomy helps assess disease stage and prognosis;
4. gastric cancer can show “skip” nodal metastasis, meaning involved nodes can occasionally occur beyond the first expected nodal level.

This is why lymph-node surgery has importance beyond simply “removing extra tissue.”

---

# 5. Lymphadenectomy Has More Than One Purpose

Lymphadenectomy should not be explained as having only one purpose.

It has at least three linked clinical functions.

## Purpose 1 — Oncologic treatment

Removing involved regional lymph nodes can be part of a curative gastric-cancer operation.

## Purpose 2 — Pathologic staging

The removed nodes are examined for cancer.

The presence and number of positive regional nodes contribute to the pathological N category.

## Purpose 3 — Disease assessment

The quality and extent of nodal assessment help clinicians understand the extent of regional disease and integrate that information with the primary tumor and distant-disease findings.

These purposes are related, but they are not identical.

# 6. Lymphadenectomy and Gastrectomy

For a patient, the simplest mental model is:

**Primary gastric tumor**

↓

**Appropriate gastric resection**

+

**Regional lymph-node management**

↓

**Pathologic examination**

↓

**Postoperative staging**

↓

**Treatment planning**

Regional lymphadenectomy therefore sits inside the oncologic surgery pathway.

NCI specifically states that regional lymphadenectomy is recommended with the relevant subtotal or total gastrectomy procedures in stage I gastric cancer and describes surgical resection with regional lymphadenectomy as the treatment of choice in stage II/III disease.

This package does not decide which gastrectomy should be performed.

That belongs to the gastrectomy packages.

---

# 7. What Does “Regional” Mean?

“Regional” means lymph nodes that are anatomically associated with the stomach and its lymphatic drainage pathways.

It does not mean distant lymph nodes or all lymph nodes in the body.

The exact nodal territory depends on the operation and the accepted surgical classification.

At a high level, the lymph-node field progresses from nodes immediately associated with the stomach toward nodes along major vessels supplying and draining the gastric region.

This anatomical concept becomes increasingly detailed when moving from general lymphadenectomy to D1 and D2.

---

# 8. Why Does the Extent of Lymphadenectomy Matter?

Not every lymphadenectomy removes exactly the same nodal territory.

The extent can be described using categories such as:

- D0;
- D1;
- D1+;
- D2;
- D2+.

These labels describe **the anatomical extent of nodal dissection**.

They should not be confused with:

- the number of nodes actually retrieved;
- the number of nodes containing cancer;
- the patient's N category;
- the patient's overall stage.

This distinction is one of the most important concepts in PP-0199.

---

# 9. D0 — Conceptual Meaning

D0 describes an incomplete lymph-node dissection that does not reach the accepted D1 extent.

D0 is therefore not the same thing as a complete regional lymphadenectomy.

This package introduces D0 only to establish the hierarchy.

Detailed assessment of inadequate nodal surgery is not a separate ownership target here.

---

# 10. D1 — Conceptual Meaning

D1 represents a defined regional/perigastric lymph-node dissection associated with the gastric resection.

NCCN defines D1 in relation to specified perigastric nodal groups.

The supplied Vietnamese guideline also describes D1 using numbered gastric nodal groups.

Because the project has a dedicated PP-0200 for D1 Lymphadenectomy, PP-0199 stops at the conceptual level.

The key patient-facing message is:

> **D1 is one defined extent of regional lymph-node dissection.**

---

# 11. D1+ — Conceptual Meaning

D1+ means D1 plus additional regional nodal groups beyond the basic D1 field.

The supplied Vietnamese guideline explicitly describes D1+ as an extension of D1.

The exact nodal groups and clinical indications are delegated to the dedicated downstream lymphadenectomy packages.

The important point for PP-0199 is that lymphadenectomy is not a binary “nodes removed versus nodes not removed” concept.

There are different extents.

---

# 12. D2 — Conceptual Meaning

D2 represents a more extensive regional lymph-node dissection than D1.

NCCN describes D2 as including D1 plus additional nodal groups along major vessels including the left gastric, common hepatic, celiac and splenic arterial territories.

The Vietnamese guideline provides a more detailed station-based description and stage-linked indications.

PP-0199 uses these details only to explain the concept.

The dedicated PP-0201 owns D2 in depth.

---

# 13. D2+ — Conceptual Meaning

D2+ describes extension beyond conventional D2 into additional nodal territories in selected circumstances.

The supplied Vietnamese guideline includes examples involving para-aortic nodes and selected additional nodal groups.

These are context-specific and should not be interpreted as routine surgery for every gastric-cancer patient.

PP-0199 therefore treats D2+ as an extension concept and delegates detailed indication and technical knowledge.

---

# 14. A Critical Distinction: Extent vs Yield vs Positivity

Three different questions are often confused.

## Question A — How extensive was the dissection?

This is an **anatomical** question.

Examples:

- D1;
- D1+;
- D2;
- D2+.

## Question B — How many lymph nodes were examined?

This is a **pathologic-yield** question.

NCCN recommends a goal of examining at least 16 lymph nodes for localized resectable gastric cancer.

## Question C — How many examined lymph nodes contained cancer?

This is a **nodal-status** question.

The number of positive regional nodes contributes to the N category.

These are three different dimensions.

> **D2 does not mean “16 nodes.”**

> **Sixteen nodes does not automatically mean D2.**

> **The number of positive nodes is not the same as the number removed.**

This separation must be preserved in every future retrieval of PP-0199.

---

# 15. Why Is ≥16 Lymph Nodes Important?

The NCCN framework gives a goal of examining at least 16 lymph nodes in localized resectable gastric cancer.

This matters because adequate nodal assessment helps provide a more informative pathological evaluation of regional disease.

However, the statement should not be turned into a simplistic guarantee.

A patient should not interpret:

> “16 nodes examined”

as meaning:

> “the surgery was perfect”

or:

> “the cancer cannot recur.”

Likewise, a node count alone cannot establish D1 versus D2.

The number examined and the anatomical extent of dissection answer different questions.

---

# 16. How Do Lymph Nodes Affect N Staging?

The N category describes regional lymph-node involvement.

The supplied NCI staging material defines:

- N1 as 1–2 positive regional nodes;
- N2 as 3–6;
- N3a as 7–15;
- N3b as 16 or more positive regional nodes.

This is why lymph-node pathology is clinically important.

The sequence is:

**Nodes removed**

↓

**Nodes examined**

↓

**Nodes positive**

↓

**N category**

↓

**Overall stage when combined with T and M**

The number of positive nodes is therefore not merely a laboratory statistic.

It contributes to cancer staging.

---

# 17. Why Adequate Nodal Assessment Matters

If regional nodes are not adequately assessed, clinicians may have less complete information about the true extent of disease.

This can affect confidence in pathological staging.

The patient-facing concept is:

> **Good cancer surgery is not only about removing the visible stomach tumor. It is also about appropriately addressing and examining the regional lymphatic field.**

This does not mean that every patient needs the same extent of dissection.

The appropriate extent depends on disease context, surgical approach, guideline framework and expertise.

---

# 18. Does More Extensive Lymphadenectomy Always Mean Better Treatment?

No.

This is a critical safety message.

It is tempting to think:

> D2 is bigger than D1, therefore D2 must always be better.

The supplied evidence does not support that universal statement.

NCCN describes historical uncertainty around the survival advantage of extended D2 dissection in Western trials and notes increased morbidity in some historical comparisons.

At the same time, modern NCCN guidance recognizes the role of D2 in appropriately selected patients when performed by experienced surgeons and centers.

The correct interpretation is:

> **The goal is an oncologically appropriate lymphadenectomy, not simply the largest possible lymphadenectomy.**

---

# 19. Why Has D2 Been Controversial?

The evidence evolved differently across regions.

## East Asian experience

D2 lymphadenectomy has long been incorporated into curative gastric-cancer surgery in East Asia.

## Historical Western experience

Some Western randomized trials compared D1 and D2 and did not demonstrate a clear overall-survival advantage for D2 in those historical settings, while showing higher morbidity and mortality.

## Modern interpretation

Later evidence and improved surgical practice support D2 in experienced, high-volume settings.

NCCN therefore emphasizes appropriate selection, technical expertise and experienced centers.

The lesson is not that one region is “right” and another is “wrong.”

The lesson is:

> **The clinical value of extended lymphadenectomy depends partly on how it is performed, in whom it is performed, and what the operation is trying to achieve.**

---

# 20. Why Does Surgeon Experience Matter?

D2 surgery is technically more demanding than a limited nodal dissection.

NCCN states that D2 requires substantial training and expertise and should be performed in experienced centers.

This has direct patient-facing relevance.

When discussing a planned D2 procedure, patients can reasonably ask:

- How often does the surgeon perform gastric-cancer surgery?
- How often does the center perform D2 lymphadenectomy?
- What is the center's experience with gastric-cancer surgery?
- How are complications managed if they occur?
- How will the lymph nodes be evaluated after surgery?

These questions do not replace the surgical team's recommendation.

They help the patient understand the context in which the recommendation is being made.

---

# 21. Does D2 Mean the Spleen Must Be Removed?

No.

This is an important misconception.

NCCN states that splenectomy is not routinely performed.

The supplied Vietnamese guideline also discusses the historical and ongoing debate around splenic hilar dissection and splenectomy and describes circumstances in which splenectomy may be considered, such as specific tumor involvement.

Therefore:

> **D2 lymphadenectomy does not automatically mean splenectomy.**

Detailed spleen-preserving surgical technique is outside PP-0199.

---

# 22. Does D2 Mean the Pancreas Must Be Removed?

No.

NCCN specifically states that routine/prophylactic pancreatectomy is not recommended.

Therefore:

> **More extensive lymph-node dissection does not automatically mean removing adjacent organs.**

Adjacent-organ resection is a separate question driven by tumor involvement and operative circumstances.

---

# 23. Curative Surgery and Lymphadenectomy

In curative-intent gastric surgery, regional lymphadenectomy is generally integrated into the oncologic operation.

NCI describes surgical resection with regional lymphadenectomy as the treatment of choice for appropriate stage II and III gastric cancer.

For stage I disease, NCI likewise describes regional lymphadenectomy as part of surgical resection.

The patient-facing message is:

> **When gastric cancer is being surgically treated with curative intent, lymph-node management is part of the cancer-control and staging strategy.**

---

# 24. Palliative Surgery Is Different

Lymphadenectomy should not be assumed to be necessary whenever a gastric operation is performed.

NCCN states that when gastric resection is performed for palliation in incurable disease, lymph-node dissection does not need to be included.

The purpose of such surgery may be symptom relief, such as management of obstruction or uncontrollable bleeding.

Therefore:

**Curative gastric surgery**

→ oncologic resection + appropriate regional lymphadenectomy.

**Selected palliative gastric surgery**

→ symptom relief; lymphadenectomy may not be required.

This distinction prevents the package from overgeneralizing curative surgical principles.

---

# 25. What Does “Skip Metastasis” Mean?

The supplied Vietnamese guideline notes that gastric cancer can show skip nodal metastasis.

This means cancer may occasionally be detected in a more distant regional nodal group without the expected pattern of involvement in an earlier nodal level.

This is one reason why simplistic assumptions such as:

> “If the nearest nodes are negative, more distant regional nodes cannot be involved”

are unsafe.

It also helps explain why lymphadenectomy classifications exist.

---

# 26. Lymphadenectomy and Pathology

After surgical removal, lymph nodes are examined by pathology.

The pathology report can provide information about:

- how many nodes were examined;
- how many contained metastatic cancer;
- the resulting regional nodal category;
- relationships to the overall pathological stage.

PP-0199 explains this pathway conceptually.

Detailed pathology-report interpretation belongs to the pathology and staging packages.

---

# 27. What Is a “Positive” Lymph Node?

A positive lymph node is a regional lymph node in which metastatic gastric cancer is identified.

The number of positive nodes contributes to the N category.

It is important to distinguish:

**positive node**

from

**node examined**

from

**nodal station**

from

**extent of lymphadenectomy**.

These terms answer different questions.

---

# 28. Can a Patient Have N0 Disease After Lymphadenectomy?

Yes.

If the examined regional nodes do not contain metastatic cancer, the regional nodal category may be N0.

That does not mean the primary tumor was harmless.

It also does not mean recurrence is impossible.

N0 is one component of the overall staging assessment.

---

# 29. Why Can Node Count Affect Confidence in Staging?

A larger and appropriate nodal assessment can provide more information about regional disease than an inadequate assessment.

This is one reason NCCN uses an examined-node goal.

However:

> **Node count should not be interpreted in isolation.**

The quality of the surgery, anatomical extent, pathology processing, tumor location, treatment context and other disease factors all matter.

---

# 30. D1 and D2 Are Not Simply “Small” and “Large”

This is another important misconception.

D1 and D2 are defined by **which anatomical nodal territories are included**, not simply by the number of lymph nodes removed.

A D2 dissection may retrieve more nodes, but the defining concept is the greater anatomical extent.

This is why:

> **Extent ≠ yield.**

---

# 31. D1 and D2 Are Not Independent of the Gastric Operation

The appropriate nodal field is linked to the gastric resection.

Tumor location and the planned gastric resection influence which nodal territories are relevant.

This is why detailed D1 and D2 packages must be interpreted together with the gastrectomy packages.

PP-0199 therefore serves as the bridge:

**Gastrectomy**

→ **regional lymphadenectomy**

→ **D1/D2 extent**

→ **pathologic nodal assessment**

---

# 32. Lymphadenectomy and Early Gastric Cancer

Early gastric cancer does not automatically mean “no lymph-node surgery.”

The supplied Vietnamese guideline describes selected T1 lesions for which D1 or D1+ may be considered when endoscopic resection is not appropriate.

This demonstrates an important principle:

> The need for lymphadenectomy depends on the estimated risk of lymph-node involvement and the overall surgical context, not simply on the word “early.”

Detailed endoscopic criteria are delegated to PP-0193–0195.

---

# 33. Lymphadenectomy After EMR/ESD

Endoscopic resection can be appropriate for selected early gastric cancers.

When endoscopic treatment is not considered oncologically adequate, surgical treatment may be required.

The lymphadenectomy question then becomes part of the surgical strategy.

PP-0199 does not determine when EMR or ESD is appropriate.

Those topics belong to:

- PP-0193 — Endoscopic Resection for Early Gastric Cancer;
- PP-0194 — EMR;
- PP-0195 — ESD.

---

# 34. Lymphadenectomy and Neoadjuvant Therapy

Some patients receive systemic or combined treatment before surgery.

The presence of preoperative treatment does not eliminate the conceptual role of lymph-node surgery in an appropriately resectable surgical pathway.

However, the exact treatment sequence is owned by the perioperative/neoadjuvant packages.

PP-0199 should therefore describe the surgical-pathology interface without prescribing a treatment sequence.

---

# 35. Lymphadenectomy and Adjuvant Therapy

Pathologic nodal findings can influence postoperative treatment planning.

However, PP-0199 should not convert nodal findings into a chemotherapy prescription.

The package stops at:

**nodal findings**

→ **staging information**

→ **clinical relevance**

and delegates actual treatment selection to downstream treatment packages.

---

# 36. Lymphadenectomy and Prognosis

Regional lymph-node involvement is an important prognostic factor.

However, lymphadenectomy itself should not be treated as a standalone prognostic test.

The patient's prognosis depends on the complete disease context, including:

- primary tumor characteristics;
- nodal status;
- distant disease;
- tumor biology;
- treatment;
- response;
- overall health.

PP-0199 therefore explains the prognostic relevance of nodal information without producing individualized survival estimates.

---

# 37. Patient Scenario 1 — “My surgeon said D2. What does that mean?”

A D2 lymphadenectomy means a more extensive regional lymph-node dissection than D1.

NCCN describes D2 as including D1 plus additional nodal groups along major arterial territories.

The exact nodal stations and operative technique are covered by PP-0201.

---

# 38. Patient Scenario 2 — “My report says 22 lymph nodes were examined. Does that mean I had D2?”

Not necessarily.

The number of lymph nodes examined is different from the anatomical extent of dissection.

A node count does not by itself establish D1 or D2.

---

# 39. Patient Scenario 3 — “My report says 4 lymph nodes were positive. Is that the same as removing 4 nodes?”

No.

Four positive nodes means four of the examined nodes contained cancer.

Many more nodes may have been examined and found to be negative.

The number examined and number positive are separate pathology findings.

---

# 40. Patient Scenario 4 — “My surgeon removed 20 nodes. Does that mean my cancer was advanced?”

No.

The number of nodes removed is not a stage by itself.

Stage is determined from the complete TNM and pathological assessment.

---

# 41. Patient Scenario 5 — “Why are they removing lymph nodes if the scan did not show cancer in them?”

Imaging cannot always identify microscopic nodal disease.

Surgical removal and pathological examination provide direct tissue information.

This is one reason regional lymphadenectomy is integrated into curative gastric surgery.

---

# 42. Patient Scenario 6 — “If my lymph nodes are negative, was the lymphadenectomy unnecessary?”

No.

The absence of cancer in the nodes is itself clinically useful information.

The operation provided pathological assessment of regional disease.

---

# 43. Patient Scenario 7 — “If my cancer is small, do I need lymph-node removal?”

Not necessarily the same extent for every patient.

Selected very early cancers may be treated endoscopically, while other T1 lesions may require surgical resection with a defined lymphadenectomy.

The decision depends on the lesion's characteristics and the treatment pathway.

---

# 44. Patient Scenario 8 — “Why can't the surgeon just remove the one suspicious node?”

Because gastric cancer can spread to multiple regional nodal territories, and skip metastasis can occur.

The appropriate surgical field is therefore determined by the oncologic procedure rather than by one visible or suspicious node alone.

---

# 45. Patient Scenario 9 — “Does D2 guarantee that all cancer cells are removed?”

No.

D2 is a surgical extent of regional lymph-node dissection.

It does not guarantee that microscopic cancer cannot exist elsewhere or that recurrence cannot occur.

---

# 46. Patient Scenario 10 — “Does D2 guarantee better survival?”

No.

The evidence is context-dependent.

D2 may be appropriate and beneficial when performed correctly in selected patients, but more extensive surgery is not automatically better for every patient.

---

# 47. Patient Scenario 11 — “Why does the hospital's experience matter?”

Because D2 is technically demanding.

NCCN recommends that D2 be performed by experienced surgeons in appropriate centers.

The safety and quality of a complex operation depend partly on expertise and institutional experience.

---

# 48. Patient Scenario 12 — “Does D2 mean my spleen will be removed?”

No.

Routine splenectomy is not recommended.

Splenectomy may be considered in selected circumstances, such as tumor involvement or specific nodal situations.

---

# 49. Patient Scenario 13 — “Does D2 mean my pancreas will be removed?”

No.

Routine or prophylactic pancreatectomy is not recommended.

Removal of an adjacent organ is a separate issue related to tumor involvement and the specific operation.

---

# 50. Patient Scenario 14 — “Why is lymphadenectomy not always done in palliative surgery?”

Because palliative surgery has a different goal.

When surgery is being used to relieve symptoms in incurable disease, extensive oncologic lymph-node dissection may not contribute to the primary palliative objective.

NCCN explicitly states that palliative gastric resection does not need to include lymph-node dissection.

---

# 51. Patient Scenario 15 — “What should I look for in my pathology report?”

Useful questions include:

1. How many lymph nodes were examined?
2. How many were positive?
3. What is the reported N category?
4. What is the final pathological stage?
5. Was the intended lymphadenectomy completed?
6. Are there any comments about the adequacy of the specimen?

Detailed pathology interpretation should be performed with the treating team.

# 52. Common Misconceptions

## Myth 1 — “Lymphadenectomy removes all lymph nodes.”

**Fact:** Gastric-cancer lymphadenectomy refers to regional lymph nodes relevant to the gastric-cancer operation.

## Myth 2 — “D2 means every lymph node around the stomach.”

**Fact:** D2 is a defined anatomical extent, not unlimited node removal.

## Myth 3 — “D2 means 16 lymph nodes.”

**Fact:** D2 describes anatomical extent. The ≥16-node goal describes the number examined.

## Myth 4 — “16 nodes means the cancer was advanced.”

**Fact:** Node count is not a stage category.

## Myth 5 — “Positive nodes are the same as removed nodes.”

**Fact:** Positive nodes are the subset containing cancer.

## Myth 6 — “More nodes always means better survival.”

**Fact:** The benefit–risk balance depends on patient selection, surgical quality, evidence context and expertise.

## Myth 7 — “D2 always requires splenectomy.”

**Fact:** Routine splenectomy is not recommended.

## Myth 8 — “D2 always requires pancreatectomy.”

**Fact:** Routine prophylactic pancreatectomy is not recommended.

## Myth 9 — “Negative nodes mean the cancer is cured.”

**Fact:** Nodal negativity is a staging finding, not a guarantee of cure.

## Myth 10 — “Palliative surgery always needs lymphadenectomy.”

**Fact:** NCCN states that lymph-node dissection is not required for palliative gastric resection.

## Myth 11 — “D1 is simply a smaller D2.”

**Fact:** D1 and D2 are distinct anatomical extents.

## Myth 12 — “The number of nodes tells you exactly how extensive the surgery was.”

**Fact:** Node yield and anatomical extent are different dimensions.

# 53. Key Messages for Patients

1. Lymphadenectomy means removal of relevant regional lymph nodes.
2. It is an important component of curative gastric-cancer surgery.
3. It helps both with cancer control and pathological staging.
4. D1 and D2 describe different anatomical extents.
5. D1/D2 should not be confused with the number of nodes examined.
6. NCCN uses a goal of examining at least 16 lymph nodes in localized resectable disease.
7. The number of positive nodes contributes to N staging.
8. More extensive surgery is not automatically better.
9. D2 requires appropriate expertise and experience.
10. D2 does not automatically mean spleen or pancreas removal.
11. Curative and palliative operations have different lymphadenectomy goals.
12. A pathology report should be interpreted as part of the complete staging picture.

# 54. Patient Questions to Ask the Surgical Team

### About the operation

- What type of gastrectomy am I having?
- What extent of lymphadenectomy is planned?
- Why is this extent appropriate for my tumor?

### About expertise

- How frequently does the surgical team perform gastric-cancer surgery?
- How frequently is D2 lymphadenectomy performed at this center?

### About pathology

- How many lymph nodes were examined?
- How many were positive?
- What is my N category?
- What is my final pathological stage?

### About adjacent organs

- Is there any reason my spleen might need to be removed?
- Is there any evidence that another organ is involved?

### About the treatment plan

- How will the lymph-node findings affect the next treatment discussion?
- Will the pathology results change whether additional treatment is recommended?
