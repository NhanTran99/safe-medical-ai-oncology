# Educational Objectives

After reading this Population Package, the reader should be able to:

1. Explain what adjuvant therapy means after definitive gastric-cancer surgery.
2. Understand why treatment may be recommended even when the visible tumor has been removed.
3. Distinguish adjuvant therapy from perioperative therapy and neoadjuvant therapy.
4. Understand why postoperative treatment decisions depend on pathology and surgical findings.
5. Recognize the importance of pathologic stage, nodal status, surgical margins, extent of lymphadenectomy, and previous systemic treatment.
6. Understand why postoperative chemotherapy is a major adjuvant modality in selected patients.
7. Understand the evidence supporting postoperative chemotherapy after D2 gastrectomy.
8. Understand the clinical meaning of the CLASSIC trial without treating its results as an individual prediction.
9. Understand the evidence for S-1 after D2 gastrectomy in the ACTS-GC trial.
10. Understand why postoperative chemoradiation is a related but distinct modality.
11. Understand why the interpretation of postoperative chemoradiation depends on prior treatment and the extent of lymphadenectomy.
12. Understand why patients who already received preoperative/perioperative systemic therapy are not necessarily in the same clinical situation as patients who underwent surgery first.
13. Understand that adjuvant therapy is intended to reduce recurrence risk rather than to prove that residual cancer is definitely present.
14. Understand why postoperative recovery, nutritional status, functional status, and treatment tolerance matter.
15. Understand why planned adjuvant therapy may be delayed, modified, or not completed.
16. Distinguish adjuvant therapy from treatment of established recurrent or metastatic disease.
17. Understand the boundaries between adjuvant strategy, individual chemotherapy regimens, chemoradiation, immunotherapy, targeted therapy, surgery, and surveillance.
18. Ask informed questions about postoperative treatment without interpreting population-level evidence as a personal prescription.

# Clinical Knowledge Blocks

## Block 1 — What Is Adjuvant Therapy?

Adjuvant therapy is treatment given after definitive local treatment, usually surgery, with the goal of lowering the risk that gastric cancer will return.

The key concept is **risk reduction**.

A patient may have no visible cancer after surgery and still have a meaningful risk that microscopic cancer cells remain somewhere in the body. These cells may be too small to be seen on routine imaging or to be removed surgically.

Adjuvant treatment is intended to address that risk.

It should not be explained as proof that the doctors know cancer is still present.

### Patient-facing message

> “The operation may remove all cancer that can be seen. Additional treatment may be recommended because there can still be a risk from cancer cells that are too small to detect.”

The NCI patient-facing treatment material describes treatment after surgery that lowers the chance of recurrence as **adjuvant therapy** and explains that chemotherapy, radiation therapy, or both may be used in selected patients. [Source: `17. Treatment of Stomach Cancer - NCI.pdf`]

## Block 2 — Why Adjuvant Therapy Is Not Automatic for Everyone

The need for postoperative treatment depends on the risk profile and the treatment pathway.

Factors that can influence postoperative management include:

- pathologic tumor stage;
- lymph-node involvement;
- margin status;
- extent of lymph-node dissection;
- whether systemic treatment was given before surgery;
- patient fitness and recovery;
- treatment tolerance.

Therefore:

> “I had surgery” does not by itself answer the question “Do I need adjuvant therapy?”

The current NCCN source set explicitly frames postoperative management around pathologic stage, nodal status, surgical margins, lymphadenectomy extent, and previous treatment.

## Block 3 — The Difference Between Adjuvant and Perioperative Therapy

### Perioperative therapy

Treatment is deliberately organized around surgery:

**systemic therapy before surgery → surgery → postoperative systemic therapy**

### Adjuvant therapy

Treatment begins after definitive surgery:

**surgery → postoperative treatment**

These concepts overlap at the postoperative time point but are not identical.

If a patient received systemic treatment before surgery, the postoperative treatment may be the continuation of the original perioperative strategy.

NCCN states that after R0 resection, patients who received systemic therapy before surgery should receive postoperative systemic therapy as part of the perioperative approach.

Therefore:

> postoperative treatment does not always mean a completely new adjuvant treatment has been started.

## Block 4 — Why the Surgical Pathology Report Matters

The surgical specimen provides information that can change the postoperative treatment plan.

Important elements include:

- how deeply the tumor invaded;
- whether lymph nodes contain cancer;
- whether the surgical margins are free of tumor;
- how extensive the lymph-node dissection was.

These findings help estimate recurrence risk and determine which postoperative strategies are appropriate.

This package uses those findings as **decision inputs**.

It does not replace the pathology package or provide a full pathology curriculum.

## Block 5 — Why Lymph-Node Status Matters

Gastric cancer can spread through regional lymphatic pathways.

If lymph nodes contain cancer, recurrence risk is generally higher than in comparable node-negative disease.

This is one reason postoperative systemic therapy is important in selected stage II/III populations.

The supplied NCCN and NCI materials specifically use nodal status in postoperative treatment decisions.

## Block 6 — Why D2 Lymphadenectomy Matters

The extent of lymph-node dissection can affect how postoperative treatment is interpreted.

The current NCCN framework distinguishes patients who underwent:

- primary D2 lymph-node dissection;
- less-than-D2 lymphadenectomy.

For appropriate patients after primary D2 dissection, postoperative chemotherapy is recommended.

When the lymphadenectomy was less extensive, postoperative chemoradiation has a more important role in selected settings.

This does **not** mean that D2 itself is a chemotherapy decision.

It means that:

> **the quality and extent of local surgery are part of the context in which adjuvant treatment evidence is interpreted.**

Detailed D2 technique belongs to PP-0201.

## Block 7 — Why Margin Status Matters

### R0 resection

No residual tumor is identified at the resection margin.

Postoperative management then follows the appropriate risk- and treatment-pathway framework.

### R1/R2

Residual disease is present at the microscopic or macroscopic level.

This is not the same clinical question as routine adjuvant therapy after an uncomplicated R0 resection.

The current NCCN source set identifies postoperative chemoradiation and possible re-resection considerations in selected margin-positive situations.

Therefore:

> **adjuvant therapy should not be presented as a universal substitute for management of known residual disease.**

Detailed R1/R2 management is outside this package.

## Block 8 — Postoperative Chemotherapy

Postoperative chemotherapy is a core adjuvant modality for selected patients.

The strongest directly relevant evidence in the supplied source set includes:

- S-1 / ACTS-GC;
- capecitabine plus oxaliplatin / CLASSIC.

These trials demonstrate that postoperative systemic treatment can improve clinically meaningful outcomes in appropriately selected resected gastric-cancer populations.

The package does not imply that every patient should receive the same regimen.

## Block 9 — CLASSIC Trial

The CLASSIC trial randomized 1,035 patients with stage IIA, IIB, IIIA, or IIIB gastric cancer after curative D2 gastrectomy to postoperative capecitabine plus oxaliplatin or follow-up alone.

The NCI PDQ reports:

- 3-year disease-free survival: 74% versus 59%;
- HR 0.56;
- 95% CI 0.44–0.72;
- P < .0001;
- 3-year overall survival: 83% versus 78%;
- HR 0.72;
- 95% CI 0.52–1.00;
- P = .0493.

This evidence supports postoperative chemotherapy after appropriate D2 surgery.

### Patient-facing interpretation

> “In a large clinical trial of patients who had D2 surgery, postoperative chemotherapy improved disease-free survival and also showed an overall-survival benefit compared with surgery and follow-up alone.”

The result is a population-level estimate.

It is not a promise about an individual patient's outcome.

## Block 10 — S-1 / ACTS-GC Trial

The NCI PDQ reports that 1,059 patients with stage II or III gastric cancer who underwent D2 gastrectomy were randomized to one year of S-1 or surgery alone.

The reported 3-year overall survival was:

- 80.1% with S-1;
- 70.1% with surgery alone;

with:

- HR 0.68;
- 95% CI 0.52–0.87;
- P = .003.

This provides an additional randomized evidence base for postoperative chemotherapy after D2 surgery.

### Why this matters

The evidence base is not tied to a single regimen.

The choice of regimen depends on:

- clinical setting;
- patient factors;
- regional practice;
- prior treatment;
- guideline recommendations;
- expected benefit and toxicity.

## Block 11 — Adjuvant Chemoradiation Is Related but Not Identical to Adjuvant Chemotherapy

Postoperative chemoradiation combines systemic treatment with radiation.

The NCI PDQ describes the SWOG-9008/INT-0116 trial, in which postoperative chemotherapy plus concurrent radiation improved survival and relapse-free survival compared with surgery alone.

The NCI reports:

- median OS 35 months with postoperative chemoradiation;
- 27 months with surgery alone;
- median relapse-free survival 27 versus 19 months.

However, interpretation has to account for the surgical and treatment context.

Therefore PP-0205 should explain:

> postoperative chemoradiation is an adjuvant modality with its own evidence base.

Detailed chemoradiation belongs to PP-0206.

## Block 12 — Why Prior Preoperative Therapy Changes the Question

The CRITICS trial is important because patients received preoperative chemotherapy before surgery and were then randomized to postoperative chemotherapy or chemoradiotherapy.

NCI reports no survival advantage from postoperative chemoradiotherapy over postoperative chemotherapy in this setting.

This illustrates a central principle:

> **The postoperative decision depends not only on what surgery was performed, but also on what treatment the patient received before surgery.**

This is one reason adjuvant therapy cannot be reduced to a single universal algorithm.

## Block 13 — ARTIST and ARTIST II

The supplied NCCN material summarizes randomized evidence from ARTIST and ARTIST II.

The overall lesson is that after adequate D2 surgery, adding radiation to postoperative chemotherapy has not established a routine survival benefit sufficient to make postoperative chemoradiation universally preferable to chemotherapy.

This supports the current guideline distinction between:

- adequate D2 surgery;
- less-than-D2 surgery;
- prior perioperative treatment.

The detailed radiation strategy remains PP-0206.

## Block 14 — Why Stage II/III Disease Is Important

The supplied NCI and Vietnamese clinical guideline materials identify stage II/III gastric cancer as an important setting for postoperative treatment.

The decision is still individualized within the guideline framework.

The package should not convert “stage II/III” into:

> “every stage II/III patient must receive exactly the same adjuvant regimen.”

Instead:

> stage is a major risk-stratification input.

## Block 15 — Why Some Patients May Be Observed

Not every patient benefits from automatically adding systemic therapy.

Selected early-stage, node-negative patients with an appropriate R0 resection may be managed with observation according to the relevant guideline pathway.

Therefore:

> adjuvant therapy is **risk-adapted**, not simply “more treatment after surgery.”

## Block 16 — What Happens When FLOT Was Given Before Surgery?

FLOT is a specific regimen owned by PP-0204.

If a patient received perioperative FLOT before surgery, postoperative FLOT may represent the postoperative part of the same perioperative strategy.

This should not be represented as:

> “The patient finished preoperative FLOT and now starts a completely unrelated adjuvant regimen.”

Instead:

**perioperative treatment plan**
→ preoperative component
→ surgery
→ postoperative component.

PP-0205 owns the strategy-level concept.

PP-0204 owns FLOT regimen details.

## Block 17 — Postoperative Recovery Matters

Surgery changes the patient's physical condition.

Before postoperative systemic treatment, clinicians may consider:

- recovery from surgery;
- postoperative complications;
- nutritional status;
- functional status;
- blood counts;
- organ function;
- performance status;
- ability to tolerate treatment.

These are clinical readiness concepts.

This package does not define universal numerical thresholds.

## Block 18 — Nutrition Is Part of Treatment Readiness

The supplied Vietnamese guideline emphasizes nutritional support before and after surgery and during adjuvant treatment.

After gastrectomy, patients may have:

- reduced oral intake;
- weight loss;
- altered gastrointestinal function;
- nutritional deficits.

Therefore postoperative treatment planning may require nutritional assessment and support.

However:

> nutrition supports treatment readiness; it does not replace indicated anticancer treatment.

Detailed nutritional management belongs to supportive-care/nutrition packages.

## Block 19 — Why Adjuvant Treatment Can Be Difficult to Complete

Postoperative therapy may be harder to deliver than a planned treatment schedule suggests.

Possible reasons include:

- slow postoperative recovery;
- infection;
- wound or anastomotic complications;
- poor nutritional status;
- fatigue;
- hematologic toxicity;
- gastrointestinal toxicity;
- neuropathy;
- organ-function changes.

Treatment may therefore be:

- delayed;
- modified;
- interrupted;
- discontinued.

A treatment delay does not automatically mean that the treatment has failed.

## Block 20 — Benefit Versus Burden

Adjuvant therapy has potential benefits and harms.

### Potential benefit

- lower recurrence risk;
- improved disease-free survival;
- in selected evidence populations, improved overall survival.

### Potential burden

- treatment toxicity;
- additional hospital/clinic visits;
- recovery demands;
- nutritional and functional challenges;
- treatment interruptions;
- impact on quality of life.

The decision is therefore a benefit-risk assessment.

## Block 21 — Adjuvant Therapy Is Not Treatment of Visible Recurrence

Adjuvant therapy is given after definitive local treatment when recurrence has not been established.

If recurrence is documented, the clinical question changes.

Treatment then moves toward:

- recurrent disease management;
- metastatic systemic therapy;
- local treatment of selected recurrence;
- palliative care where appropriate.

PP-0207 owns palliative systemic therapy.

## Block 22 — Adjuvant Therapy Is Not Surveillance

### Adjuvant therapy

Treatment intended to reduce recurrence risk.

### Surveillance

Follow-up intended to monitor the patient after treatment.

A patient may receive both as parts of a broader cancer journey, but they are different clinical functions.

## Block 23 — Adjuvant Therapy Is Not a Biomarker Test

Biomarkers may influence treatment selection in the overall gastric-cancer treatment pathway.

However:

- HER2 testing;
- PD-L1 testing;
- MSI-H/dMMR testing;
- CLDN18.2 testing;
- molecular testing;

are owned by dedicated packages.

PP-0205 may mention biomarker-directed treatment as a boundary/interface only.

## Block 24 — Adjuvant Therapy Is Not a Specific Chemotherapy Regimen

This distinction is essential.

### Adjuvant therapy

A **clinical strategy** after surgery.

### CAPOX

A **specific chemotherapy regimen**.

### S-1

A **specific postoperative systemic regimen**.

### FLOT

A **specific perioperative chemotherapy regimen**.

PP-0205 must not become a regimen compendium.

## Block 25 — Patient Scenario: “My surgeon removed all the cancer. Why do I need more treatment?”

A useful answer is:

> “Surgery removes the cancer that can be identified and removed. Additional treatment may be recommended because microscopic cancer cells can remain even when scans and the operation do not show them. Adjuvant treatment is intended to reduce the chance of the cancer returning.”

## Block 26 — Patient Scenario: “Does adjuvant therapy mean my cancer is still there?”

Not necessarily.

Adjuvant therapy is usually given because there is a **risk** of microscopic residual disease, not because residual disease has been proven.

## Block 27 — Patient Scenario: “I had chemotherapy before surgery. Do I need chemotherapy again?”

The answer depends on the treatment strategy.

If the patient received perioperative chemotherapy, postoperative treatment may be the planned continuation of that strategy.

The treating team will consider:

- what regimen was already given;
- how much treatment was delivered;
- surgical findings;
- recovery;
- current guideline pathway.

## Block 28 — Patient Scenario: “Why does D2 surgery matter?”

Because the extent of lymph-node removal affects how evidence for postoperative treatment is interpreted.

After an adequate D2 dissection, postoperative chemotherapy has strong evidence in selected patients.

After less extensive lymph-node surgery, postoperative chemoradiation may have a different role.

## Block 29 — Patient Scenario: “If I have no positive lymph nodes, do I still need treatment?”

Not automatically, and not automatically never.

The decision depends on the complete pathology and treatment context.

## Block 30 — Patient Scenario: “If I cannot tolerate chemotherapy, has the treatment failed?”

Not necessarily.

Treatment may be modified or stopped when the risks become too high.

The clinical team evaluates the total treatment course rather than defining success only by whether every planned dose was delivered.

# Common Misconceptions

## Myth 1 — “Adjuvant therapy means cancer is definitely left behind.”

**Fact:** It is treatment intended to reduce the risk from microscopic disease that may not be detectable.

## Myth 2 — “Everyone needs adjuvant chemotherapy after gastrectomy.”

**Fact:** Postoperative treatment is risk- and context-dependent.

## Myth 3 — “Adjuvant therapy is the same as FLOT.”

**Fact:** Adjuvant therapy is a treatment strategy; FLOT is a specific chemotherapy regimen used in a perioperative strategy.

## Myth 4 — “Postoperative chemotherapy and chemoradiation are interchangeable.”

**Fact:** They are different modalities with different evidence and different clinical contexts.

## Myth 5 — “D2 surgery means no further treatment is needed.”

**Fact:** D2 surgery provides important local/regional treatment and staging information, but selected patients still benefit from postoperative systemic therapy.

## Myth 6 — “A postoperative treatment delay means the cancer has progressed.”

**Fact:** Delays can be used to allow recovery or manage toxicity.

## Myth 7 — “Completing every planned cycle guarantees cure.”

**Fact:** Treatment completion is not the same as cure.

## Myth 8 — “If surgery achieved R0, postoperative treatment is never needed.”

**Fact:** R0 status is important, but pathologic stage, nodes, prior treatment, and other factors still influence postoperative management.

# Extended Clinical Reasoning Layer

## 31. The Central Clinical Logic of Adjuvant Therapy

Adjuvant therapy is easiest to understand as a sequence of decisions rather than as a drug list.

The sequence is:

**Definitive local treatment**

↓

**Pathologic and surgical risk assessment**

↓

**Review of previous systemic treatment**

↓

**Estimate whether recurrence risk is high enough to justify additional treatment**

↓

**Select the appropriate postoperative strategy**

↓

**Assess treatment readiness**

↓

**Deliver, adapt, or discontinue treatment as clinically appropriate**

↓

**Transition to follow-up/surveillance**

This sequence is the conceptual backbone of PP-0205.

## 32. Why “After Surgery” Is Not a Sufficient Definition

A treatment can occur after surgery for several different reasons.

Examples include:

- adjuvant recurrence-risk reduction;
- continuation of perioperative therapy;
- treatment of known residual disease;
- treatment of recurrent disease;
- symptom control;
- treatment of a postoperative complication.

Therefore the phrase:

> “chemotherapy after surgery”

does not identify the clinical intent by itself.

PP-0205 exists partly to resolve this ambiguity.

## 33. Treatment Intent Must Be Preserved

### Curative-intent postoperative treatment

The aim is to reduce recurrence risk after definitive treatment.

### Treatment of residual disease

Cancer is known or strongly suspected to remain.

### Treatment of recurrent/metastatic disease

Cancer has returned or spread and requires a different treatment framework.

These are not interchangeable.

## 34. Why Adjuvant Treatment Can Be Appropriate After an R0 Resection

R0 means no residual tumor is identified at the surgical margin.

It does not mean:

> no microscopic cancer cell exists anywhere in the body.

The purpose of adjuvant treatment is therefore compatible with an R0 operation.

This is a key patient-facing concept.

## 35. Why R0 Does Not Automatically Mean “No Further Treatment”

The postoperative decision remains dependent on:

- tumor stage;
- lymph-node status;
- treatment received before surgery;
- lymphadenectomy extent;
- patient fitness.

Therefore:

> R0 is an important favorable surgical result, but it does not independently determine whether postoperative treatment is indicated.

## 36. Why R1/R2 Is Different

If tumor remains at the microscopic or macroscopic level, the treatment question changes.

The clinical team may consider:

- whether additional surgery is feasible;
- postoperative chemoradiation;
- systemic treatment;
- disease status elsewhere.

The package should not reduce this situation to:

> “standard adjuvant chemotherapy.”

## 37. Why the Pathology Report Is a Decision Document

The pathology report is not merely a historical record.

It may contain information that changes:

- recurrence-risk assessment;
- postoperative treatment;
- prognosis;
- eligibility for additional treatment.

This is why pathology review is a major transition point between surgery and adjuvant treatment.

## 38. Why Node-Positive Disease Is Clinically Important

Regional lymph-node involvement indicates that tumor has demonstrated the ability to spread beyond the primary site into regional lymphatic tissue.

This increases concern about occult systemic disease.

Therefore postoperative systemic treatment may be particularly relevant.

The package should not state that node-positive disease always guarantees recurrence.

## 39. Why Node-Negative Disease Does Not Automatically Eliminate Risk

Node-negative disease can still recur.

Risk can depend on:

- depth of invasion;
- tumor biology;
- margin status;
- other pathologic features;
- quality and extent of surgery.

Therefore node status is important but is not the entire decision.

## 40. Why D2 Evidence and Adjuvant Evidence Are Linked

The CLASSIC and ACTS-GC trials were conducted in populations that underwent D2 surgery.

This means the postoperative chemotherapy evidence is embedded in a surgical context.

It would be inappropriate to remove the D2 context from the evidence and assume identical results after every possible extent of lymphadenectomy.

## 41. Why “D2” Is Not a Chemotherapy Regimen

D2 describes the extent of lymph-node dissection.

It is:

- a surgical concept;
- a staging-quality concept;
- a local-treatment context.

It is not:

- chemotherapy;
- radiation;
- systemic treatment.

PP-0201 owns the surgical concept.

PP-0205 owns the treatment implication.

## 42. Why Less-than-D2 Changes the Evidence Context

Some of the historical chemoradiation evidence was generated in populations where lymphadenectomy was less extensive than modern D2 surgery.

Current guideline interpretation therefore considers lymphadenectomy extent when deciding whether postoperative chemoradiation is appropriate.

This is why old trial results cannot be applied without context.

## 43. Why Historical Trials Still Matter

Clinical trials do not become irrelevant simply because newer guidelines exist.

Historical trials can:

- establish that a treatment works in a defined population;
- explain why a modality entered practice;
- show which treatment contexts produce benefit;
- identify limitations of older strategies.

The guideline then integrates that evidence with newer evidence and current standards.

## 44. Why Trial Date and Current Guideline Position Must Be Distinguished

A trial may be decades old while its evidence remains relevant to a specific question.

However:

> a historical trial does not automatically define current practice.

PP-0205 therefore separates:

**randomized evidence**

from

**current guideline positioning**.

## 45. Why CLASSIC Remains Important

CLASSIC directly tested postoperative chemotherapy after D2 surgery.

Its design therefore aligns closely with the postoperative chemotherapy question owned by PP-0205.

The result is not merely historical background.

It provides a clear evidence anchor for postoperative chemotherapy.

## 46. Why ACTS-GC Remains Important

ACTS-GC demonstrates postoperative benefit in a large Asian population after D2 surgery.

It is important because gastric-cancer treatment evidence is influenced by:

- geography;
- surgical practice;
- patient population;
- regimen availability.

The evidence therefore should not be reduced to a single Western regimen model.

## 47. Why FLOT4 Is Not an Adjuvant-Only Trial

FLOT4 evaluated perioperative treatment.

The treatment strategy included therapy before and after surgery.

Therefore FLOT4 is evidence for:

> **perioperative chemotherapy**

rather than a pure trial of:

> **newly initiated postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy after upfront surgery**.

This distinction protects PP-0205 from absorbing PP-0203 and PP-0204.

## 48. Why CRITICS Is Especially Valuable for PP-0205

CRITICS asks a postoperative question in patients who had already received preoperative chemotherapy.

That makes it an important bridge between:

- perioperative treatment;
- postoperative treatment;
- chemoradiation.

Its result illustrates why previous treatment must be included in postoperative decision-making.

## 49. Why INT-0116 Must Be Interpreted in Its Historical Context

INT-0116 demonstrated benefit from postoperative chemoradiation.

However, surgical extent and the role of D2 surgery have evolved.

Therefore:

> the trial supports the efficacy of postoperative chemoradiation in its studied setting, but the result cannot be transformed into a universal recommendation for all contemporary patients.

## 50. Why ARTIST and ARTIST II Matter

ARTIST and ARTIST II help answer a modernized question:

> after adequate D2 surgery and postoperative chemotherapy, does adding radiation routinely improve outcomes?

The evidence did not establish a universal survival advantage.

This helps explain the current guideline emphasis on clinical context.

## 51. Why the Package Needs Both Positive and Negative Evidence

A high-quality evidence package cannot list only trials that support treatment.

It must also explain evidence that limits or refines the treatment indication.

Therefore PP-0205 includes:

- CLASSIC;
- ACTS-GC;
- INT-0116;
- CRITICS;
- ARTIST/ARTIST II.

This creates a balanced evidence architecture.

## 52. Why “More Treatment” Is Not a Valid Clinical Principle

A patient may understandably think:

> “If some chemotherapy is good, more chemotherapy must be better.”

This is not a safe rule.

Additional treatment can create:

- toxicity;
- delays;
- reduced functional recovery;
- reduced quality of life.

Treatment should therefore be selected according to evidence and risk, not by maximizing treatment intensity.

## 53. Why Postoperative Recovery Is Part of Treatment Selection

The patient's condition after surgery can be very different from their condition at diagnosis.

They may have:

- weight loss;
- anemia;
- fatigue;
- altered eating;
- dehydration;
- infection;
- reduced performance status.

These factors can influence whether treatment can be delivered safely.

## 54. Why Nutritional Recovery Is Clinically Relevant

Gastrectomy can alter:

- meal size;
- digestion;
- absorption;
- eating patterns.

Nutritional compromise can make systemic therapy harder to tolerate.

The supplied Vietnamese guideline emphasizes nutritional support around surgery and during adjuvant treatment.

PP-0205 therefore includes nutritional readiness conceptually.

## 55. Why Nutritional Support Does Not Replace Cancer Treatment

Nutrition can help the patient:

- recover;
- maintain strength;
- tolerate treatment.

But it is not itself a substitute for indicated anticancer therapy.

This distinction is important for patient safety.

## 56. Why Functional Status Matters

ESMO-ASCO identifies functional status as one factor guiding gastric-cancer treatment strategy.

A patient's ability to:

- walk;
- eat;
- perform daily activities;
- recover from surgery;

can influence treatment feasibility.

The package intentionally avoids assigning a universal performance-status cutoff.

## 57. Why Age Alone Should Not Define Adjuvant Eligibility

Chronological age is not equivalent to treatment fitness.

Two patients of the same age can have very different:

- functional status;
- comorbidity burden;
- nutritional status;
- organ function;
- postoperative recovery.

Therefore age alone should not be used as a universal rule.

## 58. Why Comorbidities Matter

Postoperative treatment can interact with:

- renal dysfunction;
- hepatic dysfunction;
- cardiovascular disease;
- diabetes;
- neuropathy;
- frailty.

These considerations affect regimen selection and treatment tolerance.

Detailed individualized selection is outside PP-0205.

## 59. Why Treatment Readiness Is a Clinical Assessment

Treatment readiness is not a single laboratory value.

It is an overall assessment of:

- recovery;
- functional status;
- nutritional condition;
- blood counts;
- organ function;
- postoperative complications;
- expected toxicity.

This is why a simple calendar rule is unsafe.

## 60. Why Treatment May Be Delayed

Treatment can be postponed because of:

- infection;
- inadequate blood-count recovery;
- postoperative complications;
- poor oral intake;
- dehydration;
- organ-function changes.

A delay can therefore be a safety intervention.

## 61. Why Treatment May Be Modified

Dose or schedule modification may be necessary when:

- toxicity becomes clinically significant;
- recovery is incomplete;
- organ function changes;
- cumulative toxicity develops.

PP-0205 establishes this concept without supplying universal modification tables.

## 62. Why Treatment May Be Discontinued

Stopping treatment can sometimes be appropriate when:

- risks outweigh expected benefits;
- toxicity is unacceptable;
- disease status changes;
- patient goals change.

Discontinuation does not automatically mean treatment has failed.

## 63. Why Planned Treatment and Delivered Treatment Are Different

A trial protocol describes what participants were assigned to receive.

A real patient may receive:

- fewer cycles;
- delayed cycles;
- modified doses;
- a different regimen;
- no postoperative therapy.

Therefore trial protocol ≠ guaranteed clinical delivery.

## 64. Why Treatment Completion Should Not Be Used as the Sole Definition of Success

Clinical outcomes include:

- recurrence;
- disease-free survival;
- overall survival;
- quality of life;
- treatment-related harm.

Completing every planned dose is a treatment-delivery outcome, not a biological endpoint.

## 65. Why Adjuvant Therapy Can Still Be Beneficial Even If Treatment Is Modified

Clinical treatment is not always all-or-none.

A patient who requires modifications may still receive clinically meaningful therapy.

The package should avoid telling patients:

> “If you miss one cycle, the treatment no longer works.”

Such a statement is unsupported by the supplied sources.

## 66. Why the Benefit-Risk Balance Is Central

The expected benefit must be weighed against:

- toxicity;
- functional burden;
- postoperative recovery;
- patient preferences;
- alternative strategies.

This is particularly important because the goal is risk reduction rather than treatment of known gross disease.

## 67. Why Shared Decision-Making Matters

The patient should understand:

- why treatment is being considered;
- what evidence supports it;
- what the main risks are;
- what alternatives exist;
- how prior treatment changes the plan.

This is consistent with the ESMO-ASCO multidisciplinary treatment framework.

## 68. Why Multidisciplinary Review Can Be Important

Gastric-cancer treatment can involve:

- surgeons;
- medical oncologists;
- radiation oncologists;
- gastroenterologists;
- pathologists;
- radiologists;
- nutrition/supportive-care professionals.

The postoperative strategy may require integration of:

- operative findings;
- pathology;
- prior treatment;
- current patient condition.

## 69. Why Adjuvant Therapy Is Not a Standalone Decision

The decision is embedded in the entire treatment journey:

**diagnosis**

→ **staging**

→ **preoperative/perioperative strategy when applicable**

→ **surgery**

→ **pathology**

→ **postoperative treatment**

→ **surveillance**

Therefore PP-0205 must connect to neighboring PPs.

## 70. Why This Package Is Atomic Despite Having Many Evidence Blocks

The package has one central question:

> **What is postoperative/adjuvant therapy and how is it selected after surgery?**

The evidence blocks are subcomponents of that single question.

They do not create separate packages for:

- CLASSIC;
- S-1;
- INT-0116;
- CRITICS.

Those are evidence nodes supporting the same atomic educational question.

# Patient-Facing Deep Scenarios

## Scenario 11 — “My report says R0. Why is chemotherapy still being discussed?”

R0 means no residual tumor was identified at the surgical margin.

The postoperative decision may still depend on:

- stage;
- lymph nodes;
- previous treatment;
- lymphadenectomy extent.

R0 does not mean recurrence risk is zero.

## Scenario 12 — “My lymph nodes were negative. Does that mean I am cured?”

Negative lymph nodes are favorable information, but they do not by themselves guarantee cure.

Other tumor and treatment factors remain relevant.

## Scenario 13 — “Why does my doctor ask whether I had chemotherapy before surgery?”

Because postoperative treatment is interpreted differently when systemic therapy was already given before surgery.

The postoperative therapy may be a continuation of a perioperative plan.

## Scenario 14 — “Why does the surgeon's lymph-node operation affect chemotherapy?”

Because the evidence for postoperative treatment was generated in particular surgical contexts, especially D2 surgery.

The extent of lymph-node dissection therefore forms part of the treatment context.

## Scenario 15 — “Why might radiation be considered if fewer lymph nodes were removed?”

Less extensive lymph-node surgery can leave greater uncertainty about regional disease control.

Current guideline pathways therefore give postoperative chemoradiation a different role in selected patients after less-than-D2 surgery.

## Scenario 16 — “If radiation helped in INT-0116, why don't all patients receive it?”

Because later evidence and modern surgical practice changed how the result is interpreted.

The benefit of postoperative chemoradiation is context-dependent, particularly after adequate D2 surgery and after prior chemotherapy.

## Scenario 17 — “If CLASSIC was positive, why isn't CAPOX automatically given to everyone?”

Because the trial studied a defined population:

- stage IIA–IIIB;
- curative D2 surgery;
- specific regimen.

Current treatment must account for the individual patient and current guideline context.

## Scenario 18 — “If I already had FLOT before surgery, why is postoperative treatment called adjuvant?”

Terminology can overlap at the postoperative time point.

In a perioperative plan, postoperative FLOT is the postoperative component of that overall strategy.

PP-0205 focuses on the postoperative strategy; PP-0204 owns the FLOT regimen.

## Scenario 19 — “If I cannot finish chemotherapy, is my surgery wasted?”

No.

Surgery remains a major component of curative-intent treatment.

Postoperative treatment is an additional strategy intended to reduce recurrence risk.

Failure to complete all planned treatment does not erase the benefit of surgery.

## Scenario 20 — “Can I decide based only on the stage?”

Stage is important, but not the only factor.

The postoperative decision may also depend on:

- nodes;
- margins;
- lymphadenectomy extent;
- previous therapy;
- functional status;
- nutritional status;
- treatment tolerance.

# Clinical Reasoning Summary

The correct mental model is:

**Adjuvant therapy is not a drug.**

It is a **postoperative treatment strategy**.

Its purpose is:

**reduce recurrence risk**

not:

**treat proven visible recurrence**.

Its decision inputs include:

**pathology + nodes + margins + lymphadenectomy + previous treatment + patient fitness**

Its main evidence anchors include:

**CLASSIC + ACTS-GC**

with related evidence from:

**INT-0116 + CRITICS + ARTIST/ARTIST II**.

Its neighboring strategy nodes are:

**perioperative chemotherapy**

and

**chemoradiation**.

Its downstream state is:

**surveillance / recurrence management**.

1. Never define adjuvant therapy as a single drug.

3. Never ignore previous preoperative treatment.
