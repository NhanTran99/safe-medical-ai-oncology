## Educational Objectives

After reading this Population Package, the reader should be able to:

1. Explain what changes clinically once recurrent gastric cancer has been established.
2. Understand why recurrence management is not determined by the word “recurrence” alone.
3. Recognize the importance of recurrence location, extent, resectability and medical fitness.
4. Understand the distinction between potentially curative/local-control management and palliative/disease-control management.
5. Understand the strategic roles of surgery, systemic therapy, selected chemoradiation, clinical trials and supportive/palliative care.
6. Understand why previous treatment, performance status, comorbidities and patient goals influence management.
7. Understand why biomarker information may be relevant to treatment selection.
8. Understand why management may change after treatment response, progression, toxicity or a change in patient priorities.
9. Recognize the boundaries between this package and specialized downstream Population Packages.
10. Interpret the management framework without treating it as individualized medical advice.

## Clinical Knowledge Blocks

### Block 1 — What changes when recurrence is established?

Recurrence detection answers whether cancer has returned and where it appears to be present. Management begins after that clinical problem has been established.

The management question is not simply “Which drug should be used?”

The first question is:

> **What is the most appropriate overall treatment strategy for this pattern of recurrent disease and this patient?**

This distinction protects PP-0221 Recurrence Detection from being absorbed into PP-0222.

### Block 2 — Why recurrence is heterogeneous

Recurrent gastric cancer can present in different anatomical and clinical patterns.

Examples include:

- isolated locoregional recurrence;
- unresectable local recurrence;
- metastatic recurrence;
- peritoneal recurrence;
- combinations of these patterns.

These patterns can lead to substantially different management considerations.

Therefore the word “recurrent” is not, by itself, a treatment algorithm.

### Block 3 — Treatment intent

Management should first clarify the intended goal.

Possible goals include:

- potentially curative/local-control treatment in selected localized disease;
- disease control;
- prolongation of life when meaningful benefit is expected;
- symptom relief;
- preservation of function;
- preservation of quality of life;
- supportive care when disease-directed treatment is unsuitable.

A non-curative treatment intent does not mean that treatment is futile.

### Block 4 — Isolated locoregional recurrence

NCCN identifies isolated locoregional recurrence as a setting in which surgery may be considered when disease is resectable and the patient is medically fit.

The key concepts are:

1. Is the recurrence localized?
2. Is it potentially resectable?
3. Is the patient medically fit for the proposed treatment?
4. Has the case been reviewed appropriately?
5. Would the expected benefit justify treatment burden?

The surgical technique itself is outside this package.

### Block 5 — Unresectable recurrence

When recurrent disease cannot be safely or meaningfully removed, management moves toward non-surgical strategies.

Depending on context these may include:

- systemic therapy;
- selected chemoradiation;
- clinical trials;
- palliative/supportive care.

The precise regimen or radiation plan belongs to specialized packages.

### Block 6 — Metastatic recurrence

Metastatic recurrence is a management branch within this package, but not the detailed subject of this package.

The central concept is that metastatic recurrence usually requires a systemic disease-management strategy, with decisions influenced by:

- previous therapy;
- performance status;
- biomarker status;
- disease burden;
- treatment tolerance;
- patient goals.

Detailed metastatic-disease management is delegated to PP-0223.

### Block 7 — Peritoneal recurrence

Peritoneal recurrence is clinically important because it can require specialized characterization and management.

PP-0222 should recognize the branch and explain why it may lead to specialized pathways.

Detailed peritoneal carcinomatosis, peritoneal-only disease and HIPEC are delegated.

### Block 8 — Systemic therapy as a management pillar

Systemic therapy is an important component of recurrent gastric-cancer management when local treatment is not appropriate or when disease is metastatic.

At this package level, the important concepts are:

- systemic treatment may be first-line or subsequent-line in the recurrent setting;
- prior treatment influences subsequent choices;
- performance status matters;
- biomarker status may matter;
- treatment benefit must be balanced against toxicity and patient goals.

Detailed regimens belong to PP-0208 and therapy-specific packages.

### Block 9 — Selected chemoradiation

NCCN identifies chemoradiation as a possible option for selected locally unresectable recurrence when appropriate and when prior radiation history does not preclude it.

This package owns the strategic role only.

Radiation planning, dose, fields and technical execution are delegated.

### Block 10 — Biomarker-informed management

Biomarkers may influence treatment selection in recurrent/advanced disease.

The management framework may need to consider information such as:

- HER2;
- CLDN18.2;
- PD-L1;
- MSI/MMR;
- other actionable molecular findings where clinically relevant.

The purpose here is to explain why biology can change the treatment branch.

Detailed testing and therapy-specific interpretation are delegated.

### Block 11 — Previous treatment matters

Previous treatment is part of the recurrent-disease decision.

The team may need to understand:

- what surgery was previously performed;
- what systemic therapy was previously used;
- whether radiation was previously delivered;
- prior treatment response;
- cumulative toxicity;
- remaining treatment options.

NCCN systemic-therapy principles explicitly make subsequent treatment dependent on prior therapy and performance status.

### Block 12 — Performance status and medical fitness

A patient’s ability to tolerate treatment is a major management determinant.

Management is influenced by:

- performance status;
- organ function;
- comorbidities;
- nutritional status;
- frailty;
- previous treatment toxicity.

The same recurrence pattern can therefore lead to different management choices in different patients.

### Block 13 — Clinical trials

Clinical trials can be part of recurrent-disease management.

They may be relevant when:

- standard options are limited;
- a biomarker-defined treatment is being evaluated;
- a new combination is under study;
- the patient is otherwise an appropriate candidate.

A trial is not automatically a last-resort option.

### Block 14 — Treatment is a loop, not a single decision

Management should be viewed as:

**Treatment selection**
→ **treatment**
→ **response/tolerance assessment**
→ **benefit versus burden**
→ **continue, modify, change or stop**
→ **new assessment**

Response assessment and RECIST are separate packages.

PP-0222 owns the management consequence of reassessment, not the measurement methodology.

### Block 15 — Patient goals and shared decision-making

A recurrent-cancer treatment plan should reflect the patient's goals and preferences.

Important questions include:

- What benefit is realistically expected?
- What burdens or side effects are possible?
- Is the goal disease control, symptom relief, local control or another outcome?
- How will treatment affect daily function?
- What alternatives exist?
- Is supportive care appropriate alongside or instead of disease-directed treatment?

### Block 16 — Palliative and supportive care

Palliative care can be integrated with cancer-directed treatment.

Best supportive care may also become the primary approach when disease-directed therapy is unlikely to provide meaningful benefit or cannot be safely delivered.

Supportive care is active care, not “doing nothing.”

## Patient Explanation

When stomach cancer returns, treatment is not automatically the same for everyone.

The medical team first looks at **where the cancer has returned, how much disease is present, whether it can potentially be removed, what treatments have already been given, the patient's general health, the characteristics of the cancer, and the patient's goals**.

For some people with an isolated and potentially removable recurrence, surgery may be considered.

For other people, the cancer may be too extensive to remove safely, or it may have spread to distant organs. In those situations, treatment may focus on controlling the cancer with medicines, selected radiation-based treatment, clinical trials, and supportive or palliative care.

The goal is not always simply to “treat more.” The goal is to choose the approach in which the expected benefit, treatment burden, health status and patient priorities are appropriately balanced.

## Clinical Importance

The most important clinical distinction is:

> **Recurrence detection is not the same as recurrence management.**

Once recurrence is established, management requires a structured assessment of:

**Disease pattern**
→ **resectability**
→ **medical fitness**
→ **previous treatment**
→ **tumor biology**
→ **treatment intent**
→ **management option**
→ **reassessment**

This framework prevents treatment from being selected from a single variable.

## Key Concepts

### 1. Recurrence does not automatically mean metastatic disease

Some recurrences are localized or locoregional.

### 2. Localized recurrence may be potentially treatable with local therapy

Selected medically fit patients with isolated resectable locoregional recurrence may be considered for surgery.

### 3. Unresectable recurrence needs a different strategy

Systemic treatment, selected chemoradiation, trials and supportive care may become relevant.

### 4. Prior treatment changes the decision

A previous regimen or radiation course can influence what is feasible next.

### 5. Biomarkers can change treatment options

Biological features may open or close particular therapy-specific pathways.

### 6. Fitness matters

Performance status, organ function, comorbidities and nutritional condition affect treatment tolerance.

### 7. Treatment intent matters

The appropriate goal may be local control, disease control, symptom relief or quality-of-life preservation.

### 8. Management is dynamic

A plan may change after response, progression, toxicity or a change in patient priorities.

## Common Misconceptions

### Myth 1 — “If the cancer comes back, it cannot be treated.”

**Fact:**  
Some recurrences have potentially treatable patterns. Selected isolated resectable locoregional recurrence may be considered for surgery in medically fit patients.

### Myth 2 — “Every recurrence should be treated with chemotherapy.”

**Fact:**  
Management depends on recurrence pattern, resectability, previous treatment, patient fitness, tumor biology and goals.

### Myth 3 — “If recurrence is metastatic, there is nothing useful to do.”

**Fact:**  
Systemic treatment, clinical trials and supportive/palliative care may all have important roles.

### Myth 4 — “The same treatment used initially should simply be repeated.”

**Fact:**  
Previous therapy is an important determinant of subsequent management.

### Myth 5 — “A biomarker automatically determines treatment.”

**Fact:**  
Biomarkers can inform treatment selection, but the final decision also depends on disease status, previous treatment, fitness, toxicity and patient goals.

### Myth 6 — “Best supportive care means stopping care.”

**Fact:**  
Supportive care actively addresses symptoms, nutrition, function and quality of life.

### Myth 7 — “Palliative care means there is no cancer treatment.”

**Fact:**  
Palliative care can be provided alongside disease-directed treatment.

### Myth 8 — “More treatment is always better.”

**Fact:**  
Expected benefit must be balanced against toxicity, treatment burden and patient priorities.

## Key Messages

1. Management begins after recurrence has been established.
2. Recurrence pattern matters.
3. Resectability and medical fitness are central.
4. Selected isolated locoregional recurrence may be considered for surgery.
5. Unresectable or metastatic recurrence often requires systemic/palliative disease management.
6. Biomarkers can influence therapy selection.
7. Previous treatment matters.
8. Performance status and comorbidities matter.
9. Clinical trials are a legitimate option in appropriate patients.
10. Palliative and supportive care remain active components of management.
11. Treatment decisions should be revisited as disease and patient circumstances change.
12. This package provides a framework, not individualized medical advice.

## Patient Questions

### “Does recurrence always mean I need chemotherapy?”

No. The appropriate approach depends on where the cancer has returned, whether it can be removed, what treatments you have already received, your general health and the biology of the cancer.

### “Could surgery still be possible?”

In selected patients with isolated, resectable locoregional recurrence who are medically fit, surgery may be considered. This is a specialist decision.

### “Why does my previous treatment matter?”

Previous treatment can affect what treatments remain available, how well your body may tolerate further treatment and whether some local treatments remain feasible.

### “Why are biomarkers tested?”

Some tumor characteristics can help identify treatments that may be relevant to the cancer.

### “What if I am not fit enough for intensive treatment?”

The treatment plan can be adjusted. Symptom control, supportive care, palliative care and less intensive approaches may become more important.

### “Can I have palliative care while receiving cancer treatment?”

Yes. Palliative care can be integrated with cancer-directed treatment.

### “What happens if the treatment stops working?”

The team may reassess the disease, review previous treatments and available options, and consider changing treatment, a clinical trial or a stronger focus on supportive care depending on the circumstances.

## Clinical Safety Boundary

This package describes a general management framework.

It does not determine:

- whether an individual patient's recurrence is resectable;
- which drug or regimen an individual should receive;
- whether an individual should undergo radiation or surgery;
- whether treatment should be stopped;
- an individual prognosis.

Those decisions require review by the treating multidisciplinary team using the patient's actual imaging, pathology, biomarkers, previous treatment and health status.
