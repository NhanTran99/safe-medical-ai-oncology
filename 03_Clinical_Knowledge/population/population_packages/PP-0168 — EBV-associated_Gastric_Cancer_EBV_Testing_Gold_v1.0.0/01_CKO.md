## B. Objective

Explain why Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) is relevant to a subset of gastric cancers, what EBV-associated gastric cancer means, what clinical features are associated with EBV-positive tumors, and when tumor EBV testing should be considered.

## C. Atomic Clinical Question

> What is EBV-associated gastric cancer, why is EBV relevant to gastric cancer, and when should EBV status be tested in a patient with gastric cancer?

## E. Clinical Knowledge Blocks

### KB1 — What Is EBV?
EBV is a common virus. Most people are infected at some point in life, often during childhood or adolescence.

### KB2 — What Is EBV-associated Gastric Cancer?
A subset of gastric cancers is associated with EBV within the tumor. The supplied NCCN source estimates approximately 8%–10%.

### KB3 — How Common Is It?
NCI's cited systematic review/meta-analysis reported pooled EBV prevalence of 8.77% among 20,361 gastric-cancer patients. ACS describes EBV in cancer cells of about 5%–10% of people with stomach cancer.

### KB4 — Where Does EBV-associated Gastric Cancer Tend to Occur?
NCCN reports preferential occurrence in the proximal stomach and association with diffuse-type histology and early onset. NCI reports higher EBV prevalence in cardia and body than antrum.

These are associations, not diagnostic criteria.

### KB5 — Does EBV Cause Gastric Cancer?
The supplied evidence establishes a strong association between EBV infection and gastric cancer, but the precise causal mechanism is not fully established in the supplied patient-facing sources. Avoid deterministic causal wording.

### KB6 — Prognostic Significance
Some studies suggest better overall survival for EBV-positive tumors, but NCCN states that prognostic significance remains a subject of debate.

### KB7 — EBV and Immune Biology
EBV-positive tumors have been associated with elevated PD-L1 expression and an interferon-gamma-driven gene signature. These observations support biologic interest in immune therapies but do not establish EBV-directed treatment selection.

### KB8 — Systemic EBV Exposure vs Tumor EBV Status
A person may have been infected with EBV without having an EBV-associated gastric tumor. The clinical question in this PP is tumor EBV status.

### KB9 — Is EBV Testing Routine?
No. NCCN states that tumor EBV testing is not currently recommended for routine clinical care because prospective evidence is lacking and the exact association remains incompletely understood.

### KB10 — When Should EBV Testing Be Performed?
NCCN recommends EBV testing when the tumor morphology contains prominent lymphoid stroma.

### KB11 — What Does Morphology Do?
Prominent lymphoid stroma is a reason to perform EBV testing. It is not proof of EBV positivity.

### KB12 — What Does a Positive Result Mean?
A positive tumor EBV result supports classification of the tumor as EBV-associated. It does not automatically determine treatment, prognosis, or hereditary risk.

### KB13 — What Does a Negative Result Mean?
A negative result does not create an EBV-associated classification and should be interpreted within the quality and context of the clinical assay. This package does not establish assay-specific sensitivity/specificity thresholds.

### KB14 — Does EBV Status Determine Immunotherapy?
No. NCCN describes EBV as an emerging potential biomarker and notes that more data are needed before treatment implications can be substantiated.

### KB15 — Is EBV-associated Gastric Cancer Hereditary?
No. EBV association is a tumor-biologic feature, not an inherited gastric-cancer syndrome. Hereditary risk assessment and germline testing are separate packages.

## F. Patient Explanation

> Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) is a very common virus. A small proportion of stomach cancers are associated with EBV within the tumor.
>
> EBV-associated gastric cancers appear to have distinct biological features and often occur in the proximal stomach. Some studies suggest that these tumors may have different survival characteristics, but the prognostic significance of EBV status remains uncertain.
>
> EBV testing is not currently recommended routinely for every person with gastric cancer. NCCN recommends testing when the tumor has prominent lymphoid stroma on pathology.
>
> A positive tumor EBV result does not by itself determine treatment, and it does not mean that the cancer is hereditary.

## G. Common Misconceptions

### Myth 1
“I had EBV before, so my stomach cancer is EBV-positive.”

**Fact:** Common EBV exposure is not the same as tumor EBV status.

### Myth 2
“Every gastric-cancer patient should have EBV testing.”

**Fact:** NCCN does not currently recommend routine testing.

### Myth 3
“Prominent lymphoid stroma proves EBV positivity.”

**Fact:** It is a morphology-based reason to perform testing, not proof of positivity.

### Myth 4
“EBV-positive means immunotherapy is automatically indicated.”

**Fact:** Treatment implications remain uncertain and require broader clinical and biomarker context.

### Myth 5
“EBV-positive gastric cancer always has a better prognosis.”

**Fact:** Some studies suggest better survival, but prognostic significance remains debated.

### Myth 6
“EBV-positive gastric cancer is hereditary.”

**Fact:** EBV association is not an inherited cancer syndrome.

### Myth 7
“A positive blood EBV test diagnoses EBV-associated gastric cancer.”

**Fact:** The relevant clinical concept is tumor EBV status, not simply previous or systemic EBV exposure.

## H. Key Messages

- EBV is common, but only a subset of gastric cancers is EBV-associated.
- EBV-associated gastric cancer is a biologically distinct subgroup.
- EBV-positive tumors are associated with proximal location, diffuse-type histology, and early onset.
- EBV status has potential clinical relevance, but prognostic and treatment implications remain uncertain.
- EBV testing is not routine for every gastric-cancer patient.
- Prominent lymphoid stroma is the NCCN morphology-based trigger for EBV testing.
- Tumor EBV status is different from previous EBV infection.
- EBV status does not replace HER2, MSI/MMR, PD-L1, CLDN18.2, or broader molecular testing.
- EBV-positive gastric cancer is not hereditary.
