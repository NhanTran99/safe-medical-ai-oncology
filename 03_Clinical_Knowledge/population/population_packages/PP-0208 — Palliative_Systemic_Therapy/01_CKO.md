# 1. Educational Question

## Primary Question

**What is palliative systemic therapy for gastric cancer, when is it used, what are its goals, and how are systemic-treatment decisions shaped by disease setting, biomarkers, prior treatment, performance status, toxicity, and patient goals?**

This Population Package explains the **systemic-treatment decision architecture** for unresectable locally advanced, recurrent, and metastatic gastric cancer.

It does not attempt to reproduce every drug-specific treatment package.

---

# 2. Educational Objectives

After reading this Population Package, the reader should be able to:

1. Explain what “palliative systemic therapy” means in gastric cancer.
2. Distinguish palliative systemic treatment from curative-intent perioperative treatment.
3. Understand why unresectable, recurrent, and metastatic disease may require systemic treatment.
4. Explain the main goals of palliative systemic therapy.
5. Understand why treatment is organized into first-line, second-line, and subsequent-line decisions.
6. Understand why performance status, comorbidities, toxicity, and prior treatment matter.
7. Understand why biomarker information can influence systemic-treatment selection.
8. Recognize the high-level roles of HER2, PD-L1, CLDN18.2, and MSI-H/dMMR.
9. Understand that chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy may all form parts of the systemic-treatment strategy.
10. Understand why treatment may change after progression or unacceptable toxicity.
11. Understand the relationship between active anticancer treatment, palliative care, and best supportive care.
12. Recognize that palliative treatment does not mean that care has stopped.
13. Understand why individual treatment decisions cannot be made from one biomarker or one treatment option alone.
14. Identify useful questions to discuss with the treating team.

# 4. What Is Palliative Systemic Therapy?

Palliative systemic therapy is active anticancer treatment used when the treatment goal is primarily to **control advanced cancer, reduce cancer-related problems, maintain function and quality of life, and/or prolong survival**, rather than to eradicate all disease through a curative local-treatment strategy.

In gastric cancer, this framework is particularly relevant when disease is:

- unresectable;
- recurrent and not amenable to curative local treatment;
- metastatic.

The term **systemic** means that treatment acts throughout the body rather than being directed only at one local site.

The term **palliative** describes the treatment goal and clinical setting. It does not mean that the treatment is meaningless, that clinicians have stopped treating the cancer, or that the patient is necessarily at the end of life.

The NCI PDQ describes treatment options for stage IV, inoperable, and recurrent gastric cancer as including cytotoxic therapies, targeted therapies, immunotherapies, and palliative locoregional therapies.

---

# 5. Why Is Systemic Treatment Important in Advanced Disease?

Advanced gastric cancer may involve multiple sites or may no longer be safely removable with curative surgery.

When disease is systemic, treating one visible site does not necessarily control microscopic or distant disease elsewhere.

Systemic treatment therefore provides a way to treat cancer cells throughout the body.

The NCCN framework states that systemic therapy can provide:

- palliation;
- improved survival;
- enhanced quality of life

for patients with locally advanced or metastatic gastric cancer.

This is the central rationale for PP-0208.

---

# 6. Palliative Does Not Mean “No Treatment”

A frequent misunderstanding is:

> “Palliative treatment means the doctors have given up.”

That is incorrect.

Palliative systemic therapy may be active anticancer treatment.

It can aim to:

- slow cancer growth;
- shrink or control tumors;
- delay progression;
- relieve cancer-related symptoms;
- preserve function;
- prolong life;
- maintain quality of life.

At the same time, palliative treatment must remain proportional to the likely benefit and burden of treatment.

---

# 7. Palliative Systemic Therapy Versus Curative-Intent Treatment

## Curative-intent setting

In potentially resectable localized gastric cancer, systemic treatment may be integrated with surgery as:

- perioperative treatment;
- neoadjuvant treatment;
- postoperative/adjuvant treatment.

Those packages are owned by other PPs.

## Palliative setting

In unresectable, recurrent, or metastatic disease, systemic therapy is generally used to:

- control disease;
- reduce symptoms;
- prolong survival;
- maintain quality of life.

The distinction is therefore based on **disease setting and treatment intent**, not simply on the name of a drug.

A chemotherapy agent can appear in both curative-intent and palliative settings.

---

# 8. Core Treatment Contexts

## 8.1 Unresectable locally advanced disease

Some patients have locoregional disease that cannot be safely or completely removed surgically.

For medically fit patients with surgically unresectable locoregional disease, systemic therapy and/or chemoradiation may be considered depending on the clinical setting.

This PP owns the systemic-treatment component.

## 8.2 Recurrent disease

Gastric cancer may recur after previous treatment.

If recurrence is unresectable or metastatic, systemic therapy may become the principal anticancer treatment.

The specific recurrent-disease management framework is delegated to the recurrent-disease PP.

## 8.3 Metastatic disease

Metastatic gastric cancer is a principal setting for palliative systemic therapy.

Because cancer is present beyond the primary/local region, treatment generally requires a systemic strategy unless a selected local approach is specifically appropriate.

---

# 9. Main Goals of Palliative Systemic Therapy

A treatment plan may have several goals at the same time.

## 9.1 Disease control

The treatment may slow cancer growth or reduce tumor burden.

## 9.2 Symptom relief

Treatment may reduce symptoms caused by cancer.

Examples include symptoms related to:

- tumor burden;
- bleeding;
- pain;
- obstruction.

Systemic therapy is not the only way to relieve these symptoms; selected local palliative treatments may also be used.

## 9.3 Survival

Some systemic treatments prolong survival compared with supportive care or alternative treatments in selected patient populations.

## 9.4 Quality of life

The goal is not simply to prolong life at any cost.

The balance between:

- expected cancer control;
- treatment toxicity;
- functional status;
- symptom burden;
- patient priorities

is essential.

---

# 10. The Systemic-Treatment Decision Is a Sequence

Palliative systemic therapy is not one fixed treatment.

A useful conceptual pathway is:

**Advanced / recurrent / metastatic disease**

↓

**Confirm treatment setting and goals**

↓

**Assess performance status and comorbidities**

↓

**Review biomarker information**

↓

**Review previous treatment**

↓

**Choose a first-line or subsequent-line strategy**

↓

**Assess benefit and toxicity**

↓

**Continue, modify, or change treatment**

↓

**Reassess goals**

↓

**Consider subsequent therapy, clinical trial, palliative care, and/or best supportive care as appropriate**

This is a clinical reasoning framework, not a universal individualized treatment algorithm.

---

# 11. Why Biomarkers Matter

Modern systemic treatment is increasingly influenced by tumor biology.

In advanced gastric cancer, clinically relevant biomarker information can include:

- HER2;
- PD-L1;
- CLDN18.2;
- MSI/MMR.

The exact testing strategy and interpretation belong to dedicated biomarker-testing PPs.

PP-0208 owns the **clinical bridge**:

**biomarker result**

→

**possible treatment relevance**

→

**systemic-treatment consideration**

The biomarker result does not automatically prescribe a treatment.

---

# 12. HER2 in the Palliative Systemic-Treatment Pathway

HER2 overexpression can create a targeted-treatment pathway.

In appropriate advanced HER2-positive gastric/EGJ adenocarcinoma, trastuzumab may be added to chemotherapy.

Selected patients may also receive an immune checkpoint inhibitor in combination with trastuzumab and chemotherapy depending on the clinical setting and biomarker criteria.

HER2-directed treatment itself is delegated to PP-0210.

The important PP-0208 message is:

> **HER2 status can change the systemic-treatment pathway.**

---

# 13. PD-L1 in the Palliative Systemic-Treatment Pathway

PD-L1 expression, commonly summarized using the combined positive score (CPS), can influence eligibility for selected immunotherapy-containing systemic regimens.

Current NCCN treatment pathways incorporate PD-L1 status into selected first-line treatment decisions.

PP-0208 therefore explains:

> PD-L1 may help determine whether an immunotherapy-containing systemic strategy is appropriate.

Detailed PD-L1 testing and treatment selection belong to PP-0216.

---

# 14. CLDN18.2 in the Palliative Systemic-Treatment Pathway

CLDN18.2 is another biomarker that can redirect systemic treatment.

For appropriate HER2-negative, CLDN18.2-positive advanced gastric/EGJ adenocarcinoma, zolbetuximab combined with chemotherapy is incorporated into the systemic-treatment pathway.

PP-0208 uses CLDN18.2 as an example of how:

**biomarker**

→

**treatment eligibility**

→

**systemic-treatment selection**

can work.

Detailed CLDN18.2-targeted treatment belongs to PP-0211.

---

# 15. MSI-H/dMMR in the Palliative Systemic-Treatment Pathway

Microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) or mismatch-repair deficient (dMMR) disease can have important implications for immunotherapy.

Current guidance includes selected immunotherapy options for MSI-H/dMMR tumors.

The important principle is:

> **A tumor's molecular characteristics can influence systemic treatment beyond conventional chemotherapy selection.**

Detailed MSI-H/dMMR immunotherapy belongs to PP-0215.

---

# 16. First-Line Palliative Systemic Therapy

First-line therapy means the initial systemic treatment given in the palliative setting.

The choice is not determined by one factor.

It may depend on:

- HER2 status;
- PD-L1 status;
- CLDN18.2 status;
- MSI/MMR status;
- performance status;
- comorbidities;
- prior therapy;
- treatment goals;
- expected toxicity.

The NCI PDQ describes first-line palliative systemic therapy separately for HER2-negative and HER2-positive disease.

---

# 17. Chemotherapy as a First-Line Backbone

Chemotherapy remains an important component of first-line systemic therapy.

Common chemotherapy classes used in gastric cancer include:

- fluoropyrimidines;
- platinum agents;
- taxanes;
- irinotecan-containing regimens in selected contexts.

The package does not teach dosing or schedules.

The key principle is:

> **chemotherapy remains a major systemic-treatment backbone, but it may be combined with targeted therapy or immunotherapy when appropriate.**

---

# 18. Doublet Versus Triplet Intensity

NCCN states that two-drug cytotoxic regimens are generally preferred in advanced disease because of lower toxicity.

Three-drug cytotoxic regimens are reserved for selected medically fit patients with excellent performance status and the ability to undergo frequent toxicity assessment.

This illustrates an important principle:

> **More intensive treatment is not automatically better treatment.**

Treatment intensity must be matched to:

- expected benefit;
- fitness;
- toxicity;
- monitoring capacity;
- patient goals.

---

# 19. HER2-Positive First-Line Strategy

For appropriate HER2-positive advanced disease, trastuzumab-containing chemotherapy is a central systemic-treatment strategy.

Selected patients may also receive pembrolizumab with trastuzumab and chemotherapy according to current biomarker criteria.

This is an example of **multiple-biomarker and treatment-domain integration**:

**HER2**

+

**appropriate clinical setting**

+

**relevant PD-L1 status**

↓

possible combined targeted + immunotherapy + chemotherapy strategy.

PP-0208 only explains this architecture.

Detailed treatment selection belongs downstream.

---

# 20. HER2-Negative First-Line Strategy

For HER2-negative disease, first-line treatment may involve:

- chemotherapy alone;
- chemotherapy plus an immune checkpoint inhibitor;
- chemotherapy plus a biomarker-directed targeted agent in selected CLDN18.2-positive tumors.

The exact choice depends on the biomarker and clinical context.

---

# 21. Why Treatment Cannot Be Chosen From HER2 Alone

HER2 is only one part of the treatment decision.

A patient can have:

- HER2 status;
- PD-L1 status;
- CLDN18.2 status;
- MSI/MMR status;
- prior therapy;
- comorbidities;
- performance status.

These factors may intersect.

Therefore:

> **A palliative systemic-treatment decision is a multidimensional clinical decision.**

---

# 22. Performance Status

Performance status describes how well a person is functioning in everyday life.

It matters because systemic treatment can cause toxicity.

A person who is physically fit may tolerate a more intensive treatment better than a person who is already weak or significantly limited by disease.

NCCN explicitly states that systemic regimens should be selected in the context of:

- performance status;
- medical comorbidities;
- toxicity profile.

---

# 23. Comorbidities

Other medical conditions can change the balance between treatment benefit and treatment burden.

Examples may include conditions affecting:

- organ function;
- ability to tolerate intensive treatment;
- risk of specific toxicities;
- ability to attend frequent monitoring.

Therefore:

> the “best” regimen on paper may not be the best regimen for every patient.

---

# 24. Treatment Tolerance

Treatment selection must consider not only whether a treatment can work, but also whether the patient can safely receive it.

This includes:

- baseline health;
- previous toxicity;
- cumulative treatment burden;
- current symptoms;
- functional status.

Detailed toxicity management belongs to PP-0231.

---

# 25. Second-Line Therapy

Second-line treatment is considered when first-line therapy:

- stops controlling the cancer;
- becomes intolerable;
- must be discontinued for another clinical reason.

NCI states that there is no single standard second-line treatment for all patients after progression on first-line palliative chemotherapy.

Accepted options include selected:

- taxane-based therapy;
- ramucirumab-containing therapy;
- irinotecan-based therapy;
- immunotherapy for selected biomarker-defined tumors;
- HER2-directed therapy for selected HER2-positive tumors.

The exact treatment choice depends on prior therapy, performance status, biomarkers, toxicity, and clinical goals.

---

# 26. Why Second-Line Therapy Is Different

Second-line treatment is not simply “repeat first-line treatment.”

The treating team must reconsider:

- what was already used;
- how long it worked;
- whether progression occurred during or after treatment;
- cumulative toxicity;
- current performance status;
- remaining biomarker-directed options;
- patient priorities.

This is why prior treatment history is a core component of PP-0208.

---

# 27. Ramucirumab as a Later-Line Example

Ramucirumab illustrates the role of a targeted agent in later-line systemic treatment.

NCCN lists ramucirumab plus paclitaxel as a preferred second-line regimen.

Ramucirumab can also be used in selected alternative combinations.

However, ramucirumab is not routinely added to first-line chemotherapy simply because the disease is advanced.

The RAINFALL trial did not demonstrate a benefit from adding ramucirumab to first-line chemotherapy in treatment-naive metastatic gastroesophageal adenocarcinoma.

This distinction prevents a common misunderstanding:

> **A treatment can be useful in one treatment line without being useful in another.**

Detailed anti-angiogenic treatment belongs to PP-0212.

---

# 28. HER2-Positive Later-Line Therapy

For HER2-positive disease, fam-trastuzumab deruxtecan is an important later-line example.

DESTINY-Gastric01 showed higher response and survival outcomes compared with physician-choice chemotherapy in a previously treated HER2-positive population.

The study also demonstrated substantial treatment toxicity, including hematologic toxicity and interstitial lung disease/pneumonitis.

This is useful for teaching the central principle:

> **Treatment benefit must always be considered together with treatment burden.**

Detailed HER2-targeted therapy belongs to PP-0210.

---

# 29. MSI-H/dMMR Later-Line Therapy

Selected MSI-H/dMMR tumors may be candidates for immunotherapy in later-line settings.

The exact choice depends on:

- prior treatment;
- biomarker confirmation;
- clinical status;
- current guideline pathway.

PP-0208 explains the existence of this branch.

PP-0215 owns the detailed MSI-H/dMMR immunotherapy domain.

---

# 30. Third-Line and Subsequent Therapy

After progression through multiple treatment lines, treatment options may become more limited.

NCCN identifies trifluridine/tipiracil as a preferred category 1 third-line or subsequent option.

Other later-line options may be selected based on:

- prior therapy;
- biomarker status;
- performance status;
- available alternatives;
- patient goals.

The expected benefit may be smaller and treatment burden may become increasingly important.

---

# 31. Best Supportive Care

Best supportive care is an active clinical approach focused on:

- symptom control;
- comfort;
- nutrition;
- function;
- quality of life;
- psychosocial needs.

It is not equivalent to “doing nothing.”

In some patients, best supportive care may be the most appropriate pathway when anticancer treatment is unlikely to provide meaningful benefit or when treatment burden is excessive.

---

# 32. Palliative Care

Palliative care is broader than palliative systemic therapy.

It addresses the person rather than only the tumor.

It may include:

- symptom management;
- pain management;
- nutritional support;
- psychosocial support;
- communication about goals of care;
- support for caregivers.

Palliative care can be provided **while anticancer treatment continues**.

---

# 33. Palliative Systemic Therapy and Best Supportive Care Can Coexist

A patient may receive:

**active systemic therapy**

+

**palliative/supportive care**

at the same time.

The choice is not necessarily:

> chemotherapy OR supportive care.

Supportive and palliative care can accompany active treatment.

As disease progresses, supportive-care priorities may become increasingly important.

---

# 34. When Treatment May Be Changed

Systemic treatment may be changed because of:

- disease progression;
- unacceptable toxicity;
- declining performance status;
- new clinical circumstances;
- availability of another more appropriate treatment;
- patient preference;
- treatment no longer providing meaningful benefit.

A treatment change does not necessarily mean that care has failed.

It means that the balance between benefit and burden has changed.

---

# 35. When Treatment May Be Stopped

Stopping anticancer therapy may be considered when:

- expected benefit is very small;
- treatment toxicity is excessive;
- the patient's health has deteriorated;
- disease continues to progress despite available options;
- the patient no longer wishes to receive treatment.

Stopping anticancer treatment does not mean stopping medical care.

---

# 36. Symptom-Directed Local Treatment

Systemic therapy is not the only palliative treatment.

Selected patients may require local treatment for specific problems such as:

- bleeding;
- pain;
- obstruction.

NCI identifies palliative radiation as a possible way to alleviate bleeding, pain, and obstruction.

Endoluminal interventions or gastrojejunostomy may also help selected patients with obstruction.

These interventions are supporting interfaces, not the core of PP-0208.

---

# 37. Peritoneal Disease Interface

Peritoneal metastasis is a clinically important form of advanced disease.

Systemic therapy may remain a major component of treatment.

However, detailed peritoneal-disease management, including selected cytoreductive surgery and HIPEC considerations, belongs to dedicated downstream PPs.

PP-0208 should not become a peritoneal-carcinomatosis package.

---

# 38. Treatment Response and Progression

Palliative systemic therapy requires reassessment.

A simplified conceptual cycle is:

**Treatment**

↓

**clinical and/or imaging assessment**

↓

**benefit / stable disease / progression / toxicity**

↓

**continue / modify / change**

↓

**reassess**

The formal response-assessment methodology belongs to PP-0217.

RECIST belongs to PP-0218.

Post-treatment imaging belongs to PP-0219.

---

# 39. Clinical Trials

Clinical trials may be considered when:

- standard options are limited;
- a biomarker creates a trial opportunity;
- a novel treatment is available;
- the patient is medically eligible.

Clinical-trial methodology and trial participation are not owned by PP-0208.

The package only needs to establish clinical trials as one possible part of the decision framework.

---

# 40. Patient Goals

Treatment decisions should consider what matters most to the patient.

Important goals may include:

- living longer;
- staying independent;
- reducing symptoms;
- avoiding hospitalization;
- maintaining nutrition;
- minimizing treatment toxicity;
- spending time with family;
- reducing treatment burden.

These goals can change over time.

---

# 41. Shared Decision-Making

Palliative systemic treatment is often a shared decision between:

- patient;
- caregivers/family as appropriate;
- medical oncology team;
- other members of the multidisciplinary team;
- palliative-care specialists when involved.

The decision should integrate:

**evidence**

+

**clinical condition**

+

**tumor biology**

+

**treatment history**

+

**patient goals**

---

# 42. Benefit Versus Burden

A useful patient-facing model is:

| Potential Benefit | Potential Burden |
|---|---|
| Tumor control | Toxicity |
| Symptom relief | Treatment visits |
| Longer survival | Monitoring |
| Preserved function | Financial/logistical burden |
| Quality of life | Cumulative treatment burden |

The appropriate choice is not necessarily the treatment with the greatest theoretical tumor activity.

It is the treatment whose expected benefit is meaningful in the patient's clinical context.

# 43. Common Misconceptions

## Myth 1 — “Palliative means the doctors have given up.”

**Fact:** Palliative systemic therapy can actively control cancer, relieve symptoms, and prolong life.

## Myth 2 — “Palliative chemotherapy is the same as supportive care.”

**Fact:** Chemotherapy is anticancer treatment. Supportive care focuses on symptoms, function, comfort, and quality of life.

## Myth 3 — “Palliative treatment is only for the last weeks of life.”

**Fact:** Palliative systemic treatment may be used for prolonged periods when advanced cancer remains controllable.

## Myth 4 — “Everyone with metastatic gastric cancer receives the same treatment.”

**Fact:** Treatment depends on biomarkers, prior treatment, performance status, comorbidities, toxicity, and goals.

## Myth 5 — “The strongest regimen is always the best.”

**Fact:** Higher treatment intensity can produce greater toxicity and is not appropriate for every patient.

## Myth 6 — “If first-line treatment stops working, there is nothing else.”

**Fact:** Selected patients may have second-line and subsequent treatment options.

## Myth 7 — “A biomarker automatically tells the doctor which drug to give.”

**Fact:** Biomarkers contribute to treatment selection but must be interpreted within clinical context.

## Myth 8 — “Stopping chemotherapy means stopping all treatment.”

**Fact:** Supportive and palliative care continue even when anticancer treatment is stopped.

## Myth 9 — “Palliative care means hospice.”

**Fact:** Palliative care can be provided alongside active cancer treatment. Hospice is a distinct end-of-life care model.

## Myth 10 — “If a treatment shrinks the tumor, it is automatically the best treatment.”

**Fact:** Benefit must be considered together with survival, symptoms, function, toxicity, and patient goals.

# 44. Patient Questions to Bring to the Care Team

1. What is the goal of my current treatment?
2. Is the treatment curative or palliative?
3. What information about my tumor is being used to choose treatment?
4. What are my HER2, PD-L1, CLDN18.2, and MSI/MMR results, if relevant?
5. What previous treatments affect my current options?
6. Why is this regimen being recommended for me?
7. What benefit are we hoping to achieve?
8. How will we know whether treatment is helping?
9. What side effects are most important to watch for?
10. What would make us change or stop treatment?
11. What are the options if the cancer progresses?
12. Would a clinical trial be relevant?
13. Can palliative care be involved while I receive cancer treatment?
14. What supportive treatments can help with my symptoms or nutrition?
15. What matters most to me that we should consider when choosing treatment?

# 45. Evidence-Supported Clinical Use Model

## Use 1 — Initial palliative treatment

**Unresectable / recurrent / metastatic disease**

↓

**Clinical assessment + biomarkers + goals**

↓

**First-line systemic treatment**

## Use 2 — Ongoing control

**Benefit + tolerable toxicity**

↓

continue current treatment with reassessment.

## Use 3 — Progression

**Progression / unacceptable toxicity / changed clinical circumstances**

↓

review prior treatment and current status.

## Use 4 — Subsequent treatment

**Second-line / third-line / subsequent therapy**

↓

select from appropriate options based on prior treatment, biomarkers, PS, toxicity, and goals.

## Use 5 — Supportive transition or integration

**Expected benefit becomes low or treatment burden becomes high**

↓

consider best supportive care and/or palliative care, with or without further anticancer therapy according to the clinical situation.

This is a conceptual clinical-use ladder, not a universal prescription algorithm.

# 46. Evidence Classification

## Established / Guideline-Supported

- Palliative systemic therapy is a major treatment strategy for unresectable locally advanced, recurrent, and metastatic gastric cancer.
- Systemic therapy can provide palliation, improve survival, and enhance quality of life.
- Biomarker information can influence systemic-treatment selection.
- HER2, PD-L1, CLDN18.2, and MSI/MMR can be clinically relevant in advanced disease.
- Two-drug cytotoxic regimens are generally preferred over more intensive triplet cytotoxic regimens because of lower toxicity.
- Later-line treatment depends on prior therapy and performance status.
- Best supportive care is a legitimate management pathway.
- Palliative care can coexist with active anticancer treatment.

## Context-Dependent

- Exact first-line regimen.
- Whether immunotherapy should be added.
- Whether targeted therapy should be added.
- Choice of second-line treatment.
- Choice of third-line/subsequent treatment.
- Continuation after stable disease.
- Treatment change after progression.
- Whether a clinical trial is appropriate.
- Whether active anticancer therapy remains appropriate as health status changes.

## Evidence-Supported but Not Universal

- Chemotherapy can improve survival compared with supportive care in selected metastatic patients.
- Ramucirumab-based therapy can provide later-line benefit in selected populations.
- Trastuzumab deruxtecan has later-line activity in HER2-positive disease.
- CLDN18.2-directed treatment can be incorporated into first-line therapy in selected patients.
- Immunotherapy-containing regimens can improve outcomes in selected biomarker-defined settings.

---

# 47. Evidence Limitations

The Source Materials do not support:

- a universal regimen for every patient;
- a universal duration of palliative systemic therapy;
- a universal sequence of every available drug;
- a universal stopping rule;
- treatment decisions based on one biomarker alone;
- individualized treatment recommendations without complete clinical context.

Therefore, PP-0208 deliberately uses a **decision architecture** rather than a deterministic prescription algorithm.

# 50. Clinical Safety Boundary

This Population Package provides general education about palliative systemic therapy. It is not an individualized treatment recommendation.

A treatment decision requires review of the complete clinical context, including disease extent, pathology, biomarkers, previous treatment, performance status, comorbidities, treatment toxicity, current evidence, and patient goals.

No single biomarker, scan, or treatment response should be interpreted as an automatic treatment order.

---

# 51. Key Messages

1. Palliative systemic therapy is active cancer treatment used primarily to control advanced disease rather than to pursue curative local eradication.
2. It is particularly relevant to unresectable, recurrent, and metastatic gastric cancer.
3. Palliative treatment does not mean that care has stopped.
4. Chemotherapy remains an important systemic-treatment backbone.
5. Targeted therapy and immunotherapy can become part of systemic treatment in selected biomarker-defined settings.
6. HER2, PD-L1, CLDN18.2, and MSI/MMR may influence treatment pathways.
7. Performance status, comorbidities, previous treatment, toxicity, and patient goals matter as much as the tumor biomarker in many decisions.
8. First-line and later-line treatment are different decision contexts.
9. Disease progression does not automatically mean that no treatment remains.
10. Best supportive care and palliative care are active forms of care and can coexist with anticancer treatment.
11. Treatment benefit must always be balanced against treatment burden.
12. The purpose of this PP is to explain the systemic-treatment architecture, not to prescribe an individual regimen.
