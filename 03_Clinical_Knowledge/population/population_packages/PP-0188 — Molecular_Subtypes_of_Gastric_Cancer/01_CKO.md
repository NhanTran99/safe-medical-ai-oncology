# 1. Educational Objectives

After reading this Population Package, the reader should be able to:

1. Explain what a molecular subtype of gastric cancer means.
2. Understand why gastric cancer can be divided into biologically different molecular groups.
3. Name the four major TCGA gastric-cancer molecular subtypes: **EBV, MSI, GS, and CIN**.
4. Name the four ACRG gastric-cancer molecular subtypes: **MSI, MSS/EMT, MSS/TP53+, and MSS/TP53−**.
5. Understand the main biological and clinical features associated with each framework.
6. Understand that TCGA and ACRG are **overlapping but non-equivalent** classification systems.
7. Understand how molecular subtypes relate to, but do not replace, Lauren histology, TNM stage, or individual biomarker results.
8. Understand that molecular subtype research has important prognostic and translational implications, but does not by itself determine an individual's treatment.
9. Recognize common misconceptions, especially the assumption that a TCGA subtype and an ACRG subtype are interchangeable.
10. Understand why molecular classification is scientifically important even though it is not a universal routine clinical test for every patient.

# 3. Clinical Knowledge Blocks

## Block 1 — What Is a Molecular Subtype?

A **molecular subtype** is a group of cancers that share a recognizable pattern of molecular characteristics.

These characteristics can include:

- gene activity;
- DNA mutations;
- DNA methylation;
- chromosome-number or copy-number changes;
- viral association;
- protein-expression patterns;
- other molecular features.

The important point is that gastric cancer is not one biologically uniform disease. Tumors that look similar under the microscope can have different molecular drivers and may behave differently.

Molecular classification therefore adds another layer of information to the traditional clinical description of gastric cancer.

### Patient explanation

A simple way to think about this is:

> **Lauren classification describes what the cancer looks like. TNM staging describes how far it has spread. Molecular classification describes important features of the cancer's underlying biology.**

These are complementary descriptions, not replacements for one another.

---

## Block 2 — Why Are There More Than One Molecular Classification System?

There is no single universal molecular-subtype system that should be treated as the only correct classification.

Two major frameworks are particularly important:

### TCGA

The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) integrated multiple molecular data types and defined four major gastric-cancer molecular subtypes:

1. **EBV**
2. **MSI**
3. **GS — Genomically Stable**
4. **CIN — Chromosomal Instability**

### ACRG

The Asian Cancer Research Group (ACRG) developed another clinically oriented molecular framework:

1. **MSI**
2. **MSS/EMT**
3. **MSS/TP53+**
4. **MSS/TP53−**

The ACRG study directly compared its framework with the TCGA framework and found meaningful overlap but also important differences. Therefore, the two systems should **not** be treated as interchangeable. [S2]

---

## Block 3 — TCGA Molecular Classification

The TCGA framework was based on integrated molecular characterization of gastric tumors. The TCGA program incorporated multiple layers of molecular information, including somatic mutations, mRNA expression, miRNA expression, promoter methylation, somatic copy-number alterations, and protein-expression data.

The four genomic/molecular groups are:

| TCGA subtype | Main biological concept |
|---|---|
| **EBV** | Epstein–Barr virus-associated biology and strong immune/methylation features |
| **MSI** | Microsatellite instability, hypermutation, and methylation |
| **GS** | Relatively low copy-number alteration and distinctive diffuse-type/genomic-stability biology |
| **CIN** | Chromosomal instability, aneuploidy, and extensive copy-number alteration |

The TCGA clinical-validation study developed a gene-expression prediction model and tested it in two independent patient cohorts. All four subtypes were represented in the validation cohorts, supporting reproducibility. [S1]

---

## Block 4 — TCGA EBV Subtype

The **EBV subtype** is characterized by association with Epstein–Barr virus and distinctive molecular features.

Reported characteristics include:

- marked DNA/promoter hypermethylation;
- frequent PIK3CA alterations;
- strong immune/cytokine-related signals;
- evidence of active immune-cell involvement;
- relatively favorable prognosis in the TCGA clinical-validation cohorts.

In the Sohn et al. validation study, EBV was associated with the best prognosis for both recurrence-free survival and overall survival in the MD Anderson cohort and remained the best-prognosis group in the Samsung Medical Center cohort. [S1]

### Important interpretation

EBV as a molecular subtype does **not** mean that every EBV-positive tumor has identical biology.

It also does not mean that EBV status alone determines treatment.

Detailed EBV-associated gastric cancer and tumor EBV testing belong to **PP-0168**.

---

## Block 5 — TCGA MSI Subtype

The **MSI subtype** is characterized by microsatellite instability and a high mutation burden.

Reported features include:

- elevated mutation rates;
- hypermethylation;
- mismatch-repair-related biology;
- a distinctive clinical phenotype.

MSI is unusual in this knowledge architecture because it can be understood at two levels:

1. as an **individual clinically measurable biomarker**; and
2. as a **molecular-subtype category** within TCGA and ACRG.

This distinction is important.

A patient can have an MSI result without the clinical team needing to describe the tumor using the entire TCGA or ACRG classification system.

Detailed MSI/MMR testing belongs to **PP-0182**.

---

## Block 6 — TCGA Genomically Stable (GS) Subtype

The **GS subtype** was defined by relatively fewer distinctive copy-number alterations compared with CIN and is associated with a characteristic diffuse-type/genomic-stability biology.

The TCGA clinical-validation work described:

- low mutation rates relative to MSI;
- low copy-number alteration;
- frequent CDH1 and RHOA alterations in the TCGA GS group;
- association with the least favorable prognosis among the four TCGA groups in the validation cohorts. [S1]

The GS group is especially important when explaining why molecular and histologic classifications should not be collapsed into one another.

The ACRG study found that TCGA GS was enriched in ACRG MSS/EMT, but the groups were **not equivalent**. Differences in Lauren distribution and in CDH1 and RHOA alteration patterns supported this conclusion. [S2]

---

## Block 7 — TCGA Chromosomal Instability (CIN) Subtype

The **CIN subtype** is characterized by:

- marked aneuploidy;
- extensive somatic copy-number alterations;
- focal amplification of receptor tyrosine kinases and other genomic regions.

The CIN group illustrates a different form of gastric-cancer biology from MSI or GS: the major signal is genomic copy-number instability rather than hypermutation or relative genomic stability.

The TCGA clinical-validation study found CIN to have an intermediate prognosis overall, although prognosis was less homogeneous across the two validation cohorts than for some other groups. [S1]

---

## Block 8 — TCGA Subtypes and Prognosis

The TCGA clinical-validation study found a reproducible broad pattern:

**EBV → best prognosis**  
**MSI / CIN → intermediate prognosis**  
**GS → worst prognosis**

In the MD Anderson cohort, the association was significant for both recurrence-free survival and overall survival. The same broad ordering was observed in the Samsung Medical Center cohort. [S1]

### Important limitation

These findings are **population-level associations from retrospective validation cohorts**.

They should not be converted into:

> “A patient with subtype X will have outcome Y.”

Individual prognosis depends on many factors, including stage, pathology, treatment, comorbidities, tumor burden, and other biological features.

---

## Block 9 — TCGA Subtypes and Adjuvant Chemotherapy

The Sohn et al. study also examined adjuvant chemotherapy in a subset of patients.

The strongest observed association was in CIN:

- HR for recurrence with adjuvant chemotherapy: **0.39**
- 95% CI: **0.16–0.94**
- P = **0.03**

The GS group did not show a statistically significant chemotherapy benefit:

- HR: **0.83**
- 95% CI: **0.36–1.89**
- P = **0.65**

MSI showed a non-significant intermediate association, and chemotherapy benefit could not be assessed in EBV because all patients in that subgroup received chemotherapy. [S1]

### Critical interpretation

The authors explicitly cautioned that the retrospective design limited the predictive interpretation.

Therefore this evidence should be understood as:

> **hypothesis-generating evidence that molecular subgroups may differ in treatment response, not a routine subtype-guided chemotherapy rule.**

---

## Block 10 — ACRG Molecular Classification

The ACRG study analyzed 300 primary gastric tumors using gene-expression profiling and integrated molecular information with targeted sequencing and genome-wide copy-number data.

The four ACRG groups are:

1. **MSI**
2. **MSS/EMT**
3. **MSS/TP53+**
4. **MSS/TP53−**

The ACRG classification was reproduced in independent cohorts, including the Samsung Medical Center-2 cohort, a Singapore cohort, and the TCGA gastric cohort. [S2]

---

## Block 11 — ACRG MSI Subtype

ACRG MSI tumors were characterized by:

- hypermutation;
- MLH1 expression loss;
- increased methylation signature;
- predominantly intestinal Lauren histology;
- frequent antral location;
- relatively earlier stage at diagnosis.

In the ACRG cohort, more than half of MSI tumors were diagnosed at stage I/II, and more than 60% had intestinal histology. [S2]

MSI had the **best overall prognosis** among the ACRG groups.

In pooled ACRG and SMC-2 data, documented recurrence occurred in:

- **22.2%** of MSI tumors.

[S2]

Detailed MSI/MMR testing belongs to **PP-0182**.

---

## Block 12 — ACRG MSS/EMT Subtype

**MSS/EMT** means microsatellite-stable tumors with a strong epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) signature.

This subtype was associated with:

- diffuse Lauren histology;
- a large proportion of signet-ring-cell carcinomas;
- CDH1 expression loss;
- younger age at diagnosis;
- stage III/IV disease;
- the poorest prognosis;
- the highest recurrence frequency.

In the ACRG cohort, more than 80% of MSS/EMT tumors were diffuse-type and most were stage III/IV. [S2]

In pooled ACRG and SMC-2 data, documented recurrence occurred in:

- **63.4%** of MSS/EMT tumors.

Among documented recurrences, peritoneal seeding was particularly prominent:

- **64.1%** of documented MSS/EMT recurrences in the pooled analysis. [S2]

### Critical distinction

MSS/EMT is **not the same thing as diffuse gastric cancer**.

Diffuse histology is a pathological classification.

MSS/EMT is a molecular classification.

They are strongly associated in the ACRG study, but they are not interchangeable concepts.

---

## Block 13 — ACRG MSS/TP53+

MSS/TP53+ represents a microsatellite-stable subgroup with a molecular signature consistent with relatively preserved TP53 activity.

The ACRG study used a two-gene TP53-activity signature involving **MDM2** and **CDKN1A/p21** to separate TP53-active from TP53-inactive tumors.

The signature was significantly associated with TP53 mutation status, supporting its biological relevance. [S2]

MSS/TP53+ had an intermediate prognosis, but better prognosis than MSS/TP53−.

EBV-positive tumors were enriched in this group:

- 12 of 18 EBV-positive tumors were found within MSS/TP53+.

However, this does **not** make MSS/TP53+ equivalent to the TCGA EBV subtype. The ACRG authors specifically interpreted these as probably different molecular groups. [S2]

---

## Block 14 — ACRG MSS/TP53−

MSS/TP53− represents a microsatellite-stable subgroup with reduced/inactive TP53-related activity.

It was associated with:

- TP53 mutation;
- broader copy-number damage;
- intermediate prognosis;
- worse prognosis than MSS/TP53+.

In pooled ACRG and SMC-2 data, documented recurrence occurred in:

- **43.6%** of MSS/TP53− tumors.

[S2]

TP53 mutation status is related to this classification, but:

> **TP53 mutation alone should not automatically be treated as a complete ACRG subtype assignment.**

---

## Block 15 — ACRG Prognostic Pattern

The ACRG study observed a reproducible prognostic trend:

**MSS/EMT → worst**  
**MSS/TP53− → intermediate-worse**  
**MSS/TP53+ → intermediate-better**  
**MSI → best**

The survival association was reproduced across:

- the original ACRG cohort;
- SMC-2;
- Singapore;
- TCGA gastric cohort;
- merged cohorts. [S2]

This provides strong research evidence that molecular subtype captures clinically meaningful heterogeneity.

It remains, however, a cohort-level prognostic association rather than an individualized prognostic calculator.

---

## Block 16 — TCGA and ACRG Are Not Interchangeable

This is a central concept of PP-0188.

The systems overlap:

| TCGA | Broad ACRG relationship |
|---|---|
| MSI | ACRG MSI |
| EBV | Enriched in ACRG MSS/TP53+ |
| GS | Enriched in ACRG MSS/EMT |
| CIN | Enriched in ACRG MSS/TP53− |

However, the relationship is **not one-to-one**.

The ACRG study found that:

- TCGA CIN tumors were distributed across all ACRG subtypes;
- TCGA GS tumors were distributed across all ACRG subtypes;
- TCGA GS and ACRG MSS/EMT differed in Lauren distribution and CDH1/RHOA alteration patterns;
- EBV-positive cases represented only a subset of MSS/TP53+;
- ACRG was therefore considered a unique classification framework. [S2]

### Patient-facing rule

> **Do not translate a TCGA subtype directly into an ACRG subtype.**

---

## Block 17 — Why Do TCGA and ACRG Differ?

The systems were developed using different molecular inputs and classification strategies.

### TCGA

The foundational TCGA approach integrated multiple molecular layers, including:

- somatic mutations;
- RNA expression;
- miRNA;
- methylation;
- copy-number alterations;
- protein expression.

### ACRG

The ACRG approach used gene-expression signatures together with:

- genome-wide copy-number data;
- targeted sequencing;
- clinical phenotype information.

Because the input data and classification logic differ, the resulting subgroup boundaries differ.

This is not a contradiction.

It is evidence that gastric cancer contains complex, overlapping biological dimensions.

---

## Block 18 — Molecular Subtype vs Individual Biomarker

A molecular subtype is a **pattern across many molecular features**.

An individual biomarker is a **specific measurable feature**.

Examples:

- MSI can be both a biomarker and a molecular-subtype category.
- HER2 status is an individual biomarker, not a TCGA or ACRG subtype.
- PD-L1 CPS is an individual biomarker, not a molecular subtype.
- CLDN18.2 expression is an individual biomarker, not a molecular subtype.
- TMB is an individual molecular measurement, not a TCGA/ACRG subtype.
- A TP53 mutation is not by itself synonymous with the ACRG MSS/TP53− subtype.

This distinction prevents overinterpretation of molecular reports.

---

## Block 19 — Molecular Subtype vs Lauren Classification

Lauren classification describes gastric-cancer morphology, particularly the broad intestinal and diffuse patterns.

Molecular subtyping describes molecular biology.

They can be associated:

- ACRG MSI → often intestinal;
- ACRG MSS/EMT → strongly enriched for diffuse-type disease.

But they answer different questions.

Therefore:

> **Molecular subtype does not replace Lauren classification.**

Detailed Lauren classification belongs to **PP-0179**.

---

## Block 20 — Molecular Subtype vs TNM Stage

TNM stage describes the anatomical extent of cancer:

- T = primary tumor;
- N = regional lymph nodes;
- M = distant metastasis.

Molecular subtype describes tumor biology.

A patient can therefore have:

> a particular stage **and** a particular molecular profile.

Molecular classification should not be interpreted as an alternative staging system.

---

## Block 21 — Is Molecular Subtyping a Routine Clinical Test?

The molecular-subtyping studies provide strong evidence that these frameworks capture meaningful biological heterogeneity.

However, the supplied foundational studies do not establish universal routine TCGA or ACRG testing for every gastric-cancer patient.

The TCGA clinical-validation paper specifically noted that the complexity of the original molecular classification created challenges for clinical translation and that further development was needed. [S1]

The ACRG authors likewise discussed the need for simpler, standardized approaches for translation.

Therefore:

> **TCGA and ACRG should be understood primarily as major molecular research/classification frameworks, not as interchangeable universal routine clinical tests.**

Current patient care should instead rely on validated clinical biomarkers and guideline-directed testing when indicated.

---

## Block 22 — What Does Molecular Subtyping Mean for a Patient Today?

For a patient, molecular subtype can help explain why gastric cancers are biologically different.

It may provide:

- a framework for understanding tumor biology;
- context for prognosis research;
- a way to organize molecular findings;
- a foundation for translational research and clinical trials.

But a molecular subtype alone does not answer:

> “Which treatment should I receive?”

Treatment decisions require the broader clinical picture, including:

- stage;
- pathology;
- validated biomarker results;
- treatment history;
- overall health;
- current clinical guidelines;
- patient preferences and treatment goals.

# 4. Clinical Importance

Molecular subtyping is important because gastric cancer is biologically heterogeneous.

Two tumors that appear similar clinically may have very different molecular characteristics.

The TCGA and ACRG frameworks demonstrate this heterogeneity from different perspectives.

The strongest educational value of these systems is therefore not that they provide a simple treatment label, but that they show:

> **gastric cancer is a collection of biologically distinct disease patterns rather than one uniform molecular disease.**

# 5. Key Concepts

## 5.1 The two major frameworks

**TCGA:** EBV / MSI / GS / CIN

**ACRG:** MSI / MSS-EMT / MSS-TP53+ / MSS-TP53−

## 5.2 The key non-equivalence rule

**TCGA subtype ≠ automatically corresponding ACRG subtype.**

## 5.3 MSI is special

MSI can be:

- a molecular subtype;
- an individual biomarker.

## 5.4 Molecular subtype does not replace stage

Stage answers:

> How far has the cancer spread?

Molecular subtype answers:

> What broad molecular pattern does the tumor show?

## 5.5 Molecular subtype does not replace pathology

Histology and molecular biology are complementary.

## 5.6 Molecular subtype does not automatically select treatment

Research associations should not be converted into treatment rules.

# 6. Common Misconceptions

### Misconception 1
“There are exactly four gastric-cancer molecular subtypes.”

**Correction:** Different molecular frameworks exist. TCGA and ACRG each define four, but their four groups are not the same.

### Misconception 2
“TCGA and ACRG are interchangeable.”

**Correction:** They overlap but are not equivalent.

### Misconception 3
“MSI is only a laboratory result.”

**Correction:** MSI is both a clinically measurable biomarker and a molecular-subtype category.

### Misconception 4
“MSS/EMT means diffuse gastric cancer.”

**Correction:** MSS/EMT is a molecular subgroup strongly associated with diffuse/signet-ring disease, but it is not the same classification.

### Misconception 5
“GS means MSS/EMT.”

**Correction:** TCGA GS is enriched in ACRG MSS/EMT but is not equivalent.

### Misconception 6
“EBV means MSS/TP53+.”

**Correction:** EBV-positive tumors are enriched in ACRG MSS/TP53+, but the groups are not equivalent.

### Misconception 7
“CIN means MSS/TP53−.”

**Correction:** There is enrichment, but CIN tumors can occur across ACRG subtypes.

### Misconception 8
“Molecular subtype tells me exactly which treatment I need.”

**Correction:** Subtype alone does not determine treatment.

### Misconception 9
“Molecular subtype replaces TNM stage.”

**Correction:** Molecular classification and anatomical staging describe different dimensions of disease.

### Misconception 10
“A molecular subtype gives my individual prognosis.”

**Correction:** Published subtype–outcome relationships are population-level associations.

---

# 7. Key Messages

1. Gastric cancer is molecularly heterogeneous.
2. TCGA identified four major gastric-cancer molecular subtypes: **EBV, MSI, GS, and CIN**.
3. ACRG identified four: **MSI, MSS/EMT, MSS/TP53+, and MSS/TP53−**.
4. The two systems overlap but are **not interchangeable**.
5. MSI is both a molecular-subtype category and an individual clinical biomarker.
6. Molecular subtype is different from Lauren histology and TNM stage.
7. TCGA and ACRG studies show meaningful associations with prognosis and recurrence.
8. TCGA evidence concerning differential chemotherapy benefit is retrospective and should not be interpreted as a routine treatment-selection rule.
9. Molecular-subtyping research provides an important framework for understanding gastric-cancer biology and heterogeneity.
10. Current treatment decisions require the full clinical and biomarker context, not molecular subtype alone.
