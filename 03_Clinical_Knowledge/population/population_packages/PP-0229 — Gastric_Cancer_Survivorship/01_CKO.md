# Educational Objectives

After reading this Population Package, the reader should be able to:

1. Explain what gastric cancer survivorship means and why survivorship care continues after active treatment.
2. Distinguish survivorship care from gastric-cancer-specific surveillance and recurrence detection.
3. Understand the purpose of a survivorship care plan.
4. Recognize important long-term and late effects after gastric cancer treatment.
5. Understand why gastrectomy can create long-term or lifelong nutritional and gastrointestinal consequences.
6. Recognize weight loss, diarrhea, fatigue, chemotherapy-induced neuropathy, and bone-health problems as important survivorship issues.
7. Understand why vitamin and mineral deficiencies may require long-term monitoring after gastrectomy.
8. Understand the role of physical activity, healthy body weight, healthy diet, smoking cessation, alcohol limitation, preventive care, and vaccination in survivorship.
9. Understand why primary-care and specialist coordination remains important after oncology treatment becomes less intensive.
10. Recognize psychosocial, functional, family, caregiver, work, and quality-of-life needs that may persist after treatment.
11. Understand that survivorship does not necessarily mean that a person is cancer-free and does not itself determine cancer status.
12. Know which questions should be discussed with the cancer care team during survivorship care.

# Scope

## Primary Educational Question

> **What does survivorship care mean after gastric cancer treatment, and what long-term health, functional, nutritional, psychosocial, and preventive needs may remain?**

PP-0229 is a patient-facing survivorship package. It explains the transition from active treatment toward longer-term health management, the long-term consequences that can follow gastric cancer treatment, survivorship care planning, prevention and health promotion, quality of life, and coordination among oncology, primary care, and relevant specialists.

Survivorship is treated as a broad care framework. It is not synonymous with surveillance, recurrence detection, or simply being cancer-free.

# Clinical Knowledge Blocks

## Knowledge Block 1 — What Is Gastric Cancer Survivorship?

Gastric cancer survivorship refers to the continuing health and care needs of people living after or through gastric cancer treatment. It is broader than disease surveillance.

A person can finish active treatment and still need care for nutritional problems, gastrointestinal changes, fatigue, neuropathy, bone health, physical function, emotional adjustment, preventive healthcare, and other long-term needs.

Survivorship therefore asks a different question from recurrence surveillance:

> **Not only “Is the cancer coming back?” but also “How is the person living after treatment, and what care is needed to protect long-term health and quality of life?”**

## Knowledge Block 2 — Survivorship Does Not Automatically Mean “Cancer-Free”

The word survivor does not by itself establish that cancer has been cured.

Some people complete treatment with no evidence of disease. Others may live with controlled or chronic cancer and continue treatment. Survivorship care can be relevant in both situations, although the clinical priorities differ.

Cancer status must be determined from the clinical assessment, not from the label “survivor.”

## Knowledge Block 3 — Why a Survivorship Care Plan Matters

A survivorship care plan organizes information that may otherwise be lost when treatment becomes less intensive.

The plan can record:

- treatments received;
- follow-up and surveillance plans;
- screening needs;
- acute, late, and long-term treatment effects;
- health risks;
- healthy-behavior recommendations;
- roles of oncology, primary care, and subspecialists;
- ongoing needs;
- resources and referrals.

This creates continuity between cancer treatment and longer-term health care.

## Knowledge Block 4 — Survivorship and Surveillance Are Different

Gastric-cancer-specific surveillance is one component of survivorship care.

NCCN states that routine gastric-cancer-specific surveillance includes radiologic imaging, endoscopic evaluation, and tumor markers, and is not recommended beyond 5 years. At the same time, survivorship care continues through routine medical care, preventive care, cancer screening, management of long-term sequelae, and health promotion.

Therefore:

> **Surveillance can end or change while survivorship needs continue.**

Detailed surveillance belongs to PP-0220, and recurrence detection belongs to PP-0221.

## Knowledge Block 5 — Weight Loss and Nutrition

Weight loss can remain an important issue after treatment, particularly after gastrectomy.

Survivorship care may include regular weight monitoring, attention to eating patterns, assessment of medical and psychosocial contributors, and referral to nutrition professionals when individualized support is needed.

The purpose is not simply to restore a number on a scale. Stable nutrition supports strength, function, recovery, and quality of life.

## Knowledge Block 6 — Long-term Nutritional Consequences After Gastrectomy

Gastrectomy can permanently change how a person eats and absorbs nutrients.

Long-term or lifelong issues can include deficiencies involving:

- vitamin B12;
- vitamin D;
- calcium;
- iron;
- zinc.

NCCN recommends ongoing monitoring and management of nutritional sequelae with appropriate practitioners and specialists. Monitoring and replacement may be individualized, particularly after subtotal gastrectomy.

This is one of the clearest gastric-cancer-specific survivorship needs.

## Knowledge Block 7 — Dumping Syndrome and Gastrointestinal Adaptation

Dumping syndrome can occur after total gastrectomy because food moves rapidly into the small bowel.

Other long-term gastrointestinal problems may include:

- postprandial fullness;
- diarrhea;
- reflux;
- eating restrictions;
- small-intestinal bacterial overgrowth.

These problems can affect nutrition, daily activities, and quality of life.

Detailed treatment protocols belong to dedicated supportive/nutrition packages.

## Knowledge Block 8 — Fatigue

Fatigue may persist after treatment and can have multiple contributors.

Assessment should consider:

- physical factors;
- nutritional problems;
- sleep;
- emotional distress;
- treatment effects;
- other medical conditions.

Physical activity and energy-conservation strategies may be useful when tolerated.

Persistent fatigue should not automatically be interpreted as recurrence, but unexplained or worsening symptoms should be discussed with the clinical team.

## Knowledge Block 9 — Chemotherapy-induced Neuropathy

Neuropathy can persist after chemotherapy and may affect:

- sensation;
- pain;
- balance;
- walking;
- fine motor tasks;
- fall risk;
- work and daily activities.

At the survivorship level, the focus is on persistent functional impact and appropriate rehabilitation or specialist referral.

Detailed toxicity treatment remains delegated to PP-0231.

## Knowledge Block 10 — Bone Health

Osteopenia and osteoporosis are recognized long-term survivorship concerns.

NCCN links these problems to deficiencies in vitamin D, calcium, phosphorus, and other nutrients and recommends regular bone-density assessment and management according to established national guidance, with vitamin D assessment/replacement when clinically indicated.

Bone health matters because fractures and loss of physical function can substantially affect long-term independence.

## Knowledge Block 11 — Quality of Life

Long-term quality of life can be affected by:

- diarrhea;
- dysphagia;
- reflux;
- eating restrictions;
- physical functioning;
- cognitive functioning;
- fatigue;
- nutritional problems;
- gastrointestinal dysfunction.

Survivorship care therefore should not define success solely as the absence of detectable cancer.

## Knowledge Block 12 — Physical Activity and Healthy Behaviors

NCCN survivorship guidance recommends:

- maintaining a healthy body weight;
- physical activity and avoiding inactivity;
- healthy diet;
- limiting alcohol;
- smoking cessation;
- preventive health measures and immunizations.

Activity recommendations should be adapted to treatment sequelae such as neuropathy and functional limitations.

These behaviors support general long-term health. They should not be presented as guarantees that gastric cancer will not recur.

## Knowledge Block 13 — Primary Care and Coordination

Survivorship is not an oncology-only responsibility.

NCCN encourages maintaining a therapeutic relationship with a primary-care physician throughout life and defining roles among oncology, primary care, and subspecialists.

This allows routine medical care to continue even when gastric-cancer-specific treatment and surveillance become less intensive.

## Knowledge Block 14 — Psychosocial Survivorship

Cancer can affect emotional health, identity, relationships, work, finances, and family life even after treatment.

Important survivorship concerns can include:

- fear of recurrence;
- anxiety;
- depression or distress;
- loss of confidence;
- social reintegration;
- work-related difficulties;
- caregiver burden.

The package provides recognition and referral concepts rather than detailed psychological treatment.

## Knowledge Block 15 — Second Primary Cancers and Preventive Screening

Survivorship care includes appropriate routine cancer screening and preventive healthcare.

This does not mean every survivor requires every screening test. Screening depends on age, risk factors, family history, prior treatments, and applicable guidelines.

PP-0229 therefore teaches the principle of continued preventive care rather than reproducing disease-specific screening algorithms.

## Knowledge Block 16 — Long-term Follow-up Is an Interface, Not the Same Package

Long-term follow-up is necessary to organize care over time, but it is not synonymous with survivorship.

PP-0229 defines the **needs and content** of survivorship.

PP-0230 owns the **operational long-term follow-up framework**.

This distinction prevents duplicate ownership.

# Patient Explanation

A useful way to think about survivorship is:

**Cancer treatment changes → the body and life may also change → some changes need monitoring or support → long-term health care continues.**

Finishing treatment does not mean that every treatment effect disappears immediately.

For some people, the most important survivorship issue may be nutrition after gastrectomy. For others, it may be fatigue, neuropathy, bone health, emotional recovery, work, physical function, or fear of recurrence.

The survivorship plan should therefore be individualized to the person's treatment history and current needs.

---

# Clinical Importance

Gastric cancer has particularly important survivorship considerations because gastrectomy can alter gastrointestinal anatomy and nutrition for the long term.

NCCN identifies common issues including weight loss, diarrhea, chemotherapy-induced neuropathy, fatigue, and bone-health problems. Gastrectomy survivors can also have long-term or lifelong nutritional and gastrointestinal consequences.

These problems may affect:

- nutrition;
- physical function;
- independence;
- emotional well-being;
- social participation;
- quality of life.

Recognizing them as survivorship issues helps prevent the false assumption that cancer treatment is the only clinically important phase of the cancer journey.

# Key Concepts

1. Survivorship is broader than surveillance.
2. Survivorship does not itself define cancer status.
3. A survivorship care plan preserves treatment history and future care needs.
4. Long-term treatment effects are part of survivorship.
5. Gastrectomy can create lifelong nutritional and gastrointestinal consequences.
6. Weight, nutrition, bone health, fatigue, neuropathy, and function may require ongoing attention.
7. Healthy behaviors are part of survivorship care.
8. Primary care remains important.
9. Psychosocial and caregiver needs are legitimate clinical needs.
10. Surveillance and recurrence detection remain separate packages.
11. Long-term follow-up is a separate operational package.
12. Individual survivorship needs should be assessed rather than assumed.

# Common Misconceptions

### Myth 1 — “If treatment is finished, cancer-related care is finished.”

**Fact:** Some treatment effects and health risks can persist for years or lifelong.

### Myth 2 — “Survivorship means I am definitely cured.”

**Fact:** Survivorship is a care concept; it does not by itself establish cure or cancer status.

### Myth 3 — “Survivorship is just surveillance.”

**Fact:** Surveillance is only one component of survivorship.

### Myth 4 — “If gastric-cancer surveillance changes after several years, I no longer need medical care.”

**Fact:** Routine medical care, prevention, screening, and long-term sequelae management continue.

### Myth 5 — “Nutritional problems after gastrectomy are only temporary.”

**Fact:** Some nutritional consequences can require long-term or lifelong monitoring.

### Myth 6 — “Persistent fatigue automatically means recurrence.”

**Fact:** Fatigue has many possible causes and should be clinically assessed.

### Myth 7 — “Neuropathy always disappears when chemotherapy stops.”

**Fact:** Neuropathy can persist and affect function.

### Myth 8 — “Healthy lifestyle advice guarantees that cancer will not return.”

**Fact:** Healthy behaviors support overall health but do not guarantee prevention of recurrence.

### Myth 9 — “My oncologist must manage every health problem forever.”

**Fact:** Survivorship commonly involves coordination among oncology, primary care, and subspecialists.

### Myth 10 — “Quality of life is secondary once treatment is complete.”

**Fact:** Function, nutrition, symptoms, emotional health, and quality of life are central survivorship concerns.

# Patient Questions to Bring to the Care Team

1. What treatment did I receive, and what long-term effects should I watch for?
2. Do I need a written survivorship care plan?
3. How long will gastric-cancer-specific surveillance continue?
4. What symptoms should I report promptly?
5. How should my weight and nutrition be monitored?
6. Do I need monitoring for vitamin B12, iron, vitamin D, calcium, or other deficiencies?
7. Do I need bone-density assessment?
8. Could my fatigue or neuropathy have long-term treatment-related causes?
9. Would nutrition, rehabilitation, occupational, or physical therapy help me?
10. Which care should be provided by my oncologist, primary-care physician, or another specialist?
11. What preventive screening and vaccinations should I continue?
12. What lifestyle changes are realistic and appropriate for me?
13. What support is available for anxiety, fear of recurrence, work, finances, or family concerns?
14. Who should I contact if my needs change after oncology visits become less frequent?

# Key Messages

> **Survivorship is not simply “the period after cancer.” It is ongoing care for the person's long-term health, function, well-being, and quality of life.**

> **After gastrectomy, some nutritional and gastrointestinal consequences may require long-term or lifelong monitoring.**

> **Surveillance, recurrence detection, and survivorship are related but different clinical functions.**

> **A survivorship care plan helps connect cancer treatment history with long-term health care.**

> **Primary care, oncology, and relevant specialists may all have important roles in survivorship.**

> **Healthy behaviors support long-term health, but they do not guarantee that cancer will not recur.**

# Clinical Safety Boundary

This package is educational. It does not determine whether an individual is cured, whether recurrence is present, whether a symptom is caused by cancer or treatment, or which treatment or surveillance schedule an individual should receive.

Persistent, new, worsening, or concerning symptoms require assessment by the appropriate clinical team rather than self-attribution to survivorship.
