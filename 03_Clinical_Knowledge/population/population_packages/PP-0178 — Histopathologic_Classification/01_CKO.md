## B. Educational Objectives

After reading this Population Package, the reader should be able to:

1.  Explain what histopathology means in gastric cancer.
2.  Distinguish biopsy from histopathologic examination.
3.  Understand what histologic classification means.
4.  Distinguish histologic type from tumor grade and TNM stage.
5.  Recognize the major intestinal and diffuse patterns of gastric
    adenocarcinoma.
6.  Understand tubular, papillary, mucinous, signet-ring-cell and poorly
    cohesive terminology at a patient-facing level.
7.  Understand why specimen type affects the amount of pathological
    information available.
8.  Understand invasion, margins, lymphovascular invasion and lymph-node
    findings as pathology concepts.
9.  Understand the relationship between histopathology, Lauren
    classification, molecular classification and biomarker testing.
10. Interpret the limitations of a small or nonrepresentative specimen
    without over-interpreting a pathology result.
11. Understand why pathology may be refined after additional tissue, IHC
    or molecular testing.
12. Recognize that histopathology is a major component of diagnosis but
    does not independently determine stage, prognosis or treatment.

## D. Clinical Knowledge Blocks

### CKB-01 --- What Is Histopathology?

**Patient Explanation**

Histopathology is the examination and interpretation of tissue at the
microscopic level. For suspected gastric cancer, tissue obtained by
biopsy or resection is examined so that the pathologist can determine
whether malignant tissue is present and characterize its microscopic
features. Histopathology therefore sits between tissue acquisition and
the later clinical integration of diagnosis, staging and biomarker
information.

**Clinical Importance**

It converts a tissue specimen into a structured pathological
description. It is not the same thing as the biopsy procedure, the
pathology report as a whole, or the cancer stage.

**Key Concepts**

-   Histopathology describes what is seen in the sampled tissue.
-   The available specimen determines how complete the pathological
    assessment can be.
-   Histologic type, grade and invasion answer different questions.
-   Pathology findings must be integrated with the wider gastric-cancer
    work-up.

**Evidence Anchor**

NCCN identifies histologic type, grade and invasion as core
pathology-report elements; ESMO--ASCO places histology, grading,
pathology reporting and clinically relevant microscopic features within
core oncology pathology competencies.

### CKB-02 --- How Does Pathology Examine a Gastric Biopsy?

**Patient Explanation**

A biopsy provides a sample of tissue. Histopathology then evaluates the
sampled tissue microscopically and records the features needed to
characterize the lesion. The exact information available depends on how
much tissue was obtained and whether the specimen is a small biopsy, an
endoscopic resection specimen or a gastrectomy specimen.

**Clinical Importance**

A small biopsy can establish important diagnostic information but may
not show the full architecture or extent of a tumor. This is why
pathology findings must always be interpreted in relation to the
specimen type.

**Key Concepts**

-   Histopathology describes what is seen in the sampled tissue.
-   The available specimen determines how complete the pathological
    assessment can be.
-   Histologic type, grade and invasion answer different questions.
-   Pathology findings must be integrated with the wider gastric-cancer
    work-up.

**Evidence Anchor**

NCCN uses specimen-specific pathology reporting requirements for biopsy,
EMR and gastrectomy specimens.

### CKB-03 --- What Is Histologic Classification?

**Patient Explanation**

Histologic classification organizes tumors into categories according to
their microscopic appearance and structural features. In gastric
adenocarcinoma, classification may describe the predominant cellular and
architectural pattern rather than the anatomic stage of the disease.

**Clinical Importance**

Classification is one layer of the pathological assessment. It should
not be confused with grade, TNM stage or molecular classification.

**Key Concepts**

-   Histopathology describes what is seen in the sampled tissue.
-   The available specimen determines how complete the pathological
    assessment can be.
-   Histologic type, grade and invasion answer different questions.
-   Pathology findings must be integrated with the wider gastric-cancer
    work-up.

**Evidence Anchor**

NCI and NCCN describe intestinal and diffuse histologic patterns;
ESMO--ASCO emphasizes interpretation of histologic and WHO
classification systems.

### CKB-04 --- What Is a Histologic Type?

**Patient Explanation**

A histologic type is a category based on what tumor cells and their
architecture look like under the microscope. Examples relevant to
gastric adenocarcinoma include intestinal and diffuse patterns, with
additional morphologic forms such as tubular, papillary, mucinous and
signet-ring-cell morphology described in the source set.

**Clinical Importance**

Histologic type tells clinicians what the tumor looks like
microscopically. It is different from how far the cancer has spread.

**Key Concepts**

-   Histopathology describes what is seen in the sampled tissue.
-   The available specimen determines how complete the pathological
    assessment can be.
-   Histologic type, grade and invasion answer different questions.
-   Pathology findings must be integrated with the wider gastric-cancer
    work-up.

**Evidence Anchor**

NCCN states that gastric adenocarcinomas are typically classified by
anatomic location and histologic type, including diffuse and intestinal
types.

### CKB-05 --- What Is Gastric Adenocarcinoma Under the Microscope?

**Patient Explanation**

More than 95% of gastric cancers are adenocarcinomas. Microscopically,
adenocarcinoma is characterized by malignant epithelial cells with
patterns that can include glandular or tubular formation, discohesive
cells, mucin production or other defined morphologic features.

**Clinical Importance**

The microscopic pattern helps establish the diagnosis and provides
information that can affect subsequent classification and testing.

**Key Concepts**

-   Histopathology describes what is seen in the sampled tissue.
-   The available specimen determines how complete the pathological
    assessment can be.
-   Histologic type, grade and invasion answer different questions.
-   Pathology findings must be integrated with the wider gastric-cancer
    work-up.

**Evidence Anchor**

NCCN and NCI identify adenocarcinoma as the dominant gastric malignancy
and describe major intestinal and diffuse patterns.

### CKB-06 --- What Is the Intestinal Pattern?

**Patient Explanation**

The intestinal pattern tends to form a mass lesion and is characterized
by variably differentiated tumor cells arranged in tubular or glandular
patterns, with scattered goblet cells described by NCCN. NCI similarly
describes intestinal adenocarcinomas as generally showing
tubular/glandular architecture.

**Clinical Importance**

The term intestinal describes the tumor's microscopic pattern; it does
not mean that the cancer originated in the intestine.

**Key Concepts**

-   Histopathology describes what is seen in the sampled tissue.
-   The available specimen determines how complete the pathological
    assessment can be.
-   Histologic type, grade and invasion answer different questions.
-   Pathology findings must be integrated with the wider gastric-cancer
    work-up.

**Evidence Anchor**

NCCN and NCI support the intestinal pattern and its tubular/glandular
architecture.

### CKB-07 --- What Is the Diffuse Pattern?

**Patient Explanation**

The diffuse pattern is characterized by poorly differentiated and
discohesive tumor cells that infiltrate the gastric wall. Cells may have
signet-ring or non-signet-ring morphology. Diffuse tumors may therefore
lack the obvious glandular architecture that is characteristic of many
intestinal-type tumors.

**Clinical Importance**

Diffuse is a histologic pattern, not a synonym for metastatic disease or
stage IV disease.

**Key Concepts**

-   Histopathology describes what is seen in the sampled tissue.
-   The available specimen determines how complete the pathological
    assessment can be.
-   Histologic type, grade and invasion answer different questions.
-   Pathology findings must be integrated with the wider gastric-cancer
    work-up.

**Evidence Anchor**

NCCN describes diffuse gastric adenocarcinoma as poorly differentiated
and discohesive with diffuse infiltration of the gastric wall; NCI
describes diffuse tumors as poorly/undifferentiated and lacking gland
formation.

### CKB-08 --- What Are Mixed Histologic Features?

**Patient Explanation**

Not every gastric adenocarcinoma fits a single clean microscopic
pattern. Mixed intestinal and diffuse features may occur. This matters
because classification is a structured description of morphology rather
than a statement that every tumor is biologically uniform.

**Clinical Importance**

Mixed morphology should not be interpreted as an error in pathology. It
reflects the fact that tumor architecture can contain more than one
recognizable pattern.

**Key Concepts**

### CKB-09 --- What Are Tubular, Papillary and Mucinous Patterns?

**Patient Explanation**

Tubular, papillary and mucinous forms are morphologic patterns that can
occur within gastric adenocarcinoma. Tubular/glandular architecture is
particularly characteristic of the intestinal pattern. Mucinous
morphology reflects prominent mucin production as a defining microscopic
feature.

**Clinical Importance**

These terms describe microscopic structure and composition. They should
not automatically be treated as independent statements about stage or

### CKB-10 --- What Is Signet-Ring-Cell Carcinoma?

**Patient Explanation**

Signet-ring cells are tumor cells in which abundant intracellular mucin
displaces the nucleus toward the cell periphery, producing a
characteristic microscopic appearance. Signet-ring morphology is
particularly associated with poorly cohesive/diffuse gastric carcinoma
and is also important in hereditary diffuse gastric cancer.

**Clinical Importance**

Signet-ring morphology is a histologic observation. It does not by
itself establish a hereditary syndrome; hereditary interpretation
requires separate clinical and genetic assessment.

**Key Concepts**

-   Histopathology describes what is seen in the sampled tissue.
-   The available specimen determines how complete the pathological
    assessment can be.
-   Histologic type, grade and invasion answer different questions.
-   Pathology findings must be integrated with the wider gastric-cancer
    work-up.

**Evidence Anchor**

NCCN describes diffuse tumors with signet-ring or non-signet-ring
morphology; NCI HDGC PDQ describes intramucosal signet-ring-cell
carcinoma in hereditary diffuse gastric cancer.

### CKB-11 --- What Does Poorly Cohesive Mean?

**Patient Explanation**

**Clinical Importance**

### CKB-12 --- What Is Linitis Plastica From a Pathology Perspective?

**Patient Explanation**

Diffuse gastric carcinoma can infiltrate the gastric wall rather than
forming a discrete mass. When extensive infiltration produces a
thickened, rigid stomach wall, the clinical-pathologic entity is
commonly referred to as linitis plastica.

**Clinical Importance**

Linitis plastica is a pattern of diffuse infiltrative disease. It should
not be used as a synonym for every diffuse-type gastric cancer.

**Clinical Importance**

### CKB-14 --- Why Does Depth of Invasion Matter?

The depth to which gastric cancer extends into the wall is clinically

classification. However, a small biopsy often cannot determine the full
depth of invasion of the entire tumor.

**Clinical Importance**

### CKB-16 --- What Does Differentiation Mean?

**Patient Explanation**

Differentiation describes how closely malignant cells and their
organization resemble the normal tissue from which they arose.
Well-differentiated tumors retain more recognizable tissue-like
organization, whereas poorly differentiated tumors retain less
recognizable organization.

**Clinical Importance**

Differentiation is related to grade but is not identical to histologic
type. A tumor can have a named histologic pattern and also receive a
grade/differentiation assessment.

### CKB-17 --- What Is the Difference Between Histologic Type and Grade?

**Patient Explanation**

Histologic type asks what pattern the tumor has. Grade asks how
differentiated the tumor appears within the applicable grading
framework. They are separate dimensions of pathology.

**Clinical Importance**

A report may therefore contain both a histologic type and a grade.
Neither is the same as TNM stage.

**Key Concepts**

### CKB-18 --- Why Can Biopsy and Resection Pathology Differ in Detail?

**Patient Explanation**

A biopsy samples only part of a lesion. An EMR/ESD or gastrectomy
specimen can contain much more tissue and architecture, allowing the
pathologist to assess additional features such as depth, margins,
vascular/lymphatic invasion and lymph nodes.

**Clinical Importance**

More detailed information in a later resection report does not
necessarily mean the original biopsy was wrong. The specimens answer
different levels of pathological information.

**Clinical Importance**

### CKB-32 --- Why Does Histologic Classification Not Determine Treatment by Itself?

**Patient Explanation**

A histologic label is only one component of the overall clinical
picture. Treatment planning requires integration of stage,
resectability, biomarker results, molecular information, prior treatment

### CKB-37 --- What Is the Difference Between Dysplasia and Invasive Carcinoma?

**Patient Explanation**

tissue compartments.

**Clinical Importance**

## Common Misconceptions

### Myth

**"Histopathology is just another word for biopsy."**

**Fact:** Biopsy is the process of obtaining tissue. Histopathology is
the microscopic examination and interpretation of that tissue.

### Myth

**"If the endoscopy looks like cancer, pathology is only a formality."**

**Fact:** Endoscopy identifies a suspicious lesion, but pathology
establishes what the sampled tissue actually represents.

### Myth

**"Histologic type is the same as cancer stage."**

**Fact:** Histologic type describes microscopic appearance. Stage
describes the extent of disease.

### Myth

**"Poorly differentiated means stage IV."**

**Fact:** Poor differentiation is a grading/differentiation concept. It
does not by itself establish metastatic disease.

### Myth

**"Diffuse type means metastatic cancer."**

**Fact:** Diffuse is a histologic growth pattern. It does not by itself
define metastasis.

### Myth

**"Intestinal type means the cancer came from the intestine."**

**Fact:** Intestinal describes a microscopic pattern of gastric
adenocarcinoma.

### Myth

**"Signet-ring cells prove hereditary gastric cancer."**

**Fact:** Signet-ring morphology can occur without a hereditary
syndrome. Hereditary assessment requires clinical and genetic context.

### Myth

**"One biopsy gives the complete pathology of the whole tumor."**

**Fact:** A biopsy samples only part of a lesion. Larger specimens can
provide additional architecture and pathological features.

### Myth

**"A more detailed resection report means the biopsy was wrong."**

**Fact:** The specimens contain different amounts and types of
information. A resection can legitimately provide more complete
characterization.

### Myth

**"Margins can be assessed from any biopsy."**

**Fact:** Margin assessment applies to appropriate resection specimens,
not a routine forceps biopsy.

### Myth

**"Lymph-node status is part of every pathology specimen."**

**Fact:** Lymph-node assessment requires lymph nodes in the specimen,
such as a gastrectomy specimen.

### Myth

**"Histology alone determines treatment."**

**Fact:** Treatment integrates histology with stage, biomarkers,
molecular findings and patient-specific factors.

### Myth

**"Lauren classification and molecular classification are the same."**

**Fact:** Lauren is a histologic framework; molecular classification
describes molecular/genomic characteristics.

### Myth

**"A negative or limited biopsy proves there is no cancer."**

**Fact:** Sampling limitations mean that a biopsy result must be
interpreted in the context of the lesion and specimen adequacy.

### Myth

**"Pathology terminology never changes."**

**Fact:** Additional review, tissue, IHC or molecular information can
refine the pathological characterization.

## Key Messages

-   Histopathology is the microscopic characterization of tissue
    obtained by biopsy or resection.
-   Histologic type, tumor grade and invasion are distinct components of
    pathological assessment.
-   Gastric adenocarcinoma includes major intestinal and diffuse
    morphologic patterns.
-   Diffuse-type disease may show discohesive or signet-ring morphology
    and can infiltrate the gastric wall.
-   A biopsy may establish cancer while remaining limited for complete
    assessment of invasion, margins or lymph nodes.
-   Larger EMR/ESD and gastrectomy specimens allow additional
    pathological features to be assessed.
-   Histopathology contributes to staging but is not the same as TNM
    stage.
-   Lauren classification, molecular classification and biomarker
    testing are complementary downstream layers.
-   Histology may inform clinical context and treatment planning but
    does not independently determine treatment.
-   Pathology findings should be interpreted together with the clinical,
    endoscopic, imaging and molecular context.
