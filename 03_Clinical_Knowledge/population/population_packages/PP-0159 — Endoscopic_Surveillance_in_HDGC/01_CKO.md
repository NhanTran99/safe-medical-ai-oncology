## Educational Objective

Explain how endoscopic surveillance is used in hereditary diffuse gastric cancer (HDGC) when the stomach is retained, who may undergo surveillance, why HDGC is difficult to detect endoscopically, why systematic/random and targeted biopsies are used, how surveillance intervals are approached, what negative and positive findings mean, and when findings should trigger multidisciplinary reassessment.

## Scope

### Included

- HDGC-specific endoscopic surveillance.
- CDH1 pathogenic-variant carriers who defer or decline risk-reducing total gastrectomy (RRTG).
- Younger CDH1 carriers for whom RRTG is not yet recommended.
- Selected HDGC-like/high-risk families when surveillance is individualized.
- Expert high-quality endoscopic examination.
- Recognition of subtle mucosal findings.
- Targeted and systematic/random biopsies.
- Cambridge and Bethesda protocol concepts.
- Annual / 6–12-month surveillance context.
- Interpretation of negative endoscopy.
- Signet-ring-cell findings and multidisciplinary handoff.
- Limitations of surveillance.
- Surveillance versus RRTG.

### Not Included

- Exact HDGC genetic-testing criteria.
- CDH1/CTNNA1 variant interpretation.
- Genetic counseling.
- Cascade testing.
- RRTG operative technique.
- Detailed pathology classification.
- Generic endoscopy preparation or generic biopsy technique.
- Generic gastric-cancer screening.
- Individualized cancer-risk calculation.
- Chemotherapy/radiotherapy.
- Jurisdiction-specific legal or financial advice.

## Clinical Knowledge Blocks

### KB1 — What Is Endoscopic Surveillance in HDGC?

HDGC-specific endoscopic surveillance is scheduled specialist examination of the retained stomach in people at elevated hereditary gastric-cancer risk when risk-reducing gastrectomy is deferred, declined, not yet appropriate, or when a defined HDGC-like pathway calls for surveillance.

It is not the same as routine diagnostic endoscopy or average-risk gastric-cancer screening.

### KB2 — Who May Undergo Surveillance?

NCI describes surveillance as an option for:

- CDH1 pathogenic-variant carriers who decline or defer RRTG;
- younger CDH1 carriers;
- selected people from families suggestive of HDGC without an identified pathogenic variant;
- selected families with intestinal-type gastric cancer, where surveillance is individualized.

The risk context is not equivalent across these groups.

### KB3 — Why Is Surveillance Needed?

RRTG remains the gold-standard risk-reduction approach for HDGC, but some patients decline or defer surgery. In these situations, surveillance provides an active monitoring pathway while the stomach is retained.

### KB4 — Why Is HDGC Difficult to Detect?

Early HDGC can consist of microscopic signet-ring cell carcinoma (SRCC) beneath apparently normal gastric epithelium. NCI states that there are no definitive endoscopic features that reliably identify early diffuse gastric cancer or its precursors.

Therefore:

> A normal-looking stomach does not reliably exclude microscopic HDGC.

### KB5 — What Does the Endoscopist Look For?

Potentially suspicious findings include:

- pale spots;
- erythema;
- nodularity/nodules;
- polyps;
- erosions;
- scars;
- ulcers;
- other subtle mucosal abnormalities.

Visual inspection alone is insufficient because the epithelium overlying SRCC may appear normal.

### KB6 — Why Are Random/Systematic Biopsies Important?

NCI reports that targeted biopsies alone have low yield for microscopic SRCC and that random biopsies improve detection.

This is a defining feature of HDGC surveillance:

> **Targeted biopsy + systematic/random sampling**

rather than targeted biopsy alone.

### KB7 — Targeted Versus Random Biopsy

Targeted biopsies sample visible abnormalities. Systematic/random biopsies sample mucosa that may look normal.

NCI reports that targeted biopsies of ulcers, erythema, nodularity, polyps, erosions and scars identified SRCC in fewer than 10% of samples, while random biopsies improved detection.

### KB8 — Cambridge and Bethesda Approaches

Published surveillance cohorts have used named protocols including the Cambridge and Bethesda approaches. NCI summarizes a prospective cohort of 270 asymptomatic CDH1 carriers in which 9% underwent the Cambridge protocol and 91% the Bethesda protocol.

The package should explain these as surveillance protocol concepts rather than implying one universally mandatory protocol.

### KB9 — How Often Is Surveillance Performed?

NCI states that if an endoscopic approach is used for HDGC management, annual surveillance is recommended unless signet-ring-cell features are found.

The prospective cohort summarized by NCI used recommended intervals of approximately 6–12 months.

For HDGC-like families, NCI describes yearly surveillance for at least 2 years, with later interval individualization based on previous findings and family history.

### KB10 — What Does a Negative Endoscopy Mean?

A negative or normal-appearing endoscopy does not prove that microscopic HDGC is absent.

NCI describes CDH1 carriers with normal EGDs and negative random biopsies who subsequently had microscopic SRCC identified in RRTG specimens.

Therefore:

> Negative surveillance reduces detected evidence of disease; it does not eliminate hereditary gastric-cancer risk.

### KB11 — What If a Suspicious Lesion Is Found?

Suspicious abnormalities should undergo appropriate targeted biopsy and pathology assessment.

The package does not replace detailed pathology interpretation.

### KB12 — What If Signet-Ring Cells Are Found?

NCI recommends multidisciplinary discussion when signet-ring-cell lesions are found during surveillance.

The finding is clinically important, but the natural history of endoscopically detected foci is not fully defined. Therefore the result should not be converted into an automatic simplistic treatment rule.

### KB13 — Role of Enhanced Imaging

Enhanced imaging may help characterize subtle abnormalities. NCI describes historical chromoendoscopy experience and also illustrates small pale lesions detectable with narrow-band imaging.

Historical Congo red–methylene blue chromoendoscopy increased biopsy yield in one study, but concerns about Congo red carcinogenicity meant that this approach did not persist.

Enhanced imaging should therefore be presented as an adjunct, not as a replacement for systematic biopsy.

### KB14 — Why Expert Endoscopy Matters

HDGC surveillance is technically different from routine EGD because microscopic disease may be invisible and because systematic sampling is required.

The surveillance pathway should therefore be delivered by clinicians/centers experienced with hereditary gastric cancer when available.

### KB15 — Limitations of HDGC Surveillance

Key limitations include:

- microscopic disease may be invisible;
- targeted biopsy alone has poor sensitivity;
- random biopsy can still miss lesions;
- a negative examination does not eliminate hereditary risk;
- the optimal long-term surveillance strategy remains an evolving evidence area;
- progression of intramucosal signet-ring lesions to advanced cancer is unpredictable.

### KB16 — Surveillance Versus RRTG

RRTG removes high-risk gastric tissue and remains the gold-standard risk-reduction strategy.

Surveillance retains the stomach and attempts to identify disease through repeated expert endoscopy and biopsy.

They should not be presented as equivalent strategies.

### KB17 — Surveillance in HDGC-like Families

Selected HDGC-like families may undergo surveillance even without an identified pathogenic variant. The classification of HDGC-like status belongs to PP-0153; PP-0159 owns the surveillance pathway once surveillance is selected.

### KB18 — When Surveillance Leads to Reassessment

Important findings, particularly signet-ring-cell lesions, should lead to multidisciplinary reassessment and possible reconsideration of risk-reducing management.

The detailed RRTG decision belongs to PP-0158.

## Patient Explanation

> If you have a hereditary risk of diffuse gastric cancer and your stomach has not been removed, your doctors may recommend regular specialist endoscopy. This is different from an ordinary endoscopy because early HDGC can consist of tiny areas of cancer that may look completely normal.
>
> During HDGC surveillance, the endoscopist looks carefully for subtle abnormalities and takes both targeted biopsies of suspicious areas and systematic/random biopsies from the stomach. This improves the chance of finding microscopic disease, but it does not guarantee that every focus will be detected.
>
> If surveillance finds signet-ring-cell changes or another concerning result, the finding should be reviewed by an experienced multidisciplinary team. A normal examination does not mean that hereditary risk has disappeared.

## Clinical Importance

This package addresses a high-stakes surveillance strategy in which:

- disease can be microscopic and visually occult;
- biopsy strategy materially affects detection;
- surveillance is an alternative to, rather than a proven equivalent of, RRTG;
- negative examinations have important limitations;
- positive findings require multidisciplinary interpretation.

## Common Misconceptions

### Myth 1
A normal endoscopy means there is no HDGC.

**Fact:** Microscopic SRCC can be present beneath normal-appearing mucosa.

### Myth 2
Looking for visible lesions is enough.

**Fact:** Systematic/random biopsies are important because microscopic disease may not be visible.

### Myth 3
A negative biopsy means the stomach is cancer-free.

**Fact:** Sampling can miss microscopic disease.

### Myth 4
Surveillance is equivalent to preventive gastrectomy.

**Fact:** RRTG removes high-risk gastric tissue; surveillance retains the stomach and attempts to detect disease.

### Myth 5
Finding signet-ring cells automatically gives one universal treatment answer.

**Fact:** Findings should prompt expert multidisciplinary assessment because the natural history of endoscopically detected foci is uncertain.

### Myth 6
HDGC surveillance is the same as routine gastric-cancer screening.

**Fact:** HDGC surveillance is a specialized hereditary-risk pathway with systematic biopsy requirements.

## Key Messages

- HDGC surveillance is a specialized endoscopic pathway for people who retain the stomach.
- It is particularly relevant when CDH1 carriers defer or decline RRTG.
- Early HDGC may be microscopic and invisible to routine visual inspection.
- Targeted biopsy alone is insufficient; systematic/random biopsy is important.
- Annual surveillance is recommended when an endoscopic HDGC approach is used, with published prospective cohorts using 6–12-month intervals.
- A negative endoscopy does not eliminate hereditary gastric-cancer risk.
- Signet-ring-cell findings require multidisciplinary review.
- Surveillance is not established as universally equivalent to RRTG.
- HDGC-like surveillance requires individualized interpretation of the underlying risk.
- Detailed genetic testing, counseling, RRTG, and pathology belong to adjacent packages.
