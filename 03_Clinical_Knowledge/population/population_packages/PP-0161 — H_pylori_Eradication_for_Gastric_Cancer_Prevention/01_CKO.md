## Educational Objectives

After reading this package, the reader should be able to:
- understand what H. pylori eradication means in gastric-cancer prevention;
- understand the main clinical contexts in which eradication is relevant;
- understand the principles of regimen selection, treatment completion, and confirmation of eradication;
- understand treatment failure, recurrence/reinfection, and antimicrobial-resistance considerations;
- understand why successful eradication lowers but does not eliminate future gastric-cancer risk.

## Clinical Knowledge Blocks

### KB1 — What Is H. pylori Eradication?

Eradication means treating an H. pylori infection with the goal of eliminating the bacterium. In this package, the focus is the use of eradication as a gastric-cancer prevention intervention.

### KB2 — Why Is Eradication Used for Cancer Prevention?

Chronic H. pylori infection is an established cause of gastric adenocarcinoma. Removing the infection removes an ongoing carcinogenic stimulus and can reduce future gastric-cancer incidence.

### KB3 — Primary Versus Secondary Prevention

**Primary prevention:** an infected person without gastric cancer receives appropriate eradication to reduce future risk.

**Secondary prevention:** a person previously treated for early gastric cancer receives eradication to reduce the risk of metachronous gastric cancer.

### KB4 — Who May Benefit?

Evidence supports benefit in:
- asymptomatic infected individuals;
- selected higher-risk populations, including people with a family history of gastric cancer;
- patients after treatment of early gastric cancer;
- selected patients with established precursor changes.

The decision to test and treat should remain clinically appropriate rather than being interpreted as an automatic universal policy.

### KB5 — Eradication After Early Gastric Cancer

NCCN states that patients positive for H. pylori who undergo curative endoscopic resection or gastric subtotal resection should receive an eradication regimen. NCI also summarizes randomized evidence showing lower metachronous gastric-cancer risk after eradication.

### KB6 — Eradication With Atrophic Gastritis or Intestinal Metaplasia

Eradication may remain relevant when these precursor changes are already present. However, eliminating H. pylori does not necessarily reverse established mucosal damage or eliminate future cancer risk.

Detailed management belongs to PP-0165 and PP-0166.

### KB7 — How Is a Regimen Selected?

The current source set supports the principle that regimen choice and duration should follow contemporary H. pylori guidance.

NCCN specifically directs clinicians to the latest American College of Gastroenterology or Maastricht Consensus H. pylori guidance.

This package therefore does not convert historical trial regimens into current universal prescriptions.

### KB8 — Why Historical Trial Regimens Must Not Be Treated as Current Prescriptions

The prevention evidence includes historical regimens such as amoxicillin plus omeprazole in the Shandong randomized trial and lansoprazole, amoxicillin, and clarithromycin in a family-history trial.

These regimens demonstrate the preventive effect of eradication in those trials. They should not automatically be presented as the current standard regimen.

### KB9 — Treatment Completion and Adherence

Successful eradication requires appropriate treatment to be taken as prescribed. Incomplete treatment can result in persistent infection and may contribute to antimicrobial resistance.

### KB10 — How Is Eradication Success Considered?

Completion of treatment does not by itself prove that H. pylori has been eliminated. Confirmation of eradication is an important clinical concept.

The current source set does not provide a sufficiently detailed contemporary test-of-cure protocol; exact test selection and timing are therefore outside this Gold package.

### KB11 — What If Treatment Fails?

Persistent infection should prompt clinical reassessment rather than automatic repetition of the same regimen. Subsequent treatment should take account of prior therapy and current H. pylori treatment guidance.

The current source set does not support a detailed contemporary salvage algorithm.

### KB12 — Antimicrobial Resistance

Unnecessary or inappropriate eradication can contribute to antibiotic resistance. Prevention therefore requires balancing potential gastric-cancer benefit with appropriate antimicrobial stewardship.

### KB13 — Reinfection, Recurrence, and Household Transmission

NCI notes that transmission mechanisms and reinfection/recurrence rates vary and remain incompletely understood. A small randomized study suggested lower recurrent positivity when infected family members were also treated.

This does not establish a universal rule that every household member should receive eradication.

### KB14 — Family History

Family history can increase baseline gastric-cancer risk. Evidence from a randomized trial in people with a first-degree relative with gastric cancer supports a prevention benefit from eradication.

Family history itself belongs to PP-0155.

### KB15 — Does Eradication Eliminate Cancer Risk?

No. Successful eradication reduces risk but does not guarantee that gastric cancer will never occur. Residual risk may be particularly relevant when advanced precursor changes or previous gastric cancer are present.

### KB16 — What Eradication Can and Cannot Prevent

Eradication can:
- eliminate H. pylori infection when successful;
- reduce future gastric-cancer risk;
- reduce metachronous gastric-cancer risk in relevant post-treatment populations.

Eradication cannot:
- guarantee zero future cancer risk;
- necessarily reverse established atrophy/metaplasia;
- replace risk-appropriate follow-up;
- justify indiscriminate antibiotic treatment.

### KB17 — Practical Prevention Pathway

**Appropriate H. pylori identification**
→ **clinical decision to eradicate**
→ **current-guideline-based regimen selection**
→ **complete treatment**
→ **confirm eradication when appropriate**
→ **manage residual risk / precursor conditions as indicated**.

## Patient Explanation

> H. pylori eradication means treating an H. pylori infection with the goal of clearing the bacterium. Because chronic H. pylori infection can cause gastric cancer, successful eradication can lower future cancer risk.
>
> The exact treatment should be chosen using current H. pylori treatment guidance, because antibiotic resistance and previous antibiotic exposure can affect which regimen is appropriate. Historical research trials used specific antibiotic combinations, but those trial regimens should not automatically be used as today's standard treatment.
>
> Finishing treatment does not necessarily prove that the infection has been cleared. Your clinician may recommend confirmation of eradication. Even after successful eradication, some people remain at increased risk because previous gastric damage or previous gastric cancer may leave residual risk.

## Common Misconceptions

### Myth 1
“Any historical H. pylori antibiotic regimen is still a current standard.”

**Fact:** Contemporary regimen choice should follow current H. pylori guidance.

### Myth 2
“If I finish the antibiotics, the infection is definitely gone.”

**Fact:** Treatment completion and confirmed eradication are different concepts.

### Myth 3
“Eradication means I can never develop gastric cancer.”

**Fact:** Eradication lowers risk but does not guarantee zero future risk.

### Myth 4
“If treatment fails, I should simply repeat the same antibiotics.”

**Fact:** Treatment failure requires reassessment and guideline-based selection of subsequent therapy.

### Myth 5
“Everyone in my family should automatically receive antibiotics.”

**Fact:** Family transmission may matter, but the current evidence does not establish universal family-wide eradication.

### Myth 6
“More antibiotic treatment is always better for prevention.”

**Fact:** Unnecessary treatment can contribute to antimicrobial resistance.

## Key Messages

- H. pylori eradication is an important gastric-cancer prevention intervention.
- It can reduce gastric-cancer incidence and metachronous gastric cancer in relevant populations.
- NCCN recommends eradication for H. pylori-positive patients after curative endoscopic resection or gastric subtotal resection.
- Current treatment selection should follow contemporary H. pylori guidance.
- Historical trial regimens are evidence examples, not universal current prescriptions.
- Treatment completion does not necessarily prove eradication.
- Treatment failure requires reassessment.
- Antimicrobial stewardship is part of safe prevention.
- Reinfection and household transmission remain context-dependent.
- Successful eradication lowers but does not eliminate gastric-cancer risk.
