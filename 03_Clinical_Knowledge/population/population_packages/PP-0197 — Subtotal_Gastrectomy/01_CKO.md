## Educational Objectives

- Explain what subtotal gastrectomy means and how it differs from total gastrectomy.
- Understand why the operation may be considered when gastric cancer can be removed adequately while part of the stomach is preserved.
- Recognize the importance of tumor location, extent, resectability, and adequate microscopic margins.
- Understand that oncologic gastrectomy generally includes management of regional lymph nodes, while detailed lymphadenectomy is a separate topic.
- Understand distal and selected proximal subtotal gastrectomy at a conceptual, patient-facing level.
- Understand that reconstruction is needed to restore continuity of the digestive tract after part of the stomach is removed.
- Understand that open, laparoscopic, and robotic approaches may be considered in selected settings and that experience matters.
- Recognize major short-term risks and longer-term digestive and nutritional consequences without turning the package into a complication-management manual.
- Distinguish curative-intent subtotal gastrectomy from selected palliative gastric resection.
- Understand why subtotal gastrectomy is not appropriate for every gastric cancer and why the final operation is individualized by the treating team.

## Clinical Knowledge Blocks

### 1. What is subtotal gastrectomy?

Subtotal gastrectomy, also called partial gastrectomy, means that only part of the stomach is removed. The purpose in gastric cancer is to remove the cancer-containing portion of the stomach while preserving a remaining portion when that can be done safely and with adequate cancer margins.

The operation is therefore not defined simply by how much stomach is removed. It is defined by the relationship between the tumor, the remaining stomach, the required cancer margins, and the overall oncologic plan.

The American Cancer Society describes subtotal gastrectomy as removal of part of the stomach. It notes that the operation may be distal when the cancer is in the lower stomach and may be proximal in selected upper-stomach situations. The NCI PDQ similarly describes distal subtotal gastrectomy as an established surgical option for selected lesions that do not involve the fundus or cardioesophageal junction.

### 2. Why might only part of the stomach be removed?

The central reason is oncologic adequacy. If the cancer can be removed completely with an adequate microscopic margin while preserving part of the stomach, removing the entire stomach is not automatically necessary.

The NCI PDQ states that for selected stage I disease, subtotal gastrectomy has demonstrated equivalent survival compared with total gastrectomy and is associated with decreased morbidity. This statement applies to appropriately selected disease and should not be generalized to every gastric cancer.

The practical principle is therefore: remove enough stomach to treat the cancer adequately, but do not assume that removing more stomach is automatically better.

### 3. When is subtotal gastrectomy considered?

Selection depends on tumor location, tumor extent, resectability, and whether adequate microscopic margins can be achieved.

The NCI PDQ describes subtotal gastrectomy as the procedure of choice when the lesion is not in the cardioesophageal junction and does not diffusely involve the stomach in the clinical settings described. NCCN 2026 states that resectable T1b–T3 disease requires adequate gastric resection to achieve negative microscopic margins together with lymphadenectomy and lists distal, subtotal, and total gastrectomy among possible resection approaches.

This means that the same operation cannot be prescribed from the tumor stage alone. Location and the ability to obtain an adequate resection are central.

### 4. Distal subtotal gastrectomy

Distal subtotal gastrectomy removes the portion of the stomach containing a tumor in the lower or distal stomach while leaving a proximal portion of stomach.

The ACS describes this as the common form of subtotal gastrectomy when cancer is confined to the lower stomach. NCI describes distal subtotal gastrectomy for lesions that are not in the fundus or cardioesophageal junction.

At the patient-facing level, the important concept is that the stomach is divided into a portion that is removed and a portion that remains. The operation then requires reconstruction so that food can continue through the digestive tract.

### 5. Proximal subtotal gastrectomy

Proximal subtotal gastrectomy is a selected option when the tumor involves an appropriate proximal or cardia region and sufficient stomach can remain while preserving oncologic adequacy.

It is not the default interpretation of the term subtotal gastrectomy. The NCI PDQ identifies proximal subtotal gastrectomy or total gastrectomy as possible approaches for cardia lesions in selected circumstances, while the ACS notes that proximal gastrectomy is less commonly performed than distal gastrectomy.

Because proximal and gastroesophageal-junction tumors have additional anatomic considerations, this package keeps the topic conceptual and does not replace dedicated EGJ or total-gastrectomy decision packages.

### 6. The margin principle

A subtotal gastrectomy intended to treat cancer must leave an adequate microscopic margin around the cancer. NCCN 2026 explicitly frames resectable T1b–T3 gastric surgery around adequate gastric resection to achieve negative microscopic margins.

A negative microscopic margin means that the pathologist does not find cancer cells at the examined surgical edge. The broader goal is an R0 resection when cure is intended.

The exact margin distance can depend on tumor characteristics, location, histology, surgical approach, and guideline context. This package therefore teaches the principle of adequate margins rather than presenting one numerical distance as a universal rule.

### 7. Why location matters

The stomach is not a uniform surgical target. A tumor near the cardia or gastroesophageal junction creates different margin and reconstruction considerations from a tumor in the distal stomach.

The NCI PDQ uses location to distinguish distal subtotal gastrectomy, proximal subtotal or total gastrectomy, and total gastrectomy for diffuse disease. NCCN separately identifies gastroesophageal-junction cancers requiring Siewert classification and notes that some tumors may require additional esophageal resection to obtain adequate margins.

The educational message is simple: the operation follows the cancer's location and extent; the name of the operation does not determine the cancer's anatomy.

### 8. Subtotal versus total gastrectomy

Subtotal gastrectomy preserves part of the stomach. Total gastrectomy removes the entire stomach.

For selected disease, subtotal gastrectomy can provide comparable survival with lower morbidity than total gastrectomy according to the NCI PDQ. However, total gastrectomy may be required when the cancer is diffuse or when the tumor location and extent make preservation of an adequate stomach impossible.

Therefore, subtotal gastrectomy should not be described as universally preferable. The appropriate operation is the one that provides adequate cancer treatment while fitting the anatomy and overall clinical plan.

### 9. Lymph nodes are part of the cancer operation

Gastric cancer can spread to regional lymph nodes. NCCN states that gastric resection should include regional lymphatics, and the ACS explains that nearby lymph nodes are commonly removed during subtotal or total gastrectomy.

This package treats lymphadenectomy as an integrated part of the oncologic operation but does not own the detailed D1 or D2 procedures. Those topics are separate Population Packages.

For patients, the important message is that a cancer operation is more than simply removing the visible stomach tumor.

### 10. Omentum and nearby structures

The ACS notes that some omentum is typically removed with subtotal gastrectomy and that nearby lymph nodes are removed. If cancer has reached the spleen or other nearby organs, additional resection may sometimes be required.

NCCN also states that T4b tumors require en-bloc resection of involved structures. This package includes that concept only to explain that the extent of surgery may expand when the tumor directly involves adjacent structures.

Detailed multivisceral surgical technique remains outside this PP.

### 11. Spleen preservation

Routine spleen removal is not a defining part of subtotal gastrectomy. NCCN states that routine splenectomy is not indicated unless the spleen is involved or there is extensive hilar adenopathy.

This matters because patients may assume that a large abdominal cancer operation automatically requires removal of neighboring organs. It does not. Additional organ removal is generally driven by disease involvement and the need for adequate oncologic surgery.

### 12. Reconstruction after subtotal gastrectomy

After part of the stomach is removed, the remaining digestive tract must be reconnected so that food can continue to pass toward the small intestine.

The ACS explains that the remaining stomach is reattached to the small intestine and that this can be done in different ways. The exact reconstruction method is not owned by this PP.

For patient education, the key idea is that subtotal gastrectomy changes the route and capacity of the upper digestive tract but does not mean that digestion stops.

### 13. Open, laparoscopic, and robotic approaches

Subtotal gastrectomy may be performed through an open approach or, in selected settings, by minimally invasive techniques such as laparoscopy or robotic-assisted laparoscopy.

NCCN 2026 states that minimally invasive approaches may be considered in selected cases when the surgeon has appropriate laparoscopic or robotic foregut and lymphadenectomy experience. NCCN generally does not recommend minimally invasive approaches for T4b or bulky N2 gastric cancer.

The ACS notes that minimally invasive approaches can be associated with shorter hospital stay, less pain, and shorter recovery in some settings, while long-term outcomes can be similar. The most important patient-facing principle is that the approach must still permit safe and oncologically adequate surgery.

### 14. Why surgeon and center experience matter

Gastric cancer surgery is technically demanding. The ACS emphasizes the importance of a surgeon being skilled and experienced with the selected approach. NCCN similarly ties minimally invasive surgery to surgeon experience in foregut procedures and lymphadenectomy.

Experience is not a guarantee of a particular outcome. It is a reason to ask about the team's experience with the operation and the patient's disease context.

### 15. Curative-intent subtotal gastrectomy

When surgery is performed with curative intent, the goal is complete removal of the cancer with appropriate margins and regional nodal management, within an overall treatment plan.

For potentially resectable disease, ACS describes subtotal or total gastrectomy as possible surgical approaches, often combined with treatment before and/or after surgery. This PP does not prescribe the sequence of systemic therapy.

The patient-facing message is that surgery is one component of a broader gastric-cancer treatment plan.

### 16. Palliative subtotal gastrectomy

Subtotal gastric resection can sometimes be used for symptom relief when cancer cannot be cured by surgery. ACS describes selected palliative subtotal gastrectomy for problems such as bleeding, pain, or blockage of food passage.

NCCN states that in incurable disease, gastric resection should be reserved for palliation of symptoms such as obstruction or uncontrollable bleeding when less invasive options are not feasible. Palliative surgery has a different goal from curative surgery.

This distinction must remain explicit: symptom relief is not the same as removing all cancer.

### 17. Short-term risks and recovery

Major gastric surgery can cause complications such as bleeding, infection, injury to nearby structures, blood clots, and leakage from a surgical connection. Patients may also experience pain, nausea, reflux, abdominal discomfort, or diarrhea.

This package explains these risks at a high level so that patients understand why recovery requires monitoring. It does not provide diagnostic thresholds or treatment algorithms for complications.

### 18. Eating after subtotal gastrectomy

Removing part of the stomach changes its storage capacity and how food enters the small intestine. Many patients need to adapt by eating smaller amounts more frequently.

NCCN's survivorship guidance describes weight loss and other long-term issues after gastrectomy and recommends clinical monitoring and dietary adaptation. The ACS also describes the need for dietary adjustment after stomach surgery.

The degree of change varies. Subtotal gastrectomy preserves more stomach than total gastrectomy, but it does not guarantee completely normal eating afterward.

### 19. Nutritional consequences

Patients after gastrectomy can develop nutritional deficiencies. NCCN identifies long-term risks including deficiencies involving vitamin B12, vitamin D, calcium, iron, and zinc, along with weight loss and other gastrointestinal symptoms.

This package therefore teaches the need for long-term nutritional awareness and monitoring. It deliberately does not prescribe supplement doses or laboratory schedules because those decisions depend on the individual patient.

### 20. Dumping-type symptoms

Rapid movement of food into the small intestine can contribute to post-meal symptoms commonly described as dumping syndrome. NCCN identifies dumping syndrome among long-term issues after gastrectomy.

Symptoms can include gastrointestinal discomfort and altered bowel function after eating. The package should explain the concept without presenting every post-meal symptom as dumping syndrome and without giving individualized treatment instructions.

### 21. Feeding support

Some patients cannot eat or drink enough during recovery or because of advanced disease. ACS describes feeding tubes placed into the stomach or small intestine as a way to provide nutrition in selected circumstances. NCCN also notes feeding-tube consideration in selected surgical contexts.

A feeding tube is not a routine requirement for every subtotal gastrectomy. It is a supportive intervention considered according to clinical need.

### 22. Pathology after surgery

The removed stomach specimen and lymph nodes are examined by pathology. The results help establish whether the cancer was removed with negative margins, characterize the tumor, and determine the extent of nodal involvement.

This package explains the interface between surgery and pathology but does not own detailed pathology-report interpretation. That is a separate educational layer.

### 23. What happens after surgery?

The next steps depend on the final pathology and the overall treatment plan. Some patients receive additional systemic therapy or other treatment; others may proceed primarily to follow-up.

ACS describes treatment before and after surgery for potentially resectable disease, while NCCN provides disease-specific treatment pathways. This PP does not prescribe which postoperative regimen an individual should receive.

### 24. When subtotal gastrectomy is not the right operation

Subtotal gastrectomy is not appropriate when adequate cancer removal cannot be achieved while preserving part of the stomach.

Diffuse gastric involvement, certain proximal or gastroesophageal-junction tumors, and disease involving structures that require a different resection may lead to other operations. NCI and NCCN both emphasize that tumor location and extent determine the appropriate surgical approach.

The correct lesson is not that subtotal gastrectomy is 'better' than total gastrectomy. It is that subtotal gastrectomy is appropriate only when its oncologic and anatomic goals can be met.

### 25. Relationship to EMR and ESD

Endoscopic resection and surgical gastrectomy are different levels of intervention. Selected early gastric cancers may be treated endoscopically, but if an endoscopic procedure does not adequately remove the cancer or if the disease is outside endoscopic criteria, surgery may be required.

ACS and NCI describe surgery as a treatment option when endoscopic resection is not sufficient. PP-0193, PP-0194, and PP-0195 own the endoscopic-resection branch; this PP owns subtotal surgical resection.

### 26. Patient decision questions

Patients may reasonably ask: Why is a subtotal rather than total gastrectomy being recommended? Where is the tumor? Can adequate margins be achieved? Which part of the stomach will remain? Will lymph nodes be removed? What reconstruction is planned? Is the approach open or minimally invasive? How experienced is the team? What eating and nutritional changes should be expected? What will determine the next treatment after pathology?

These questions support shared understanding without converting the package into individualized medical advice.

## Patient Explanation

### What does subtotal gastrectomy mean?

It means removing the part of the stomach that contains the cancer while keeping a remaining part of the stomach when the cancer can still be removed adequately.

### Why not remove the whole stomach?

If the cancer can be removed with adequate margins while preserving part of the stomach, total removal may not be necessary. The NCI notes that selected patients can have comparable survival with less morbidity after subtotal gastrectomy.

### Will I still have a stomach?

Yes. A subtotal gastrectomy leaves part of the stomach. How much remains depends on where the cancer is and how much must be removed to treat it safely.

### Will I be able to eat normally?

Eating often changes after surgery because the stomach has less capacity and food may pass into the intestine differently. Many people need smaller, more frequent meals, and nutritional follow-up may be needed.

### Will lymph nodes be removed?

Usually, regional lymph-node removal is part of an oncologic gastrectomy. The exact extent is a separate surgical decision.

### Does subtotal gastrectomy mean my spleen will be removed?

No. Routine spleen removal is not indicated simply because gastrectomy is performed. It is considered when disease or specific nodal involvement makes it necessary.

### Can the operation be done laparoscopically or robotically?

In selected patients and experienced centers, minimally invasive approaches may be considered. Suitability depends on the cancer and the surgeon's expertise.

### Is subtotal gastrectomy always better than total gastrectomy?

No. It is appropriate only when adequate cancer treatment can be achieved while preserving part of the stomach.

### Can subtotal gastrectomy be used when cancer cannot be cured?

In selected patients it may be used to relieve symptoms such as bleeding, pain, or blockage. That is palliative surgery and has a different goal from curative surgery.

### What will determine what happens next?

The final pathology, the stage and features of the cancer, recovery, and the overall treatment plan determine whether additional treatment or follow-up is needed.

## Clinical Importance

- Subtotal gastrectomy is an oncologic operation, not merely a mechanical removal of part of the stomach.
- The operation is appropriate only when adequate cancer resection can be achieved.
- Preserving part of the stomach can reduce the functional burden compared with total gastrectomy in selected disease.
- Tumor location and extent are central to the surgical choice.
- Regional lymph-node management is part of the oncologic concept, while detailed lymphadenectomy is separately owned.
- Reconstruction restores gastrointestinal continuity and creates predictable functional consequences.
- Long-term nutritional monitoring is part of survivorship after gastrectomy.
- Curative and palliative operations must never be conflated.

## Key Concepts

**Subtotal gastrectomy:** Removal of part of the stomach rather than the entire stomach.

**Distal subtotal gastrectomy:** Partial gastric resection used for selected lower/distal gastric tumors.

**Proximal subtotal gastrectomy:** Partial gastric resection used in selected proximal/cardia situations.

**Negative microscopic margin:** No cancer cells identified at the examined surgical edge.

**R0 concept:** Curative-intent resection in which no residual microscopic tumor is identified.

**Lymphadenectomy:** Removal of regional lymph nodes as part of oncologic gastric surgery.

**Reconstruction:** Reconnection of the remaining digestive tract after gastric resection.

**Curative intent:** Treatment intended to remove all clinically detectable cancer and achieve long-term disease control.

**Palliative intent:** Treatment intended primarily to relieve symptoms or complications rather than cure the cancer.

**Dumping syndrome:** A group of post-meal symptoms related to rapid movement of stomach contents into the small intestine.

**Nutritional monitoring:** Follow-up for weight, dietary intake, and potential vitamin/mineral deficiencies after gastrectomy.

## Common Misconceptions

### “More stomach removed means better cancer treatment.”
False. The goal is adequate oncologic resection. Removing more stomach is not automatically better if an adequate cancer resection can be achieved with preservation.

### “Subtotal gastrectomy means the cancer is early.”
False. Subtotal gastrectomy can be used in selected resectable disease beyond the earliest stage when adequate resection is feasible.

### “Subtotal always means distal.”
False. Distal subtotal gastrectomy is common, but proximal subtotal gastrectomy is also described for selected proximal/cardia disease.

### “A subtotal gastrectomy is just removal of the visible tumor.”
False. Oncologic gastrectomy includes attention to margins and regional lymph nodes.

### “Laparoscopic means less cancer treatment.”
Not necessarily. In selected settings, minimally invasive surgery can provide comparable oncologic outcomes when performed by experienced teams.

### “A negative margin means the cancer can never come back.”
False. A negative margin is an important surgical endpoint but does not eliminate all risk of recurrence.

### “Nutritional problems happen only after total gastrectomy.”
False. Nutritional and digestive consequences can occur after partial gastrectomy as well.

### “Palliative subtotal gastrectomy cures the cancer.”
False. Palliative surgery is performed to relieve symptoms when cure is not the goal.

## Key Messages

- Subtotal gastrectomy removes part of the stomach while preserving a remaining portion when oncologically appropriate.
- The choice depends on tumor location, extent, resectability, and the ability to achieve adequate microscopic margins.
- Distal subtotal gastrectomy is a major form for selected lower-stomach cancers; proximal subtotal gastrectomy is used in selected proximal disease.
- Subtotal gastrectomy is not automatically preferable to total gastrectomy; it is appropriate only when cancer-control goals can be met.
- Regional lymph-node management is part of oncologic gastric surgery.
- The remaining stomach must be reconnected to the digestive tract.
- Open and minimally invasive approaches may be appropriate in different settings.
- Eating patterns and nutritional needs can change after surgery.
- Palliative subtotal gastrectomy has a symptom-relief goal and should not be confused with curative surgery.
- The final pathology and multidisciplinary treatment plan determine what happens after surgery.

# Extended Clinical Reasoning and Patient-Facing Detail
## Scenario A — Distal tumor with adequate remaining stomach

A tumor is located in the lower stomach and the surgical team believes the cancer can be removed with adequate microscopic margins while leaving a functioning proximal stomach. This is the clearest conceptual setting for distal subtotal gastrectomy. The package should explain that the reason for preserving the remaining stomach is not cosmetic; it is because oncologic adequacy can be achieved without removing the entire organ.

## Scenario B — Proximal/cardial tumor

A proximal or cardia tumor may require a different assessment of margins and reconstruction. NCI identifies proximal subtotal gastrectomy or total gastrectomy as possible options in selected cardia disease. The package should avoid telling a patient that one is universally preferred because the choice depends on the tumor and the ability to achieve adequate resection.

## Scenario C — Diffuse gastric involvement

If disease diffusely involves the stomach, preserving a meaningful cancer-free gastric remnant may not be feasible. NCI identifies total gastrectomy as potentially required in diffuse disease. This is an important boundary test because it prevents the educational message from becoming 'subtotal is better.'

## Scenario D — Tumor close to the gastroesophageal junction

A lesion near the gastroesophageal junction raises additional questions about the exact anatomic epicenter and required margins. NCCN requires Siewert classification for adenocarcinomas involving the EGJ and notes that some cases require additional esophageal resection. PP-0197 can flag this issue but should not own EGJ-specific treatment.

## Scenario E — Potentially resectable locally advanced disease

A patient may receive treatment before surgery and then undergo subtotal or total gastrectomy if the disease remains resectable. The operation is therefore embedded in a treatment sequence rather than functioning as an isolated intervention. PP-0197 explains this interface without prescribing the systemic regimen.

## Scenario F — Surgery with curative intent

Curative-intent surgery aims for complete oncologic removal. The educational hierarchy is margin adequacy first, lymphatic management second as part of the operation, and preservation of stomach only insofar as those cancer-control goals remain achievable.

## Scenario G — Selected palliative resection

A patient with incurable disease may undergo limited gastric resection for bleeding or obstruction when less invasive options are unsuitable. The educational goal is symptom relief. The package must never describe this operation as curative.

## Scenario H — Minimally invasive approach

A patient with a suitable disease extent is treated at a center with experienced minimally invasive gastric surgeons. NCCN allows minimally invasive approaches in selected cases. The package should explain the selection principle rather than promise faster recovery or better survival for every patient.

## Scenario I — T4b or bulky N2 disease

NCCN generally does not recommend minimally invasive approaches for T4b or bulky N2 disease. This provides an explicit example of why surgical approach cannot be chosen solely from a patient's preference for small incisions.

## Scenario J — Lymphadenectomy question

A patient asks whether lymph nodes will be removed. The answer should be that regional lymphadenectomy is generally integrated into oncologic gastric resection, while the exact extent is a separate surgical question covered by PP-0199–0201.

## Scenario K — Spleen question

A patient asks whether the spleen will be removed because it is near the stomach. NCCN states routine splenectomy is not indicated unless specific involvement or extensive hilar adenopathy exists. The patient-facing answer should therefore avoid implying routine organ removal.

## Scenario L — Eating after surgery

A patient may retain part of the stomach but still experience smaller meal capacity, early fullness, diarrhea, or other digestive changes. Preserving stomach function reduces but does not eliminate postoperative adaptation.

## Scenario M — Nutritional deficiency

A patient can develop nutritional deficiencies after partial gastrectomy. The package should identify this as a long-term monitoring issue, not assume that only total gastrectomy causes deficiencies.

## Scenario N — Feeding support

A feeding tube may be considered when adequate oral nutrition is not possible. It is a supportive measure for selected patients and not a defining component of subtotal gastrectomy.

## Scenario O — Inadequate endoscopic resection

After EMR or ESD, surgery may be recommended when the endoscopic procedure has not adequately removed the cancer. The endoscopic packages own the criteria for EMR/ESD; subtotal gastrectomy owns the surgical branch.

## Decision Logic — Patient-Facing Interpretation

### Question 1 — Can the cancer be removed adequately while keeping part of the stomach?
This is the central decision concept. If the answer is yes, subtotal gastrectomy may be appropriate. If the answer is no, another surgical approach may be needed.

### Question 2 — Where is the tumor?
Distal, proximal, cardia, gastroesophageal-junction, and diffuse locations have different implications. Location determines how much stomach can safely remain and whether additional structures may need assessment.

### Question 3 — Can negative microscopic margins be achieved?
NCCN makes adequate resection to achieve negative microscopic margins a core principle. This is more important than preserving a particular amount of stomach.

### Question 4 — What lymph nodes need to be addressed?
Regional lymphadenectomy is part of oncologic gastrectomy. The exact D1/D2 extent is not owned here.

### Question 5 — How will continuity be restored?
After partial stomach removal, the remaining stomach is reconnected to the small intestine. The exact reconstruction is technical and delegated.

### Question 6 — What approach will be used?
Open, laparoscopic, and robotic approaches may be considered depending on disease and expertise. Minimally invasive surgery is not automatically appropriate for every case.

### Question 7 — What happens after pathology?
The pathology findings and the overall cancer plan determine whether further treatment or follow-up is needed.

## Patient FAQ — Extended

### Will I have enough stomach left to eat?
Most patients with a subtotal gastrectomy retain part of the stomach, but the amount and function vary. Eating patterns often need to change, and smaller meals may be easier.

### Does keeping part of the stomach mean the cancer was less serious?
No. The operation is chosen according to where the cancer is and whether adequate cancer removal is possible. The amount of stomach removed does not by itself describe cancer severity.

### Does subtotal gastrectomy mean there will be no chemotherapy?
No. Surgery and systemic treatment are separate decisions within the overall treatment plan.

### Can the operation be done after chemotherapy?
Yes, surgery may be performed after preoperative/perioperative treatment when the disease remains appropriately resectable. The exact sequence belongs to the broader treatment plan.

### What does a negative margin mean?
It means cancer cells were not found at the examined surgical edge. It is an important goal of curative surgery but does not guarantee that recurrence can never occur.

### Will lymph nodes be removed even if scans do not show enlarged nodes?
Regional lymph-node management is generally part of oncologic gastric resection because microscopic disease may not be visible on imaging. The exact extent is a separate surgical decision.

### Will I lose my whole stomach?
Not with a subtotal gastrectomy. Part of the stomach remains. Total gastrectomy is a different operation.

### Why might the surgeon remove some omentum?
The omentum is closely associated with the stomach and lymphatic drainage. Some omentum is commonly removed as part of oncologic surgery.

### What if the cancer touches another organ?
If the tumor directly involves an adjacent structure, the surgical plan may need to include en-bloc removal of the involved structure. This is determined by the cancer anatomy and is not routine for every patient.

### Will I have a feeding tube?
Not necessarily. A feeding tube is considered when oral nutrition is inadequate or when the clinical situation makes temporary nutritional support useful.

### What is dumping syndrome?
It refers to symptoms that can occur when stomach contents move too quickly into the small intestine after stomach surgery. It is one possible long-term consequence of gastrectomy.

### Can subtotal gastrectomy cause anemia?
Nutritional deficiencies, including iron and vitamin B12 deficiency, can occur after gastrectomy. Monitoring is therefore part of long-term care.

### Will my bowel movements change?
Some patients experience diarrhea or other changes in bowel function after gastrectomy. The exact pattern varies.

### Is robotic surgery safer?
Not automatically. It is one surgical approach that may be appropriate in selected cases. The cancer extent and the experience of the surgical team remain important.

### Is open surgery outdated?
No. Open surgery remains an appropriate approach in selected situations. The best approach depends on the cancer and the team's expertise.

### Can subtotal gastrectomy cure gastric cancer?
It can be performed with curative intent in appropriately resectable disease, but no operation guarantees cure.

### Can subtotal gastrectomy be used for symptom relief?
Yes, selected palliative gastric resections may relieve bleeding, pain, or obstruction when cure is not achievable.

### What should I ask my surgeon?
Ask where the tumor is, why subtotal rather than total gastrectomy is recommended, whether adequate margins are expected, what lymph-node surgery is planned, how reconstruction will be performed, what approach will be used, and what nutritional changes to expect.

### Why is the exact margin distance not explained here?
Because a single number is not a universal rule for every gastric cancer. The surgical team applies the relevant disease and guideline context.

### Why is D2 not explained in detail here?

# Clinical Knowledge Block Expansion — Deep Patient Education

## A. Understanding the purpose of preserving part of the stomach

Preserving part of the stomach is meaningful only when it remains compatible with cancer control. The package therefore treats stomach preservation as a consequence of adequate oncologic resection, not as an independent treatment goal. The reader should understand that surgeons do not choose a smaller operation merely to minimize surgery. They choose an operation that can remove the cancer appropriately while fitting the tumor's location and extent.


## B. Understanding the word 'subtotal'

The word subtotal describes the amount of stomach removed, not the cancer stage, prognosis, or guarantee of cure. A subtotal gastrectomy can occur in different anatomic settings. The patient should therefore avoid interpreting 'subtotal' as a shorthand for early disease.


## C. Understanding why the operation is different from local tumor removal

A gastrectomy removes a portion of the stomach rather than simply shaving away the visible tumor. In cancer surgery, the resection is planned around the tumor and adequate margins, with regional lymph-node management as part of the oncologic operation. This distinguishes gastrectomy from endoscopic removal of selected early lesions.


## D. Understanding distal disease

When a cancer is located in the lower stomach, distal subtotal gastrectomy may permit removal of the diseased segment while preserving the proximal stomach. The exact boundary of resection is determined intraoperatively and pathologically in clinical practice; this package intentionally avoids teaching an operative cut line.


## E. Understanding proximal disease

Proximal subtotal gastrectomy is a selected option for appropriate proximal/cardia tumors. Because these tumors can approach the gastroesophageal junction, margin adequacy and the possible need for additional esophageal resection require specialist assessment. The package flags this complexity without becoming an EGJ surgery manual.


## F. Understanding diffuse disease

Diffuse involvement changes the feasibility of preserving a meaningful gastric remnant. The NCI PDQ identifies total gastrectomy as potentially required when disease diffusely involves the stomach. This is an important counterexample that protects against overgeneralization.


## G. Understanding microscopic margins

The surgeon cannot judge cancer clearance from the visible tumor alone. The microscopic margin is evaluated by pathology. A negative microscopic margin supports the goal of complete surgical removal, but it is only one part of the overall cancer prognosis.


## H. Understanding R0 without overpromising

R0 is a surgical/pathologic concept describing the absence of residual microscopic tumor at the resection margin in the intended curative setting. It should never be translated into 'the cancer can never return.' Recurrence can depend on disease biology, stage, lymph nodes, systemic spread, and other factors.


## I. Understanding lymph-node removal

Regional lymphadenectomy is part of the oncologic framework because gastric cancer may spread through lymphatic pathways. The patient does not need to memorize D1 or D2 station lists to understand the principle. Detailed nodal anatomy belongs to the dedicated lymphadenectomy packages.


## J. Understanding the omentum

The omentum is a fatty tissue structure associated with the abdominal organs. ACS notes that some omentum is typically removed with subtotal gastrectomy. The package treats this as an associated surgical component rather than an independent treatment decision.


## K. Understanding adjacent-organ resection

If a tumor directly involves an adjacent structure, an en-bloc resection may sometimes be necessary to achieve an oncologically appropriate operation. This does not mean nearby organs are routinely removed. The need is determined by disease involvement.


## L. Understanding spleen preservation

NCCN states that routine splenectomy is not indicated unless the spleen is involved or there is extensive hilar adenopathy. This is a useful patient-safety message because it prevents the assumption that gastrectomy automatically requires splenic removal.


## M. Understanding reconstruction

After part of the stomach is removed, continuity of the digestive tract must be restored. ACS describes reconnection of the remaining stomach to the small intestine. Different reconstructive methods exist, but this package intentionally stops at the conceptual level.


## N. Understanding the difference between anatomy and function

Having part of the stomach remain does not mean that its function will be identical to the original stomach. Storage capacity, meal size, gastric emptying, and nutritional physiology can change. Patient education should therefore address both anatomy and function.


## O. Understanding recovery

Recovery after subtotal gastrectomy involves healing from major abdominal surgery as well as adapting to a new digestive pattern. The package covers common risks and expected functional changes but does not provide a personalized recovery timetable.


## P. Understanding postoperative weight loss

Weight loss can occur after gastrectomy. NCCN identifies weight loss among common survivorship issues. The educational point is to recognize it early and maintain clinical follow-up, not to prescribe a universal diet.


## Q. Understanding nutritional deficiency

Gastrectomy can be associated with deficiencies involving nutrients such as iron, vitamin B12, vitamin D, calcium, and zinc. The exact risk and need for supplementation vary between patients, so the package emphasizes monitoring and individualized care.


## R. Understanding dumping syndrome

Dumping syndrome is associated with rapid movement of gastric contents into the small bowel. It is one possible long-term consequence after gastrectomy. Not every post-meal symptom is automatically dumping syndrome, so persistent symptoms require clinical assessment.


## S. Understanding diarrhea and altered bowel function

Diarrhea and other bowel changes can occur after gastric surgery. They can affect quality of life and nutrition. The package recognizes these symptoms as part of the postoperative functional landscape without assigning an individual diagnosis.


## T. Understanding palliative intent

Palliative subtotal resection can be considered when symptoms such as bleeding or obstruction need control and less invasive options are not feasible. The goal is symptom relief. The patient should understand that a palliative operation is fundamentally different from curative-intent resection.
