# Educational Objectives

After reading this package, the reader should be able to:

1. Explain what recurrence of gastric cancer means.
2. Distinguish recurrence from persistent disease and progression during active treatment.
3. Understand how a new symptom, examination finding or abnormal surveillance result can trigger recurrence evaluation.
4. Understand why a suspicious finding does not automatically prove recurrence.
5. Describe the broad clinical sequence used when recurrence is suspected.
6. Understand the complementary roles of clinical assessment, laboratory testing, imaging, endoscopy and pathology.
7. Understand why the location of a suspected recurrence influences the diagnostic work-up.
8. Distinguish local/locoregional recurrence from metastatic and peritoneal recurrence.
9. Understand the conceptual difference between suspected and established recurrence.
10. Understand why resectability and medical operability become important after recurrence is characterized.
11. Understand why recurrence detection is different from recurrence treatment.
12. Understand the limitations of recurrence detection and why no single test establishes recurrence in every situation.
13. Know what questions to ask the treating team when recurrence is suspected.

---

# Primary Educational Question

> **How is recurrent gastric cancer recognized, evaluated, and distinguished from other causes of a new abnormal finding after previous treatment?**

PP-0221 owns the clinical transition from **concern for recurrence** to **establishment and characterization of recurrent disease**.

It does not own treatment after recurrence has been established.

# Clinical Knowledge Blocks

## Block 1 — What Is Recurrence?

Recurrence means that gastric cancer returns after previous treatment.

It is a different clinical question from:

- whether the original cancer is still present immediately after treatment;
- whether a tumor is responding during active treatment;
- whether a new finding is caused by treatment rather than cancer.

The key transition is:

> **Previously treated disease + new clinical concern → determine whether recurrent cancer is present.**

---

## Block 2 — Recurrence Is Not the Same as Progression During Active Treatment

During active therapy, clinicians may ask:

> Is the cancer responding, stable, or progressing under the current treatment?

That belongs primarily to response assessment and standardized response/progression assessment.

Recurrence asks a different question:

> **Has previously treated gastric cancer returned?**

The temporal and clinical context therefore matters.

PP-0217 and PP-0218 own treatment-response/progression assessment.

PP-0221 owns recurrence after prior treatment.

---

## Block 3 — How Can Recurrence First Become Suspected?

Recurrence can enter the diagnostic pathway through:

- a new symptom;
- a persistent or worsening symptom;
- a new physical finding;
- an abnormal laboratory result;
- an abnormal surveillance study;
- a suspicious imaging finding;
- a suspicious endoscopic finding;
- another clinically concerning observation.

Routine surveillance belongs to PP-0220.

Once a finding changes the clinical question to:

> **Could this be recurrent gastric cancer?**

the recurrence-detection pathway begins.

---

## Block 4 — Symptoms Are Triggers, Not Diagnoses

Symptoms after gastric-cancer treatment can have many causes.

They may reflect:

- recurrence;
- postoperative changes;
- treatment effects;
- benign gastrointestinal conditions;
- infection/inflammation;
- another unrelated disease;
- a second primary cancer.

Therefore:

> **A symptom can trigger recurrence evaluation, but a symptom alone does not establish recurrence.**

The ACS material emphasizes that gastric-cancer symptoms can be nonspecific and can also be caused by conditions other than cancer. fileciteturn27file2

---

## Block 5 — What Happens When Recurrence Is Suspected?

A useful conceptual pathway is:

**New concern**

↓

**Clinical reassessment**

↓

**Appropriate diagnostic evaluation**

↓

**Determine location and extent**

↓

**Establish or exclude recurrence**

↓

**Characterize recurrence**

↓

**Handoff to management**

The exact tests depend on:

- where recurrence is suspected;
- prior treatment;
- remaining anatomy;
- symptoms;
- clinical findings;
- whether a test result will change management.

---

## Block 6 — What Tests Can Help Evaluate Recurrence?

Depending on context, evaluation may involve:

- history and physical examination;
- laboratory testing;
- CT;
- FDG-PET/CT when clinically indicated;
- MRI in selected contexts;
- EGD;
- biopsy/pathology;
- diagnostic laparoscopy;
- other targeted investigations.

These tests have complementary roles.

No single test should be presented as universally sufficient.

---

## Block 7 — What Is the Role of Imaging?

Imaging can identify:

- a new mass;
- enlarged or suspicious lymph nodes;
- new distant lesions;
- peritoneal disease;
- other changes that may raise concern for recurrence.

The NCCN recurrence-related architecture includes CT chest/abdomen/pelvis and FDG-PET/CT as clinically indicated in relevant recurrent/metastatic pathways. fileciteturn26file0

However:

> **Imaging suspicion is not automatically the same as confirmed recurrence.**

Detailed image acquisition and interpretation belong to imaging-specific packages.

---

## Block 8 — What Is the Role of Endoscopy?

Endoscopy can be useful when suspected disease is accessible through the gastrointestinal lumen.

NCCN describes diagnostic and surveillance endoscopy as being used to determine the presence and location of neoplastic disease and to biopsy suspicious lesions. fileciteturn26file0

Therefore PP-0221 owns the **diagnostic role of endoscopy in recurrence evaluation**, not the procedure itself.

---

## Block 9 — What Is the Role of Biopsy and Pathology?

Biopsy can provide tissue for pathologic evaluation when tissue confirmation is clinically appropriate.

This is important because:

**suspicious finding**

is not always equivalent to:

**confirmed recurrent cancer**.

The need for tissue confirmation is context-dependent.

PP-0221 should therefore teach:

> **Tissue confirmation may be important when it will clarify the diagnosis or affect management, but there is no universal rule that every suspected recurrence must be biopsied.**

Detailed biopsy technique and pathology methodology are delegated.

---

## Block 10 — Why Does the Location of Suspected Recurrence Matter?

Different disease locations can require different evaluation.

Broadly, recurrence may be characterized as:

1. local/locoregional;
2. peritoneal;
3. distant/metastatic.

The location affects:

- what tests are useful;
- whether tissue can be obtained;
- whether laparoscopy is helpful;
- whether disease may be potentially resectable;
- which downstream management pathway applies.

---

## Block 11 — What Is Locoregional Recurrence?

Locoregional recurrence refers to recurrence in the local/regional disease territory after prior treatment.

NCCN GAST-8 distinguishes locoregional recurrence from metastatic disease and asks whether surgery is appropriate for isolated local recurrence. fileciteturn26file12

PP-0221 should therefore explain:

> **A recurrence may be localized or locoregional rather than automatically metastatic.**

---

## Block 12 — What Is Metastatic Recurrence?

Metastatic recurrence means recurrent disease with distant spread.

NCCN separates metastatic disease from locoregional recurrence within GAST-8. fileciteturn26file12

PP-0221 owns the recognition and classification of this pattern at a conceptual level.

Detailed metastatic-disease management belongs to PP-0222/PP-0223 and related treatment packages.

---

## Block 13 — What Is Peritoneal Recurrence?

Peritoneal dissemination is an important recurrence/progression pattern in gastric cancer.

The NCCN material has a dedicated GAST-10 pathway for peritoneal carcinoma as only disease, including:

- CT;
- diagnostic laparoscopy with washings;
- documented PCI;
- possible biopsy;
- consideration of FDG-PET/CT;
- consideration of EGD. fileciteturn26file0

PP-0221 should explain why peritoneal disease may require a specific diagnostic approach.

It should not become the treatment package for peritoneal disease.

---

## Block 14 — What Does “Disease Extent” Mean?

Once recurrence is suspected, clinicians need to understand:

- where the disease is;
- how much disease is present;
- whether disease is localized;
- whether distant disease exists;
- whether peritoneal disease exists;
- whether the disease appears potentially resectable.

This is the bridge between:

**recurrence detection**

and

**recurrence management**.

---

## Block 15 — What Does “Resectable” Mean in This Context?

At a conceptual level, resectable recurrence means disease that may be amenable to surgical removal in an appropriate patient.

NCCN states that surgery should be considered for medically fit patients with isolated resectable locoregional recurrence. fileciteturn26file12

PP-0221 does not own the surgical algorithm.

It only establishes why **resectability and medical operability** become important after recurrence is characterized.

---

## Block 16 — What Happens When Recurrence Is Unresectable or Metastatic?

The clinical question changes.

It moves from:

> **Can we establish and characterize recurrence?**

to:

> **How should recurrent disease be managed?**

NCCN routes unresectable or medically inoperable locoregional recurrence and metastatic disease to palliative management. fileciteturn26file12

PP-0221 should stop at this transition.

---

## Block 17 — Can a Normal Test Rule Out Future Recurrence?

No.

A normal assessment can indicate that no recurrence is detectable at that time.

It cannot guarantee that recurrence will never occur later.

NCCN notes that most gastric-cancer relapses occur within two years and approximately 90% occur by five years, while potentially actionable relapse has occasionally been recognized beyond five years. fileciteturn26file8

---

## Block 18 — Can Recurrence Occur After Five Years?

Yes.

The probability of recurrence generally decreases with time, but five years is not a biological guarantee that recurrence cannot occur.

This distinction is important for patient-facing education.

It should not be converted into a recommendation for indefinite intensive diagnostic testing.

---

## Block 19 — Why Is Recurrence Detection Sometimes Difficult?

Because a new finding can have several explanations.

For example:

**New abdominal symptom**

could reflect:

- recurrence;
- postoperative anatomy;
- treatment effect;
- benign gastrointestinal disease;
- another condition.

Likewise:

**New imaging abnormality**

could represent:

- recurrence;
- inflammation;
- infection;
- postoperative change;
- benign disease;
- another malignancy.

Therefore recurrence detection is a process of **clinical correlation and appropriate diagnostic evaluation**, not a single-test decision.

---

## Block 20 — Is Every Suspected Recurrence Biopsied?

No universal rule should be applied.

The role of biopsy depends on:

- location;
- accessibility;
- diagnostic uncertainty;
- whether the result will change management;
- the clinical context.

The project sources support biopsy of suspicious lesions and selected metastatic sites, but do not justify a universal “biopsy every recurrence” rule. fileciteturn26file0turn26file7

---

## Block 21 — Are Tumor Markers Enough to Diagnose Recurrence?

No universal tumor-marker-only algorithm is supported by the project source set.

Tumor markers may be part of broader clinical assessment in some contexts, but recurrence should not be represented as:

> **marker abnormality = recurrence confirmed.**

This prevents overclaiming and preserves the evidence hierarchy.

---

## Block 22 — Is ctDNA a Standard Recurrence Test in This Package?

Not as a universal standard.

The project architecture contains molecular-testing and ctDNA concepts, but the supplied core materials do not justify turning PP-0221 into a universal ctDNA/MRD recurrence-detection algorithm.

Potential molecular recurrence applications may be acknowledged as an emerging/deferred area.

Detailed molecular recurrence detection is delegated.

---

## Block 23 — Recurrence After Different Initial Treatments

Recurrence can be evaluated after:

- endoscopic resection;
- subtotal/partial gastrectomy;
- total gastrectomy;
- perioperative therapy;
- neoadjuvant therapy;
- adjuvant therapy;
- chemoradiation;
- other treatment pathways.

Prior treatment matters because it changes:

- anatomy;
- expected recurrence patterns;
- available diagnostic approaches;
- interpretation of postoperative findings.

PP-0221 should explain this principle without creating a separate algorithm for every prior treatment.

---

## Block 24 — What Happens After Recurrence Is Confirmed?

The clinical question changes from:

> **Is this recurrence?**

to:

> **How should this recurrence be treated?**

That is the explicit handoff to:

# PP-0222 — Management of Recurrent Gastric Cancer

Specialized disease-state packages then address:

- metastatic gastric cancer;
- peritoneal carcinomatosis;
- peritoneal-only disease;
- HIPEC;
- palliative care;
- best supportive care.

# Clinical Importance

## 1. Recurrence detection is a transition point

PP-0221 is not merely a list of tests.

Its most important role is to define the transition:

**surveillance**

→ **clinical concern**

→ **diagnostic evaluation**

→ **recurrence established/characterized**

→ **management**.

---

## 2. Recurrence is not synonymous with metastatic disease

NCCN GAST-8 explicitly distinguishes locoregional recurrence from metastatic disease. fileciteturn26file12

This is essential because isolated locoregional recurrence can enter a different downstream pathway from metastatic recurrence.

---

## 3. Detection and treatment must remain separate

The package should not jump from:

> “CT suggests recurrence”

directly to:

> “start treatment X.”

There is an intermediate clinical process:

- confirm/characterize disease;
- define extent;
- assess resectability/medical fitness;
- determine appropriate management.

---

# Patient Explanation

## A simple way to understand recurrence detection

After treatment for gastric cancer, you may continue to have regular follow-up.

Sometimes a new problem appears:

- a new symptom;
- a change on a scan;
- an abnormal endoscopy;
- a physical finding;
- another test result that concerns the medical team.

That does **not automatically mean that the cancer has returned**.

The doctors may need to investigate further.

They may use:

- an examination;
- blood tests;
- CT or another scan;
- endoscopy;
- a biopsy;
- other tests depending on where the problem is located.

The purpose is to answer two questions:

> **Has the gastric cancer returned?**

and, if it has:

> **Where is it and how extensive is it?**

Only after those questions are sufficiently answered does the care team move to treatment planning.

# Evidence Maturity

## Established / Guideline-Supported

- Recurrence is a distinct clinical pathway in NCCN GAST-8.
- Locoregional recurrence and metastatic disease are distinguished.
- Isolated resectable locoregional recurrence may be considered for surgery in medically fit patients.
- Unresectable or metastatic recurrence enters a downstream palliative-management pathway.
- CT and clinically indicated FDG-PET/CT can contribute to evaluation.
- Endoscopy can identify and biopsy suspicious lesions.
- Peritoneal-only disease has a dedicated evaluation pathway.
- Recurrence timing is concentrated in the first two years, with most recurrence by five years, while late actionable relapse can occur. fileciteturn26file12turn26file0turn26file8

## Context-Dependent

- Need for biopsy.
- Choice of imaging.
- Role of endoscopy.
- Diagnostic laparoscopy.
- Laboratory testing.
- Determination of resectability.
- Multidisciplinary review.
- Additional follow-up after five years.

## Emerging / Uncertain

- Universal ctDNA-based recurrence detection.
- Universal MRD monitoring.
- Universal tumor-marker-only recurrence detection.
- A single optimal diagnostic algorithm for every recurrence pattern.
- Universal mandatory tissue confirmation.

## Not Established / Excluded

- One test that detects every recurrence.
- A universal biopsy rule.
- A universal tumor-marker threshold.
- A universal ctDNA recurrence threshold.
- Individual recurrence probability derived from population timing statistics.
- A claim that normal testing permanently rules out recurrence.

# Common Misconceptions

## Myth 1 — “Surveillance and recurrence detection are the same thing.”

**Fact:**  
Surveillance is planned follow-up. Recurrence detection begins when a clinical concern requires evaluation.

## Myth 2 — “An abnormal scan proves recurrence.”

**Fact:**  
An abnormal scan may raise suspicion but does not automatically establish recurrence.

## Myth 3 — “Symptoms diagnose recurrence.”

**Fact:**  
Symptoms can have many causes.

## Myth 4 — “No symptoms means no recurrence.”

**Fact:**  
Recurrence can sometimes be identified through surveillance or other testing before prominent symptoms appear.

## Myth 5 — “Every recurrence is metastatic.”

**Fact:**  
Locoregional recurrence and metastatic disease are distinct categories.

## Myth 6 — “Every recurrence is treated in the same way.”

**Fact:**  
Management depends on location, extent, resectability, medical fitness and other clinical factors.

## Myth 7 — “Five years without recurrence means recurrence is impossible.”

**Fact:**  
Most recurrence occurs earlier, but late actionable relapse can occur.

## Myth 8 — “Every suspected recurrence requires biopsy.”

**Fact:**  
The need for tissue confirmation depends on the clinical situation.

## Myth 9 — “Tumor markers alone diagnose recurrence.”

**Fact:**  
No universal tumor-marker-only diagnostic algorithm is established by the supplied source set.

## Myth 10 — “Recurrence detection automatically includes treatment.”

**Fact:**  
Detection and treatment are separate clinical stages.

# Clinical Safety Boundary

This CKO provides general, source-grounded education.

It does not:

- diagnose recurrence in an individual;
- interpret an individual CT, PET/CT, MRI or endoscopy;
- determine whether a biopsy is required for an individual;
- prescribe a diagnostic test;
- estimate an individual's recurrence probability;
- determine resectability for an individual;
- prescribe surgery;
- prescribe systemic therapy;
- prescribe immunotherapy or targeted therapy.

NCCN itself emphasizes that clinical application requires independent medical judgment in the context of individual circumstances. fileciteturn26file13
