# B. Educational Objectives

After reading this Population Package, the reader should be able to:

1. Explain what gastrectomy means in gastric-cancer care.
2. Understand why surgery is a major treatment option for localized/resectable gastric cancer.
3. Distinguish curative-intent gastrectomy from palliative gastric surgery.
4. Explain the central goal of complete cancer resection with negative microscopic margins.
5. Understand the meaning of R0, R1, and R2 resection.
6. Understand why tumor location and extent influence how much of the stomach is removed.
7. Distinguish subtotal/partial and total gastrectomy at a conceptual level.
8. Understand why more extensive surgery is not automatically better surgery.
9. Understand why regional lymph-node removal is part of oncologic gastrectomy.
10. Recognize D1 and D2 as different conceptual extents of lymph-node dissection.
11. Understand why D2 surgery requires appropriate training and center expertise.
12. Understand why routine spleen removal is not recommended.
13. Understand the concept of en-bloc resection for selected locally advanced tumors.
14. Understand why preoperative staging is needed before surgical planning.
15. Understand why occult peritoneal or distant disease can change the surgical plan.
16. Understand the conceptual difference between open and minimally invasive gastrectomy.
17. Understand why minimally invasive surgery is not appropriate for every patient.
18. Understand that gastrectomy may occur within a multimodality treatment pathway.
19. Understand that the final surgical specimen and lymph nodes provide important pathologic information.
20. Understand that the operation may be curative or palliative depending on disease status and treatment goals.
21. Recognize the main short-term and long-term consequences of gastrectomy.
22. Understand why eating patterns and nutritional needs can change after surgery.
23. Recognize dumping syndrome as a possible post-gastrectomy problem.
24. Understand why vitamin and mineral monitoring may be needed.
25. Understand why surgeon and center experience matter.
26. Identify practical questions to ask before gastrectomy.
27. Correct common misconceptions about gastrectomy.
28. Understand that the final operation is individualized and cannot be selected from a single tumor feature alone.

# D. Clinical Knowledge Blocks

## Knowledge Block 1 — What Is Gastrectomy?

### Definition

Gastrectomy is surgery that removes part or all of the stomach.

In gastric cancer, gastrectomy is not simply a procedure for physically removing a visible mass. It is an oncologic operation designed to remove the primary cancer with an adequate margin and, when indicated, the regional lymph nodes.

A partial or subtotal gastrectomy removes only part of the stomach.

A total gastrectomy removes the entire stomach.

The choice is determined by the location and extent of the cancer and by what is required to achieve an adequate cancer operation.

### Patient Explanation

If your doctor recommends a gastrectomy, the question is not simply:

> “How much stomach can be removed?”

The more important question is:

> “What is the smallest extent of surgery that can safely achieve an adequate cancer operation?”

That distinction is central to understanding gastric-cancer surgery.

---

## Knowledge Block 2 — Why Is Surgery Used?

Surgery is a primary treatment option for localized gastric cancer when the cancer can be adequately resected.

The purpose of surgery may be:

- curative, when complete cancer removal is the treatment goal; or
- palliative, when surgery is used mainly to relieve a cancer-related complication or symptom.

The surgical plan depends on staging, resectability, tumor location, tumor extent, the patient's fitness for surgery, and the overall treatment pathway.

### Key Message

**Gastrectomy is a cancer-treatment strategy, not simply removal of the stomach.**

---

## Knowledge Block 3 — What Does Curative-Intent Surgery Mean?

Curative-intent surgery aims to remove all visible and microscopic disease that can be addressed surgically.

The central surgical objective is an R0 resection:

> no residual microscopic disease at the completion of resection.

This requires more than removing the tumor itself.

The operation also needs appropriate margins and regional lymph-node treatment when indicated.

### Important Distinction

A surgeon may remove the visible tumor but still find that the operation did not achieve an R0 result.

Therefore:

**Tumor removed**

does not automatically mean:

**complete oncologic resection achieved.**

---

## Knowledge Block 4 — R0, R1, and R2

### R0

No residual microscopic disease.

### R1

Microscopic residual disease remains.

### R2

Macroscopic residual disease remains in the absence of distant metastasis.

These terms are important because they describe the completeness of cancer resection.

### Patient Explanation

A pathology report or surgical discussion may use these terms to describe how completely the cancer was removed.

They are not interchangeable with:

- tumor size;
- tumor stage;
- margin length.

They describe residual disease after surgery.

---

## Knowledge Block 5 — Why Do Surgical Margins Matter?

A surgical margin is the tissue boundary around the removed cancer.

For curative-intent gastric surgery, the goal is a negative microscopic margin.

This is why the surgeon needs to plan the operation based on:

- where the tumor is;
- how far it extends;
- whether adjacent structures are involved;
- how much stomach must be removed to obtain an adequate margin.

### Patient Message

The goal is not to remove “as much stomach as possible.”

The goal is to remove **enough tissue to achieve an oncologically adequate resection**.

---

## Knowledge Block 6 — How Does Tumor Location Affect Surgery?

Tumor location is one of the major determinants of the extent of gastrectomy.

A tumor in the distal stomach may be suitable for a distal or subtotal gastrectomy when an adequate margin can be achieved.

A tumor involving the proximal stomach or cardia may require a proximal gastrectomy or total gastrectomy depending on its exact location and extent.

Diffuse involvement of the stomach may require total gastrectomy.

Tumors involving the esophagogastric junction require additional classification and may follow different treatment pathways depending on their Siewert type.

### Key Message

**The location of the cancer helps determine how much stomach needs to be removed.**

---

## Knowledge Block 7 — Subtotal Versus Total Gastrectomy

### Subtotal Gastrectomy

Part of the stomach is removed while part of the stomach remains.

This can be appropriate for selected distal gastric cancers when an adequate oncologic resection is possible.

### Total Gastrectomy

The entire stomach is removed.

This may be required when disease is diffuse, when tumor location makes partial removal inadequate, or when the required cancer margins cannot otherwise be achieved.

### Important Principle

In appropriate distal gastric cancers, subtotal gastrectomy can provide similar cancer outcomes to total gastrectomy while causing fewer complications.

Therefore:

> **More extensive surgery is not automatically better surgery.**

Detailed subtotal gastrectomy belongs to PP-0197.

Detailed total gastrectomy belongs to PP-0198.

---

## Knowledge Block 8 — Why Preserve the Stomach When Possible?

Preserving part of the stomach can reduce the functional and nutritional burden of surgery when the cancer can still be removed adequately.

This does not mean:

> “Always preserve as much stomach as possible.”

The correct principle is:

> **Preserve the stomach when doing so remains oncologically safe.**

If preserving part of the stomach would compromise the cancer operation or margins, more extensive surgery may be required.

---

## Knowledge Block 9 — Why Are Lymph Nodes Removed?

Gastric cancer can spread through regional lymphatic pathways.

Therefore, curative gastric resection generally includes regional lymph-node removal.

Lymphadenectomy serves two important purposes:

1. it contributes to the completeness of regional cancer resection;
2. it provides information for pathologic staging and prognosis.

### Patient Explanation

The surgeon is not removing lymph nodes simply because they are “near the stomach.”

They are removed because gastric cancer can spread through regional lymphatic pathways and because examining the nodes helps determine how far the cancer has spread.

---

## Knowledge Block 10 — What Are D1 and D2?

D1 and D2 describe different extents of regional lymph-node dissection.

At a high level:

- D1 focuses on perigastric/regional lymph nodes.
- D2 includes D1 plus additional lymph nodes along named vessels of the celiac axis.

The exact nodal anatomy and operative technique are not part of this package.

### Boundary

PP-0196 explains **why lymphadenectomy is part of gastrectomy**.

PP-0199 explains **lymphadenectomy itself**.

PP-0200 explains **D1**.

PP-0201 explains **D2**.

---

## Knowledge Block 11 — Why Does Lymph-Node Assessment Matter?

The number and distribution of involved lymph nodes help determine pathologic stage.

NCCN describes a goal of examining at least 16 lymph nodes in the relevant surgical framework.

The purpose is not to treat “16” as a magic number.

Rather, adequate lymph-node evaluation improves staging and helps the clinical team understand the extent of disease.

### Important Message

A high-quality cancer operation is judged by more than the stomach specimen.

The lymph-node component matters too.

---

## Knowledge Block 12 — Why Is Routine Splenectomy Not Recommended?

Routine removal of the spleen is not indicated simply because a total or proximal gastrectomy is being performed.

NCCN states that splenectomy is generally reserved for situations such as:

- direct splenic involvement; or
- extensive hilar adenopathy.

Evidence cited by NCCN shows greater morbidity with prophylactic splenectomy without a survival advantage compared with spleen-preserving surgery.

### Patient Message

**Total gastrectomy does not automatically mean spleen removal.**

---

## Knowledge Block 13 — What Is En-Bloc Resection?

Some locally advanced gastric cancers directly involve nearby structures.

For selected T4b tumors, NCCN recommends en-bloc resection of involved structures.

This means that the involved structure may be removed together with the primary tumor when this is necessary to achieve an appropriate oncologic resection.

### Important Boundary

This package explains the principle.

It does not teach multivisceral surgical technique.

---

## Knowledge Block 14 — Why Is Staging Needed Before Gastrectomy?

Before surgery, clinicians need to determine:

- where the primary tumor is;
- how deeply it may extend;
- whether regional lymph nodes appear involved;
- whether there is distant or peritoneal disease;
- whether a curative operation is realistic.

CT of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis is part of the staging framework.

EUS may be used in selected patients when additional local staging information is needed and no metastatic disease is seen on CT.

### Patient Message

The operation is planned **after the team understands the disease as well as possible**.

---

## Knowledge Block 15 — What If Staging Finds Metastatic or Unresectable Disease?

A gastrectomy intended to cure cancer may not be appropriate if the disease cannot be completely resected.

NCCN describes unresectability for cure in settings including:

- peritoneal involvement;
- positive peritoneal cytology;
- distant metastases;
- selected locally advanced involvement such as major vascular encasement.

In these situations, the treatment goal may shift from curative surgery to systemic or palliative management.

### Important Message

**A patient can be medically fit for surgery but still not be a candidate for curative gastrectomy if the cancer cannot be adequately resected.**

---

## Knowledge Block 16 — What Is Staging Laparoscopy?

Some patients have a risk of metastatic disease that is not visible on routine imaging.

In selected patients, diagnostic laparoscopy with peritoneal washings can identify radiographically occult peritoneal disease.

A positive peritoneal cytology result without visible implants is classified as metastatic disease in the NCCN framework.

### Boundary

PP-0196 explains why this may change surgical planning.

Detailed staging-laparoscopy methodology belongs to a dedicated staging package.

---

## Knowledge Block 17 — Open, Laparoscopic, and Robotic Surgery

Gastrectomy can be performed through:

- open surgery;
- laparoscopic surgery;
- robotic-assisted minimally invasive surgery.

The choice depends on:

- tumor characteristics;
- disease extent;
- patient factors;
- surgeon expertise;
- center experience.

NCCN supports consideration of minimally invasive approaches in selected cases when the surgeon has appropriate experience with foregut procedures and lymphadenectomy.

### Important Message

Minimally invasive does not automatically mean appropriate.

---

## Knowledge Block 18 — When Is Minimally Invasive Surgery Less Appropriate?

NCCN states that minimally invasive approaches are generally not recommended for:

- T4b gastric cancer;
- bulky N2 disease.

The reason is not simply the size of the incision.

The operation must still accomplish the necessary oncologic resection safely.

### Principle

**The surgical approach should serve the cancer operation, not replace it.**

---

## Knowledge Block 19 — What Does Surgical Expertise Mean?

Gastrectomy is a complex cancer operation.

Expertise matters particularly for:

- lymphadenectomy;
- minimally invasive surgery;
- locally advanced tumors;
- complex reconstruction;
- management of complications.

NCCN specifically states that D2 lymphadenectomy requires significant training and expertise and should be performed in experienced centers.

ACS also emphasizes the importance of experienced surgeons and hospitals for stomach-cancer surgery.

### Patient Message

It is reasonable to ask:

> “How experienced is the surgical team with gastric-cancer surgery?”

---

## Knowledge Block 20 — Gastrectomy Within Multimodality Treatment

Gastrectomy may be only one component of treatment.

Depending on stage and disease characteristics, treatment may include:

- therapy before surgery;
- surgery;
- therapy after surgery.

The final treatment sequence is not owned by this package.

### Key Message

**Surgery does not necessarily mean the entire cancer treatment is finished.**

---

## Knowledge Block 21 — What Is Reconstruction?

When part or all of the stomach is removed, the digestive tract must be reconnected so that food can continue to pass from the esophagus toward the small intestine.

After subtotal gastrectomy, the remaining stomach is connected to the intestine.

After total gastrectomy, the esophagus is connected to the small intestine.

### Boundary

This package explains the purpose of reconstruction.

Detailed reconstruction options and operative techniques belong to downstream surgical packages.

---

## Knowledge Block 22 — What Is Palliative Gastrectomy?

Palliative surgery has a different goal from curative surgery.

In incurable disease, gastric resection may be considered when symptoms such as:

- obstruction;
- uncontrollable bleeding

cannot be adequately managed with less invasive approaches.

The goal is symptom relief rather than complete cancer eradication.

### Important Distinction

**Curative gastrectomy**

→ aims to remove cancer completely.

**Palliative gastric surgery**

→ aims to improve symptoms or manage complications.

---

## Knowledge Block 23 — Palliative Bypass Is Not the Same as Gastrectomy

A gastrojejunostomy can bypass an obstructed part of the stomach without necessarily removing the stomach.

Other options, including endoscopic stenting, may be considered in selected patients.

Therefore:

> **Bypass is a palliative strategy, not simply another name for gastrectomy.**

Detailed palliative procedures belong to dedicated palliative-care packages.

---

## Knowledge Block 24 — What Are the Main Surgical Risks?

Gastrectomy is major surgery.

Potential complications include:

- bleeding;
- blood clots;
- injury to nearby organs;
- leakage from a new gastrointestinal connection;
- infection and other postoperative complications.

The exact risk depends on:

- the extent of surgery;
- the surgical approach;
- disease complexity;
- patient health;
- center expertise.

### Safety Message

No patient can be assigned a meaningful individualized risk percentage from this package alone.

---

## Knowledge Block 25 — What Changes After Gastrectomy?

The digestive system changes after removal of part or all of the stomach.

Patients may experience changes in:

- meal size;
- meal frequency;
- tolerance of certain foods;
- bowel symptoms;
- appetite;
- weight;
- nutritional status.

The changes are generally greater after total gastrectomy.

### Patient Message

Recovery is not only about the incision healing.

The body also has to adapt to a new way of handling food.

---

## Knowledge Block 26 — Why Are Smaller, More Frequent Meals Often Needed?

After gastrectomy, the stomach's ability to store food may be reduced or absent.

As a result, many patients need to adapt by:

- eating smaller meals;
- eating more frequently;
- monitoring which foods cause symptoms;
- working with a dietitian when needed.

The exact dietary plan is individualized.

---

## Knowledge Block 27 — What Is Dumping Syndrome?

Dumping syndrome is a group of symptoms that can occur after gastric surgery.

NCCN describes:

### Early dumping

Symptoms can occur soon after eating and may include:

- palpitations;
- diarrhea;
- nausea;
- abdominal cramps.

### Late dumping

Symptoms can occur later after eating and may include:

- dizziness;
- hunger;
- sweating;
- faintness.

### Important Message

Dumping syndrome is a recognized post-gastrectomy problem and does not mean that the cancer has returned.

---

## Knowledge Block 28 — Why Can Vitamin Deficiencies Occur?

Gastrectomy can alter:

- food intake;
- digestion;
- nutrient handling;
- absorption.

Potential deficiencies include:

- vitamin B12;
- iron;
- vitamin D;
- calcium;
- zinc;
- other micronutrients.

The risk is particularly important after total gastrectomy.

### Key Message

Nutritional follow-up is part of cancer survivorship after gastrectomy.

---

## Knowledge Block 29 — Why Might a Feeding Tube Be Considered?

Some patients, especially those undergoing total gastrectomy, may have difficulty meeting nutritional needs after surgery.

NCCN notes that a feeding tube may be considered selectively, particularly when postoperative chemoradiation is likely.

This is not required for every patient.

The decision depends on:

- nutritional status;
- expected recovery;
- treatment plan;
- surgical context.

---

## Knowledge Block 30 — What Does the Final Pathology Tell Us?

The surgical specimen allows the team to assess:

- the primary tumor;
- depth and extent of invasion;
- surgical margins;
- lymph-node involvement;
- pathologic stage.

This information may influence:

- postoperative treatment;
- prognosis;
- follow-up planning.

### Boundary

PP-0196 explains the role of postoperative pathology.

Detailed pathology methodology belongs to the pathology package.

---

## Knowledge Block 31 — Why Does the Final Pathology Matter After Surgery?

The preoperative plan is based on the best available staging information.

The final specimen provides direct information about the disease that was actually removed.

Therefore:

> **The operation is not the end of the information pathway.**

Surgery produces a specimen.

Pathology analyzes that specimen.

The final findings then inform the next clinical decisions.

---

## Knowledge Block 32 — Why Is “More Surgery” Not Always Better?

A larger operation may create:

- greater nutritional burden;
- more postoperative complications;
- greater functional consequences.

NCCN and NCI support subtotal gastrectomy as an appropriate option for selected distal cancers, with comparable outcomes and fewer complications than total gastrectomy in appropriate settings.

The correct principle is:

> **Do enough surgery to achieve oncologic adequacy, but do not assume that unnecessary additional resection improves cancer control.**

---

## Knowledge Block 33 — Patient Questions Before Surgery

Useful questions include:

1. Why do I need gastrectomy?
2. Is the goal curative or palliative?
3. Where exactly is my tumor?
4. Why is subtotal or total gastrectomy recommended?
5. Can the surgeon achieve negative margins?
6. Will lymph nodes be removed?
7. What extent of lymphadenectomy is planned?
8. Is minimally invasive surgery appropriate for me?
9. How experienced is the surgical team with gastric cancer?
10. Could nearby organs need to be removed?
11. How will my digestive tract be reconstructed?
12. What are the main short-term complications?
13. How might my eating change?
14. Will I need nutritional monitoring?
15. Could I need vitamin or mineral supplements?
16. Could I need a feeding tube?
17. What will the final pathology tell us?
18. Will I need treatment before or after surgery?
19. What symptoms after surgery should prompt urgent contact?
20. What follow-up will I need?

# E. Common Misconceptions

## Myth 1

**“Gastrectomy always means removing the whole stomach.”**

### Fact

Gastrectomy can be partial/subtotal or total.

---

## Myth 2

**“Total gastrectomy is always more effective because more tissue is removed.”**

### Fact

The goal is an adequate oncologic resection, not maximum tissue removal.

---

## Myth 3

**“If the tumor is removed, the operation is automatically curative.”**

### Fact

Curative assessment depends on margins, tumor extent, lymph nodes, and other pathologic findings.

---

## Myth 4

**“Every gastrectomy includes spleen removal.”**

### Fact

Routine splenectomy is not recommended unless there is a specific oncologic reason.

---

## Myth 5

**“Lymph nodes are removed only if they look enlarged.”**

### Fact

Regional lymphadenectomy is part of the oncologic surgical framework because gastric cancer can spread through regional lymphatics.

---

## Myth 6

**“D2 is simply a bigger version of D1 with no additional expertise required.”**

### Fact

D2 is more extensive and requires appropriate training and center expertise.

---

## Myth 7

**“Laparoscopic or robotic surgery is always better than open surgery.”**

### Fact

Minimally invasive approaches are appropriate only in selected settings and when oncologic surgery can be performed safely by an experienced team.

---

## Myth 8

**“If imaging shows no spread, surgery is always curative.”**

### Fact

Occult peritoneal or metastatic disease may be found during staging, and final pathology may reveal more extensive disease than expected.

---

## Myth 9

**“Palliative gastrectomy is intended to cure metastatic cancer.”**

### Fact

Palliative surgery is primarily intended to relieve symptoms or complications.

---

## Myth 10

**“Gastrectomy only affects the stomach.”**

### Fact

It can affect eating, digestion, weight, vitamin status, and long-term nutrition.

---

## Myth 11

**“Nutritional problems only happen after total gastrectomy.”**

### Fact

They can occur after both subtotal and total gastrectomy, although the burden is generally greater after total removal.

---

## Myth 12

**“Once surgery is completed, no more cancer treatment is needed.”**

### Fact

Some patients need systemic therapy or other treatment before or after surgery depending on stage and treatment plan.

---
