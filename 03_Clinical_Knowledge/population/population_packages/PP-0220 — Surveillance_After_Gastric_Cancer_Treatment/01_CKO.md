# Educational Objectives

After reading this Population Package, the reader should be able to:

1. Explain what cancer-specific surveillance means after treatment for gastric cancer.
2. Understand why surveillance is different from post-treatment imaging, recurrence detection and survivorship.
3. Understand why surveillance depends on stage, treatment modality and surgical anatomy rather than following one universal schedule.
4. Describe the major components of guideline-based surveillance: clinical assessment, selected laboratory testing, selected endoscopy, selected imaging and nutritional monitoring.
5. Understand the important differences between surveillance after endoscopic resection, surgical resection, partial/subtotal gastrectomy and total gastrectomy.
6. Understand the surveillance time frame and why the first several years receive greater attention.
7. Understand why the end of routine gastric-cancer-specific surveillance does not mean the end of healthcare.
8. Recognize that a concerning surveillance finding does not automatically establish recurrence and may lead to a separate diagnostic evaluation.
9. Understand the limitations and uncertainty of the evidence supporting surveillance strategies.
10. Use patient-facing questions to clarify an individualized surveillance plan with the treating team.

# Primary Educational Question

> **How is a patient followed after treatment for gastric cancer?**

PP-0220 is intentionally framed around **longitudinal surveillance after treatment**, rather than around one imaging modality, one test, or recurrence diagnosis.

# Clinical Knowledge Blocks

## Block 1 — What Is Surveillance After Gastric Cancer Treatment?

Surveillance is the planned follow-up of a person after treatment for gastric cancer.

It is broader than simply looking for recurrence.

The surveillance process can include clinical visits, symptom review, physical examination, selected laboratory testing, selected endoscopy, selected imaging, and monitoring of treatment consequences.

The exact combination depends on the patient's disease stage, treatment, surgical anatomy and clinical context.

The central idea is:

> **Treatment completion begins a new phase of care; it does not necessarily mean that follow-up stops.**

---

## Block 2 — Why Is Surveillance Needed?

Surveillance has several linked purposes.

### Purpose 1 — Monitor for clinically important recurrence

Gastric cancer can recur after apparently successful treatment. Surveillance provides a structured opportunity to identify symptoms, examination findings or test abnormalities that may require further evaluation.

### Purpose 2 — Monitor the consequences of treatment

Gastrectomy and other cancer treatments can have lasting effects.

Nutritional deficiencies are particularly important after gastric resection, especially total gastrectomy.

### Purpose 3 — Maintain continuity of care

Follow-up creates a framework for assessing the patient's general condition, addressing treatment-related problems and connecting cancer-specific care with broader health care.

Therefore:

> **Surveillance is not synonymous with recurrence screening.**

---

## Block 3 — Surveillance Is Not the Same as Post-treatment Imaging

PP-0219 owns the clinical concept of post-treatment imaging.

PP-0220 owns the longitudinal surveillance framework.

The distinction is:

**PP-0219**
→ imaging after a treatment milestone  
→ reassessment of the current disease state.

**PP-0220**
→ follow-up over time  
→ clinical surveillance  
→ selected imaging when appropriate  
→ identification of findings that may require further evaluation.

Imaging can therefore appear in both packages without creating duplicate ownership.

The difference is the **clinical purpose and time structure**.

---

## Block 4 — Surveillance Is Not the Same as Recurrence Detection

A surveillance visit may identify:

- a new symptom;
- an abnormal examination;
- an abnormal laboratory result;
- an endoscopic abnormality;
- an imaging finding.

Such a finding may trigger further evaluation.

That does not mean the surveillance package should perform the recurrence diagnosis itself.

The conceptual transition is:

**Routine surveillance**

↓

**Concerning finding**

↓

**Further evaluation**

↓

**Possible recurrence**

↓

**PP-0221 Recurrence Detection**

This preserves the boundary between monitoring and diagnosis.

---

## Block 5 — Surveillance Is Not the Same as Survivorship

Cancer-specific surveillance is one component of life after cancer treatment.

Survivorship is broader.

Survivorship may address:

- long-term health;
- quality of life;
- nutritional consequences;
- physical functioning;
- psychosocial issues;
- preventive health;
- chronic treatment sequelae.

PP-0220 can introduce the transition toward survivorship but does not own the full survivorship domain.

The downstream relationship is:

**PP-0220 Surveillance**

→ **PP-0229 Gastric Cancer Survivorship**

→ **PP-0230 Long-term Follow-up**

---

## Block 6 — Why Is There No Single Surveillance Schedule for Everyone?

NCCN GAST-7 separates surveillance pathways according to treatment and stage.

The supplied NCCN v2.2026 material identifies three broad pathways:

1. Tis successfully treated by endoscopic resection.
2. Pathologic stage I disease treated by surgical resection or appropriate endoscopic resection.
3. Pathologic stage II/III or ypStage I–III disease treated with neoadjuvant ± adjuvant therapy.

The guideline also specifies a special consideration for patients who underwent total gastrectomy.

This means:

> **A surveillance schedule must be interpreted in the context of how the cancer was treated and what anatomy remains.**

---

## Block 7 — What Happens at a Surveillance Visit?

The core clinical follow-up component is history and physical examination.

NCCN GAST-7 specifies:

> H&P every 3–6 months for 1–2 years, then every 6–12 months for 3–5 years.

The purpose of a follow-up visit is not simply to “check a scan.”

The clinical team may consider:

- new or changing symptoms;
- weight and nutritional status;
- physical findings;
- treatment consequences;
- laboratory abnormalities;
- whether additional testing is clinically indicated.

The schedule is a guideline framework, not an individualized prescription.

---

## Block 8 — What Blood Tests May Be Used?

NCCN specifies:

> CBC and chemistry profile as clinically indicated.

This wording is important.

It does **not** mean that every patient necessarily needs the same blood tests at every visit.

Laboratory testing can be selected according to the patient's treatment history, symptoms, nutritional status and other clinical considerations.

Laboratory testing should not be represented as a stand-alone method that can exclude recurrence.

---

## Block 9 — What Is the Role of Endoscopy?

Endoscopic surveillance depends strongly on the treatment pathway.

For patients treated by endoscopic resection, NCCN specifies:

> EGD every 6 months for 1 year, then annually for up to 5 years; thereafter, as needed based on symptoms and/or radiographic findings.

For patients treated by surgical resection, EGD may be performed as clinically indicated.

This distinction reflects the different anatomy and residual gastric tissue after endoscopic treatment versus surgery.

PP-0220 owns the **surveillance role of EGD**.

Detailed EGD technique and biopsy methodology are delegated to the relevant endoscopy and biopsy packages.

---

## Block 10 — What Changes After Total Gastrectomy?

Total gastrectomy creates a specific surveillance boundary.

NCCN states that patients undergoing total gastrectomy for curative intent should follow the surveillance recommendations except for routine endoscopy; endoscopy is performed as clinically indicated unless the patient is symptomatic.

The other major issue is nutritional sequelae.

Lifelong monitoring and management may be required for nutritional consequences of gastrectomy, including deficiencies involving:

- vitamin B12;
- iron;
- zinc;
- calcium;
- vitamin D.

The surveillance package should therefore teach:

> **The anatomy created by treatment changes what can and should be monitored.**

---

## Block 11 — What Changes After Partial or Subtotal Gastrectomy?

For patients who have undergone partial or subtotal gastrectomy, NCCN identifies EGD as clinically indicated in the relevant surveillance pathway.

The remaining stomach means that endoscopic assessment can remain clinically relevant.

Nutritional monitoring is also important after surgical resection.

However, detailed nutritional management belongs to dedicated nutrition/supportive-care content.

---

## Block 12 — What Is the Role of Imaging During Surveillance?

NCCN includes CT surveillance in selected stage/treatment pathways.

For patients with pStage II/III or ypStage I–III disease after neoadjuvant ± adjuvant therapy, NCCN specifies:

> CT chest/abdomen/pelvis with oral and IV contrast every 6 months for the first 2 years, then annually for up to 5 years.

CT is preferred; for patients who cannot undergo CT, alternatives such as PET/CT or MRI may be used as clinically indicated.

For other pathways, imaging is described as clinically indicated based on symptoms and concern for recurrence.

Therefore:

> **Routine imaging is not identical across all gastric-cancer survivors.**

The final clinical plan depends on the relevant pathway.

---

## Block 13 — Why Does Surveillance Become More Structured Early After Treatment?

NCCN GAST-H states that most gastric-cancer relapses occur within:

- the first 2 years: approximately 70–80%;
- by 5 years: approximately 90%.

This helps explain why surveillance intensity is concentrated during the earlier post-treatment years.

However, this does not mean that recurrence becomes impossible after five years.

NCCN specifically notes that potentially actionable relapses have occasionally been recognized more than five years after curative-intent therapy.

---

## Block 14 — What Happens After Five Years?

Routine gastric-cancer-specific surveillance does not automatically continue indefinitely.

NCCN indicates that after five years, additional follow-up may be considered according to:

- risk factors;
- comorbidities;
- clinical circumstances.

This creates an important distinction:

**End of routine cancer-specific surveillance**

does not mean:

**end of medical care.**

Patients may continue to need:

- survivorship care;
- nutritional monitoring;
- management of treatment sequelae;
- preventive health care;
- routine age-appropriate care.

---

## Block 15 — Why Are Surveillance Recommendations Not Perfectly Certain?

NCCN GAST-H explicitly states that surveillance strategies after curative-intent R0 resection remain controversial.

The guideline notes:

- sparse prospective data;
- the need to balance benefits and risks;
- cost considerations;
- reliance on retrospectively analyzed literature;
- expert consensus.

Therefore the surveillance schedule should not be presented as a universally proven algorithm that maximizes survival for every patient.

A more accurate explanation is:

> **Guidelines provide a structured framework based on available evidence and expert consensus, but the optimal intensity of surveillance remains an area where evidence is not complete.**

---

## Block 16 — Why Does Treatment Type Matter?

Treatment can alter both recurrence risk and what can be examined.

Examples include:

### Endoscopic resection

Residual stomach remains available for endoscopic surveillance.

### Partial/subtotal gastrectomy

Some stomach remains, and EGD may be clinically indicated.

### Total gastrectomy

Routine surveillance endoscopy is not part of the standard pathway unless clinically indicated or symptoms occur.

### More advanced stage treated with perioperative therapy

NCCN provides a more structured CT surveillance pathway.

This is why copying another patient's surveillance calendar may be inappropriate.

---

## Block 17 — What Is the Role of Nutritional Monitoring?

After gastrectomy, surveillance includes more than cancer recurrence.

NCCN emphasizes lifelong monitoring and management of nutritional sequelae.

Potential deficiencies include:

- vitamin B12;
- iron;
- zinc;
- calcium;
- vitamin D.

The need for monitoring is especially relevant after total gastrectomy.

This is a key reason PP-0220 should not define surveillance only as “tests for recurrence.”

---

## Block 18 — Can Surveillance Identify Problems Before Symptoms Appear?

Surveillance can identify findings that warrant further evaluation, but the package must avoid implying that every abnormality is clinically important or that surveillance guarantees early detection of every recurrence.

A patient may have:

- no symptoms and no evidence of disease;
- a new symptom requiring assessment;
- an incidental finding;
- an abnormal test that is not recurrence.

The next step depends on clinical context.

---

## Block 19 — What If a Surveillance Test Is Abnormal?

An abnormal surveillance finding is a **signal for evaluation**, not an automatic diagnosis.

The conceptual pathway is:

**Abnormal finding**

→ assess the finding in clinical context

→ determine whether additional diagnostic evaluation is needed

→ if recurrence is suspected, enter the recurrence-detection pathway

→ if recurrence is confirmed, management follows the appropriate recurrent-disease pathway.

PP-0220 should therefore teach patients what the process means without teaching a recurrence-treatment algorithm.

---

## Block 20 — What Symptoms Should Prompt Earlier Contact?

Patients should understand that a surveillance plan does not mean they must wait until the next scheduled appointment if a new concerning problem develops.

Examples of clinically relevant changes can include:

- new or persistent symptoms;
- unexplained weight change;
- worsening eating difficulties;
- new gastrointestinal symptoms;
- symptoms that are unusual for the individual;
- other changes that concern the patient or care team.

This is a general communication principle, not a recurrence diagnosis.

Detailed warning-symptom and emergency-care packages remain delegated.

---

## Block 21 — Does Feeling Well Mean Surveillance Is Unnecessary?

No.

A patient can feel well and still be within a period where structured follow-up is recommended.

Conversely, being on surveillance does not mean that cancer is known to be present.

The correct mental model is:

> **Surveillance is a planned follow-up process for someone who has completed treatment, not a statement that cancer is currently detectable.**

---

## Block 22 — Does a Surveillance Scan Prove That Cancer Will Never Return?

No.

A normal test can be reassuring and may support the absence of detectable disease at that time, but it cannot guarantee that recurrence will never occur.

This is particularly important because recurrence can occasionally occur after apparently successful treatment and because surveillance has limitations.

---

## Block 23 — Is More Testing Always Better?

No.

More testing can introduce:

- false-positive findings;
- incidental findings;
- additional procedures;
- costs;
- anxiety;
- exposure to risks associated with particular tests.

The NCCN evidence discussion acknowledges uncertainty about the optimal surveillance strategy and the need to balance benefits and risks.

The correct principle is:

> **Appropriate surveillance is not the same as maximum surveillance.**

---

## Block 24 — Does Surveillance Replace Ordinary Health Care?

No.

Cancer-specific surveillance should coexist with broader health care.

NCCN survivorship material emphasizes preventive health measures and coordination with primary care.

After the routine gastric-cancer-specific surveillance window, broader health maintenance and survivorship become increasingly important.

# Patient Explanation

## A simple way to understand the whole process

After treatment for gastric cancer, your medical care usually enters a follow-up phase.

That follow-up is called **surveillance**.

Surveillance does not necessarily mean that doctors think the cancer is still present.

Instead, it means that the care team continues to monitor you because:

1. gastric cancer can sometimes return after treatment;
2. treatment itself can cause problems that need monitoring;
3. surgery can change nutrition and digestion;
4. different patients need different follow-up plans.

Your surveillance may involve:

- regular clinical visits;
- questions about symptoms;
- physical examination;
- selected blood tests;
- endoscopy in some treatment pathways;
- CT or other imaging in selected pathways;
- nutritional monitoring after gastrectomy.

The schedule is not the same for everyone.

It depends on factors such as:

- the stage of the cancer;
- whether treatment was endoscopic or surgical;
- whether part or all of the stomach was removed;
- whether systemic treatment was given before or after surgery;
- symptoms and other health conditions.

# Clinical Importance

## 1. Surveillance is a longitudinal process

The most important distinction is temporal.

Post-treatment assessment asks:

> **What is the disease status now after treatment?**

Surveillance asks:

> **How should the patient be followed over time after treatment?**

Recurrence detection asks:

> **Does this new finding represent recurrence?**

Survivorship asks:

> **How can long-term health and quality of life be supported after cancer treatment?**

These are connected but different clinical questions.

---

## 2. Surveillance is treatment-context dependent

The supplied NCCN guideline does not use one surveillance schedule for every patient.

This is clinically meaningful because treatment changes:

- recurrence context;
- anatomy;
- the ability to perform endoscopy;
- the need for nutritional monitoring;
- the role and frequency of imaging.

---

## 3. Surveillance includes more than recurrence monitoring

Nutritional consequences after gastrectomy can persist for years or throughout life.

Therefore a patient may need ongoing nutritional follow-up even after the principal period of cancer-specific surveillance has ended.

---

## 4. Surveillance has limitations

Surveillance cannot guarantee:

- that every recurrence will be found early;
- that every abnormality will be meaningful;
- that recurrence will never occur;
- that more intensive testing will necessarily produce better outcomes.

The evidence base is not complete, and NCCN explicitly characterizes surveillance strategies after curative-intent R0 resection as controversial.

# Key Concepts

## Concept 1 — Surveillance

Planned follow-up after treatment.

## Concept 2 — Recurrence

Cancer returning after a period when it was not detectable.

Recurrence is not automatically established by a surveillance finding.

## Concept 3 — Clinically indicated

A test is performed because the patient's symptoms, examination, treatment history or other clinical circumstances make it appropriate.

## Concept 4 — Curative-intent treatment

Treatment given with the goal of eliminating the known cancer rather than treating established metastatic disease as a chronic condition.

## Concept 5 — R0 resection

A resection in which no cancer is identified at the resection margin.

This terminology appears in NCCN's discussion of post-treatment surveillance.

## Concept 6 — EGD

Esophagogastroduodenoscopy, an endoscopic examination of the upper gastrointestinal tract.

Its surveillance role depends on the treatment pathway and remaining anatomy.

## Concept 7 — Nutritional sequelae

Longer-term nutritional consequences of treatment, particularly gastrectomy.

## Concept 8 — Survivorship

The broader phase of care addressing long-term health and quality of life after cancer treatment.

# Evidence Maturity

## Established / Guideline-Supported

- Systematic follow-up after treatment is recommended in the NCCN framework.
- Surveillance differs by stage and treatment pathway.
- H&P is scheduled at defined intervals in GAST-7.
- CBC and chemistry testing are clinically indicated.
- EGD has pathway-specific surveillance roles.
- CT has a structured surveillance role in selected pStage II/III or ypStage I–III pathways.
- Nutritional monitoring is important after gastrectomy.
- Most recurrences occur within the first two years, and approximately 90% occur by five years in the evidence summarized by NCCN.
- Additional follow-up after five years may be considered according to risk factors and comorbidities.

## Context-Dependent / Patient-Specific

- Exact follow-up intensity.
- Need for imaging outside the structured stage/treatment pathway.
- Need for EGD after surgical resection.
- Need for laboratory testing.
- Additional follow-up after five years.
- Management of nutritional sequelae.
- Evaluation of new symptoms or abnormal findings.

## Emerging / Uncertain

- The optimal surveillance strategy after curative-intent R0 resection.
- The balance between surveillance benefits, harms and costs.
- The degree to which more intensive surveillance changes clinically important outcomes.

## Not Established / Excluded

- A universal surveillance schedule for all gastric-cancer survivors.
- A universal tumor-marker schedule.
- A claim that more surveillance necessarily produces better survival.
- A claim that a normal surveillance test guarantees no recurrence.
- A claim that recurrence cannot occur after five years.

# Common Misconceptions

## Myth 1 — “Treatment finished means follow-up finished.”

**Fact:**  
Follow-up is a defined phase of care after treatment.

## Myth 2 — “Being on surveillance means I still have cancer.”

**Fact:**  
Surveillance may be performed when there is no current evidence of disease.

## Myth 3 — “Everyone with gastric cancer follows the same schedule.”

**Fact:**  
The NCCN schedule varies by stage and treatment.

## Myth 4 — “Surveillance is just CT scans.”

**Fact:**  
It can include clinical visits, selected laboratory testing, endoscopy, imaging and nutritional monitoring.

## Myth 5 — “An abnormal surveillance test means recurrence.”

**Fact:**  
An abnormal finding requires appropriate evaluation; it does not automatically establish recurrence.

## Myth 6 — “A normal scan means recurrence is impossible.”

**Fact:**  
A normal test is reassuring but cannot guarantee that recurrence will never occur.

## Myth 7 — “After five years, I never need medical care.”

**Fact:**  
Routine gastric-cancer-specific surveillance may end, but long-term care, survivorship and follow-up may still be appropriate.

## Myth 8 — “More scans are always safer.”

**Fact:**  
Surveillance must balance potential benefit with harms, burden, cost and uncertainty.

## Myth 9 — “Endoscopy is always required after gastrectomy.”

**Fact:**  
Its role depends on the treatment pathway and remaining anatomy. After total gastrectomy, routine surveillance endoscopy is not part of the standard pathway unless clinically indicated or symptoms occur.

## Myth 10 — “Surveillance is only about finding recurrence.”

**Fact:**  
It also includes monitoring the consequences of treatment, especially nutritional sequelae after gastrectomy.

# Clinical Safety Boundary

This CKO provides general, source-grounded education.

It does not:

- diagnose recurrence;
- determine whether an individual patient is disease-free;
- prescribe an individualized surveillance schedule;
- tell a patient to undergo or avoid a particular test;
- replace the treating team's interpretation of stage, treatment and anatomy;
- determine treatment of recurrent or metastatic disease.

Where the package describes NCCN surveillance intervals, those are presented as **guideline framework examples for defined treatment/stage pathways**, not as individualized instructions.
