# Educational Objectives

After reading this Population Package, the reader should be able to:

- Understand what PM3 is.
- Understand the basic concept that most genes are present in two copies.
- Understand the plain-language meaning of **in trans**.
- Recognize that PM3 is one individual ACMG Evidence Code within the PM evidence-code family.
- Understand why identifying variants on opposite gene copies may provide moderate evidence supporting pathogenicity in certain recessive disorders.
- Recognize that PM3 is one component of evidence rather than a final diagnosis.

# Knowledge Block 1 — What Is PM3?

## Patient Explanation

**PM3** is one of the standardized **ACMG Evidence Codes** used by genetics professionals when evaluating whether a genetic variant may cause disease.

It belongs to the **PM (Moderate Pathogenic Evidence)** family and represents one specific type of **moderate scientific evidence**.

---

## Clinical Importance

PM3 contributes one category of moderate evidence during professional variant interpretation.

---

## Key Concepts

- PM3
- Moderate evidence
- ACMG Evidence Code

---

# Knowledge Block 2 — Why Do Gene Copies Matter?

## Patient Explanation

Most people have **two copies of most genes**—one inherited from each biological parent.

Sometimes the location of genetic variants across these two copies becomes important when specialists evaluate whether a variant may contribute to disease.

---

## Clinical Importance

The relationship between variants on the two copies of a gene may influence variant interpretation.

---

## Key Concepts

- Two gene copies
- Gene inheritance
- Variant location

---

# Knowledge Block 3 — What Does "In Trans" Mean?

## Patient Explanation

The term **in trans** means that two genetic variants are located on **different copies** of the same gene.

In certain inherited conditions, finding a variant **in trans** with another known disease-causing variant may provide additional scientific evidence.

---

## Clinical Importance

The relative position of variants may contribute clinically meaningful evidence in appropriate disorders.

---

## Key Concepts

- In trans
- Opposite gene copies
- Scientific evidence

---

# Knowledge Block 4 — Does PM3 Mean the Variant Definitely Causes Disease?

## Patient Explanation

No.

PM3 is **not the final classification**.

Healthcare professionals always evaluate PM3 together with many other categories of scientific evidence before determining whether a variant should be classified as pathogenic.

---

## Clinical Importance

Final variant classification depends on the complete body of available evidence.

---

## Key Concepts

- Combined evidence
- Professional interpretation
- Final classification

---

# Knowledge Block 5 — Why Is PM3 Important?

## Patient Explanation

PM3 helps genetics professionals consider how genetic variants are arranged across the two copies of a gene when interpreting certain inherited conditions.

Using standardized evidence codes allows laboratories to interpret genetic findings more consistently.

---

## Clinical Importance

Standardized evaluation of variant configuration improves consistency and reproducibility in clinical genomics.

---

## Key Concepts

- Variant configuration
- Standardization
- Clinical genomics

---

# Common Misconception

**Myth**

Finding two variants in the same gene automatically means that both cause disease.

**Fact**

The relationship between the variants, the specific disease, and many other categories of scientific evidence must all be evaluated before reaching a final classification.

---

# Key Messages

- PM3 is one ACMG Evidence Code.
- PM3 belongs to the PM (Moderate Pathogenic Evidence) family.
- Most people have two copies of most genes.
- Finding variants on opposite gene copies may provide moderate supporting evidence in appropriate situations.
- PM3 contributes one category of evidence but is not the final variant classification.
