# 2. Educational Objectives

After using this package, the patient or knowledge-system user should be able to:

1. Explain what treatment-related toxicity means.
2. Understand that toxicity can occur with chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy and immunotherapy.
3. Recognize common symptom domains without assuming that every new symptom is caused by treatment.
4. Understand why clinical assessment is needed to determine severity and likely cause.
5. Understand the concept of standardized toxicity grading without needing to memorize a grading table.
6. Recognize why early reporting of concerning symptoms matters.
7. Understand that some toxicities can be anticipated and prevented or reduced.
8. Understand that supportive care is part of active cancer treatment and does not automatically mean treatment has failed.
9. Understand that serious toxicity may require treatment delay, modification or cessation by the treating team.
10. Recognize that some toxicities are acute while others may persist or appear later.
11. Understand that immunotherapy has distinctive immune-related toxicities.
12. Know which questions to ask the cancer-care team about a treatment side effect.

# 3. Scope

PP-0231 is the **cross-cutting treatment-toxicity and supportive-management layer** of the gastric-cancer Knowledge Graph.

It does not answer “Which cancer treatment should I receive?” Instead, it answers:

> **Once a treatment is being considered or delivered, what treatment-related problems should be recognized, how are they assessed and supported, and how can toxicity influence safe treatment delivery?**

The package therefore spans:

**Treatment exposure → possible toxicity → recognition → assessment → severity → supportive/preventive management → monitoring → treatment continuation/modification interface.**

This architecture is directly aligned with the ESMO-ASCO curriculum section on systemic-therapy toxicities, which includes assessment, diagnosis, treatment, prevention, toxicity grading, patient education, patient-reported outcomes, multidisciplinary management and treatment delay/modification/cessation. [Source: ESMO-ASCO 2023]

# 6. Clinical Knowledge Blocks

## Block 1 — What is treatment-related toxicity?

Treatment-related toxicity means an unwanted effect, symptom, sign, laboratory abnormality or longer-term consequence that is caused or plausibly related to an anti-cancer treatment.

A useful distinction is:

- **Adverse event:** an unwanted medical event occurring during treatment.
- **Treatment-related toxicity:** an adverse effect judged or suspected to be related to treatment.
- **Treatment-related sequela:** a longer-lasting consequence of treatment.
- **Disease symptom:** a symptom caused by the cancer or its complications.

The same symptom can have multiple possible causes.

For example, diarrhea may be related to chemotherapy, radiotherapy, immune-mediated inflammation, infection, surgery, another medicine or the cancer itself.

Therefore:

> A symptom appearing during treatment is not automatically proof that treatment caused it.

Clinical assessment establishes the most likely cause.

---

## Block 2 — Why can cancer treatment cause toxicity?

Different treatment classes affect the body differently.

### Chemotherapy

Many cytotoxic drugs affect cells that divide rapidly. This helps explain toxicities involving:

- blood-cell production;
- gastrointestinal mucosa;
- hair follicles;
- peripheral nerves for selected drugs.

### Radiotherapy

Radiation can affect normal tissues located within or near the treatment field. Gastric-cancer radiation may produce:

- skin reactions;
- nausea/vomiting;
- diarrhea;
- fatigue;
- low blood counts;
- eating/hydration difficulties.

[Source: ACS Stomach Cancer]

### Targeted therapy

Targeted drugs act on molecular pathways that may also have important functions in normal tissues. Toxicity therefore depends on the drug and its target.

Gastric-cancer examples include:

- hypertension and bleeding with anti-angiogenic treatment;
- pulmonary toxicity with selected HER2-directed therapies;
- gastrointestinal and laboratory toxicities with selected targeted agents.

[Sources: NCCN Gastric Cancer; NCI Gastric Cancer Treatment; ACS]

### Immunotherapy

Immune checkpoint inhibitors stimulate anti-tumor immune activity. Because the immune system also regulates normal tissues, immune-related adverse events can involve multiple organ systems.

ESMO-ASCO specifically describes immunotherapy as having a unique toxicity spectrum compared with chemotherapy and targeted therapy.

---

## Block 3 — Acute versus chronic toxicity

Toxicity may be:

### Acute

Occurs during treatment or shortly after treatment.

Examples:

- nausea;
- vomiting;
- diarrhea;
- cytopenias;
- infusion-related problems;
- some skin reactions.

### Persistent or chronic

May continue after treatment or develop as a longer-term consequence.

Examples:

- persistent peripheral neuropathy;
- chronic fatigue;
- nutritional consequences;
- late radiation effects;
- persistent functional impairment.

ESMO-ASCO emphasizes that both severity and chronicity influence management.

---

## Block 4 — Individual risk is variable

Patients do not experience toxicity in identical ways.

Risk and severity may vary with:

- treatment class;
- treatment combination;
- dose/intensity;
- performance status;
- age;
- comorbidities;
- concurrent irradiation;
- previous treatment;
- organ function;
- interacting medicines;
- baseline nutritional status;
- other patient-specific factors.

Therefore:

> The toxicity profile of a treatment is a population-level description, not a prediction of exactly what will happen to one person.

---

## Block 5 — How is toxicity recognized?

Recognition may begin with:

- a symptom reported by the patient;
- a caregiver observation;
- physical examination;
- blood tests;
- other laboratory tests;
- imaging or other diagnostic studies when clinically indicated.

Patient-reported symptoms are important because some toxicities first become apparent through what the patient feels or notices.

ESMO-ASCO explicitly recognizes the value of patient education and regular patient-reported outcomes in symptom management.

---

## Block 6 — Why is clinical assessment necessary?

The same symptom can have several causes.

For example:

**shortness of breath**

could reflect:

- anemia;
- infection;
- pulmonary toxicity;
- cardiac disease;
- thromboembolism;
- disease progression;
- another medical problem.

Likewise:

**diarrhea**

could reflect:

- chemotherapy;
- radiotherapy;
- immune-mediated colitis;
- infection;
- surgery-related changes;
- another cause.

Therefore:

**Symptom → assessment → cause → severity → management**

is safer than:

**Symptom → assume toxicity → self-treatment.**

---

## Block 7 — Toxicity grading

Clinical teams commonly use standardized grading systems such as the **Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE)**.

The purpose is to:

- communicate severity;
- standardize clinical documentation;
- compare events;
- support management decisions.

The patient does not need to memorize CTCAE.

The important principle is:

> Severity is assessed systematically because the seriousness of toxicity can affect monitoring and treatment decisions.

PP-0231 does not reproduce complete CTCAE tables.

---

## Block 8 — Prevention and prophylaxis

Some toxicities are predictable enough that preventive strategies can be used.

ESMO-ASCO states that evidence-based prophylactic strategies can reduce the frequency and severity of treatment-related toxicity.

Prevention can include, depending on treatment:

- anticipatory supportive medication;
- hydration/support;
- infection-risk measures;
- monitoring;
- nutritional support;
- patient education;
- early symptom reporting.

The exact preventive regimen is treatment- and patient-specific.

---

## Block 9 — Supportive management

Supportive management aims to reduce the burden of toxicity and help patients safely continue treatment when appropriate.

It can include:

- symptom-directed medication;
- hydration;
- nutritional support;
- infection management;
- monitoring;
- rehabilitation;
- physical/occupational support;
- psychosocial support;
- specialist referral.

Supportive care is not synonymous with “no more cancer treatment.”

It can occur **during active anti-cancer treatment**.

---

## Block 10 — Chemotherapy-related toxicity

Gastric-cancer chemotherapy can produce multiple toxicity domains.

ACS identifies common examples including:

- nausea/vomiting;
- diarrhea or constipation;
- mouth sores;
- myelosuppression;
- infection risk;
- bleeding risk from low platelets;
- anemia;
- neuropathy;
- hand-foot syndrome.

[Source: ACS Chemotherapy for Stomach Cancer]

Different regimens contain different drugs and therefore have different toxicity profiles.

For example, oxaliplatin is associated with peripheral neuropathy, while fluoropyrimidines can contribute to gastrointestinal toxicity and hand-foot syndrome.

The package should therefore teach **toxicity recognition and management principles**, not imply that every chemotherapy regimen has the same side effects.

---

## Block 11 — Hematologic toxicity

Chemotherapy can suppress bone marrow and reduce blood-cell counts.

Potential consequences include:

### Neutropenia / low white-cell counts

May increase infection risk.

### Anemia

May contribute to:

- fatigue;
- weakness;
- shortness of breath.

### Thrombocytopenia

May increase:

- bruising;
- bleeding.

The clinical significance depends on severity and the individual patient's situation.

Monitoring and management are determined by the treatment team.

---

## Block 12 — Gastrointestinal toxicity

Common domains include:

- nausea;
- vomiting;
- diarrhea;
- constipation;
- abdominal discomfort;
- appetite loss;
- mucosal symptoms.

Severe diarrhea can lead to:

- dehydration;
- electrolyte disturbances;
- nutritional compromise.

Therefore early reporting is important.

A key safety principle:

> Do not assume that all diarrhea during cancer treatment is “just chemotherapy.”

The differential may include infection, immune-mediated colitis, radiation injury, surgery-related effects and disease-related causes.

---

## Block 13 — Neuropathy

Peripheral neuropathy can occur with several chemotherapy agents used in gastric cancer, including platinum agents and taxanes.

Symptoms can include:

- numbness;
- tingling;
- burning;
- pain;
- temperature sensitivity;
- weakness;
- functional impairment.

Persistent neuropathy may affect:

- walking;
- balance;
- fine motor function;
- daily activities.

NCCN/ACS materials support recognition of chemotherapy-induced neuropathy and the need for appropriate supportive or rehabilitation input when functional risk is present.

---

## Block 14 — Hand-foot syndrome

Hand-foot syndrome is a treatment-associated skin toxicity that can occur with selected fluoropyrimidines.

It may progress from:

- redness;
- sensitivity;
- tingling;

to:

- pain;
- blistering;
- peeling.

Early recognition matters because worsening skin toxicity can interfere with daily activities and treatment delivery.

---

## Block 15 — Oral/mucosal toxicity

Mouth sores and mucosal injury can make it difficult to:

- eat;
- drink;
- maintain oral hygiene.

Supportive management may therefore intersect with:

- pain control;
- hydration;
- nutrition;
- oral care.

Detailed dental/oral-medicine protocols remain outside PP-0231.

---

## Block 16 — Radiotherapy toxicity

ACS identifies gastric-cancer radiation toxicities including:

- skin changes;
- nausea/vomiting;
- diarrhea;
- fatigue;
- low blood counts.

When radiation is combined with chemotherapy, toxicity may be greater.

Some patients may have difficulty eating or maintaining fluids and may require clinical nutritional/hydration support.

Late effects may also occur because radiation can affect nearby organs.

[Source: ACS Stomach Cancer]

PP-0231 owns the toxicity-management concept; PP-0033/PP-0207 retain radiation-treatment strategy and planning ownership.

---

## Block 17 — Targeted-therapy toxicity

NCI lists gastric-cancer targeted therapies including:

- ramucirumab;
- regorafenib;
- trastuzumab;
- trastuzumab deruxtecan;
- zolbetuximab.

[Source: NCI Treatment of Stomach Cancer]

Toxicity is drug-specific.

Examples from project materials include:

### Anti-angiogenic therapy

Potential concerns include:

- hypertension;
- bleeding;
- thrombosis;
- gastrointestinal perforation;
- wound-healing problems.

### Trastuzumab deruxtecan

Important toxicities include:

- hematologic abnormalities;
- nausea;
- interstitial lung disease/pneumonitis;
- selected cardiac concerns.

### Zolbetuximab

Reported domains include:

- nausea;
- vomiting;
- diarrhea;
- abdominal symptoms;
- fatigue;
- appetite/weight effects;
- neuropathy;
- blood-count changes;
- laboratory abnormalities.

The package uses these as **representative safety examples**, not exhaustive drug monographs.

---

## Block 18 — Immunotherapy toxicity

Immune checkpoint inhibitors have a distinctive toxicity spectrum.

Immune-related adverse events may affect:

- skin;
- gastrointestinal tract;
- endocrine organs;
- liver;
- lungs;
- other organs.

ESMO-ASCO emphasizes recognizing the signs and symptoms of immune-related adverse events and their management.

NCCN gastric-cancer treatment guidance directs management of immunotherapy-related toxicity to the dedicated NCCN immunotherapy-toxicity guideline.

PP-0231 therefore owns:

- recognition;
- conceptual assessment;
- urgency awareness;
- multidisciplinary interface;
- treatment-modification concept.

It does not reproduce the complete organ-by-organ immunotherapy-toxicity guideline.

---

## Block 19 — Toxicity versus disease progression

This distinction is especially important during immunotherapy.

A new symptom or abnormal test may represent:

- treatment toxicity;
- immune-related toxicity;
- infection;
- another medical problem;
- disease progression.

Therefore:

> A new symptom after treatment does not automatically mean the cancer has progressed.

Conversely:

> A symptom that occurs during treatment should not automatically be assumed to be a harmless treatment side effect.

Clinical assessment is required.

---

## Block 20 — Toxicity and treatment modification

When toxicity is clinically significant, the treating team may consider:

- continuing treatment with monitoring;
- additional supportive management;
- temporary treatment delay;
- dose modification;
- treatment cessation.

ESMO-ASCO explicitly includes dose delay, dose modification and treatment cessation when toxicity is unacceptable.

PP-0231 explains this **decision interface** but does not prescribe individualized changes.

---

## Block 21 — Toxicity can affect treatment intensity

ESMO-ASCO notes that toxicity can affect the relative-dose intensity of subsequent anti-cancer treatment.

This means toxicity can have two clinical consequences:

1. It can directly affect the patient's well-being.
2. It can affect the ability to deliver the intended anti-cancer treatment.

This is why toxicity management is an integral component of oncology rather than an optional add-on.

---

## Block 22 — Nutrition and hydration interface

Treatment-related:

- nausea;
- vomiting;
- diarrhea;
- mucositis;
- appetite loss;
- radiation effects;

can impair eating and drinking.

Support may include:

- dietary assessment;
- oral nutritional support;
- hydration;
- enteral support when clinically required.

PP-0231 owns the **toxicity → nutrition/hydration support interface**.

Detailed nutrition management belongs to dedicated nutrition packages.

---

## Block 23 — Long-term toxicity

Some treatment-related effects may persist after active treatment.

Examples include:

- chemotherapy-induced neuropathy;
- persistent fatigue;
- nutritional consequences;
- functional impairment;
- late radiation effects.

These should be linked to:

**PP-0229 Survivorship**

and

**PP-0230 Long-term Follow-up**

rather than duplicating those packages.

---

## Block 24 — Psychologic and quality-of-life impact

Treatment toxicity can affect:

- physical functioning;
- emotional well-being;
- ability to work;
- social participation;
- sleep;
- confidence in continuing treatment.

ESMO-ASCO explicitly recognizes physical and psychologic effects of treatment toxicity.

The package therefore includes the psychosocial dimension as supporting context.

---

## Block 25 — Multidisciplinary toxicity management

Treatment toxicity may involve:

- medical oncologists;
- radiation oncologists;
- surgeons;
- nurses;
- pharmacists;
- dietitians;
- physical/occupational therapists;
- specialty physicians;
- psychosocial professionals.

PP-0231 owns:

> multidisciplinary management **of treatment toxicity**.

# 7. Patient Explanation

## The most important message

> **Treatment side effects should not simply be endured in silence. Many toxicities can be prevented, treated or reduced, and some serious symptoms need prompt assessment.**

A patient should know:

**New symptom**

→ **tell the treatment team**

→ **identify the likely cause**

→ **assess severity**

→ **provide supportive care**

→ **decide whether treatment can safely continue**

This does not mean every symptom requires stopping treatment.

It means toxicity should be assessed rather than ignored.

# 10. Common Misconceptions

### Myth 1 — “Side effects are just something I have to tolerate.”

**Fact:** Many toxicities can be prevented, treated or reduced. Early communication with the treatment team can be important.

### Myth 2 — “A side effect means the treatment is working.”

**Fact:** Toxicity does not measure treatment effectiveness.

### Myth 3 — “More severe toxicity means the treatment is more effective.”

**Fact:** Severity of toxicity does not establish anti-cancer efficacy.

### Myth 4 — “Any symptom during chemotherapy must be chemotherapy toxicity.”

**Fact:** Symptoms can have many causes and may require assessment.

### Myth 5 — “Supportive care means cancer treatment has stopped.”

**Fact:** Supportive care can be provided while active cancer treatment continues.

### Myth 6 — “Immunotherapy side effects are the same as chemotherapy side effects.”

**Fact:** Immunotherapy has a distinctive spectrum of immune-related toxicities.

### Myth 7 — “If I have a side effect, I should stop the treatment myself.”

**Fact:** Treatment changes should be decided by the treating clinical team.

### Myth 8 — “Toxicity only happens while I am receiving treatment.”

**Fact:** Some treatment effects can persist or appear later.

---

# 11. Key Messages

- Report new or worsening symptoms to the treatment team.
- Do not assume a symptom has only one possible cause.
- Toxicity is assessed according to severity and clinical context.
- Prevention and supportive care are part of modern cancer treatment.
- Serious toxicity can affect treatment delivery.
- Immunotherapy-related toxicity may require special assessment.
- Persistent treatment effects can require long-term support.
- Never independently stop, restart or alter cancer treatment based only on general educational information.

---

# 12. Patient Questions

Patients may ask:

1. What side effects are most important for my specific treatment?
2. Which symptoms should I report immediately?
3. Which symptoms can be monitored at home?
4. How will you determine whether a symptom is caused by treatment or by the cancer?
5. How will my blood counts be monitored?
6. Could this treatment cause neuropathy?
7. Could this treatment affect eating or hydration?
8. Could I develop long-term side effects?
9. What supportive treatments are available?
10. Could toxicity require a treatment delay or dose adjustment?
11. If I am receiving immunotherapy, what immune-related symptoms should I know about?
12. Who should I contact outside a scheduled appointment?
13. Could this side effect affect my ability to continue treatment?
14. Will a pharmacist, dietitian, rehabilitation specialist or other clinician be involved?

# 14. Clinical Safety Boundary

PP-0231 provides educational knowledge, not individualized medical instructions.

The package must not:

- diagnose a patient's toxicity;
- determine the patient's toxicity grade from a narrative alone;
- prescribe supportive medication;
- prescribe corticosteroids or immunosuppression;
- instruct a patient to stop or alter cancer treatment;
- provide individualized dose modifications;
- establish a universal emergency threshold independent of treatment context.

For a real patient:

> The treatment team determines the cause, severity and appropriate management of suspected toxicity.
