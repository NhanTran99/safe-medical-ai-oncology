# Educational Objectives

By the end of this Population Package, the learner should be able to:

1. Explain what next-generation sequencing (NGS) is in plain language.
2. Distinguish a testing technology/platform from an individual biomarker.
3. Understand why NGS can assess multiple genes and molecular events within one testing approach.
4. Understand the difference between single-biomarker testing and broader genomic profiling.
5. Understand when NGS may be considered in gastric cancer and why the clinical context matters.
6. Understand why adequate tumor material and validated testing are important.
7. Recognize major classes of findings that NGS may identify, including mutations, amplifications, deletions, gene fusions, MSI and TMB.
8. Understand that some gastric-cancer biomarkers are better represented by non-NGS approaches such as IHC or ISH.
9. Understand the relationship between tissue-based and selected blood-based genomic testing.
10. Interpret the concepts of a positive finding, a negative test, and “no actionable alteration identified” without overinterpreting them.
11. Understand that an NGS result does not automatically determine treatment.
12. Recognize the major limitations and boundaries of NGS testing.

# Clinical Knowledge Blocks

## Knowledge Block 1 — What Is NGS?

Next-generation sequencing (NGS) is a molecular testing technology that can analyze many genetic or genomic features in parallel. In gastric cancer, it can be used as a broader molecular-testing approach rather than as a test for one single biomarker.

The important distinction is:

> **NGS is a testing technology; HER2, MSI, TMB, or a genomic alteration are examples of molecular findings or biomarkers that may be assessed using molecular testing.**

NCCN describes NGS as offering the opportunity to assess numerous mutations simultaneously together with other molecular events such as amplification, deletions, TMB and MSI status.

---

## Knowledge Block 2 — Why Is NGS Different From a Single-Biomarker Test?

A single-biomarker test asks a focused question, for example whether a particular protein is expressed or whether a particular genomic alteration is present.

NGS can ask a broader set of molecular questions in one testing approach.

This does **not** mean that NGS is always better or should replace other tests. NCCN identifies IHC, ISH and targeted gene PCR as preferred initial approaches for biomarker assessment, while validated NGS may be considered later in the clinical course in selected patients with sufficient tumor tissue.

---

## Knowledge Block 3 — What Is Multigene Panel Testing?

A multigene panel evaluates a predefined collection of genes or molecular targets.

NGS is one technology that can be used to perform multigene testing. Therefore:

> **A gene panel describes what is being examined; NGS describes how sequencing-based analysis can be performed.**

The size and content of panels vary. No single commercial panel should be treated as universally required by this package.

---

## Knowledge Block 4 — What Can NGS Detect?

Depending on assay design, NGS may identify different classes of molecular events, including:

- sequence mutations;
- gene amplifications;
- deletions;
- gene fusions;
- MSI;
- TMB.

The exact findings that can be detected depend on the assay and the molecular targets included.

Therefore:

> **Broad testing does not mean that every possible molecular alteration will necessarily be detected.**

---

## Knowledge Block 5 — NGS and Gastric-Cancer Biomarkers

NGS may contribute to assessment of several molecular features relevant to gastric cancer.

Examples supported by the supplied NCCN framework include:

- HER2 amplification;
- BRAF alterations;
- MSI;
- NTRK fusions;
- RET fusions;
- TMB.

These examples do not transfer ownership of the dedicated biomarker packages.

PP-0181 owns HER2 testing.
PP-0182 owns MSI/MMR testing.
PP-0183 owns PD-L1 testing.
PP-0184 owns CLDN18.2 testing.
PP-0185 owns TMB.
PP-0186 owns FGFR2 testing.

PP-0187 explains how NGS can function as a broader molecular-testing platform in relation to these biomarkers.

---

## Knowledge Block 6 — Does Every Biomarker Need NGS?

No.

Some clinically important biomarkers are primarily evaluated by methods such as IHC or ISH.

For example:

- PD-L1 is assessed through protein-expression testing;
- CLDN18.2 is assessed through IHC;
- HER2 may be assessed by IHC with ISH confirmation in appropriate settings.

This is why NGS should not be presented as a universal replacement for all biomarker testing.

---

## Knowledge Block 7 — When May NGS Be Considered?

The supplied NCCN v2.2026 source states that IHC/ISH/targeted gene PCR are preferred approaches to assess biomarkers initially. It also states that NGS testing through a CLIA-approved laboratory may be considered later in the clinical course for patients with sufficient tumor tissue available for testing.

This establishes an important principle:

> **NGS use is clinically contextual rather than an automatic first test for every patient.**

In selected advanced or metastatic settings, broader genomic profiling may be useful when multiple molecular questions need to be assessed or when potentially targetable alterations are being sought.

---

## Knowledge Block 8 — Why Does Tumor Material Matter?

Tissue-based NGS requires an appropriate tumor specimen.

The clinical usefulness of the test therefore depends partly on:

- whether tumor material is available;
- whether enough material remains for testing;
- whether the specimen is appropriate for the assay.

A patient should not interpret “NGS would be useful” as meaning that NGS can always be performed successfully.

---

## Knowledge Block 9 — What Does “Validated Assay” Mean at This Level?

For patient-facing purposes, a validated assay means that the testing method has undergone appropriate laboratory validation for its intended clinical use.

The supplied NCCN source specifically refers to validated NGS and use through a CLIA-approved laboratory in the relevant clinical framework.

This package does not define technical validation thresholds or laboratory performance metrics.

---

## Knowledge Block 10 — Can NGS Be Performed on Blood?

Yes, selected blood-based genomic testing can use NGS-based approaches.

NCCN describes circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) as a blood-based source of genomic information and states that, when tissue is limited or a patient with advanced/metastatic gastric cancer cannot undergo traditional biopsy, validated NGS-based comprehensive genomic profiling/multigene panel testing in a CLIA-approved laboratory may be considered.

However:

> **Blood-based NGS and tissue-based NGS are not automatically interchangeable in every clinical situation.**

Detailed ctDNA biology and longitudinal monitoring belong to the dedicated ctDNA package.

---

## Knowledge Block 11 — What Does a Positive NGS Result Mean?

A positive result means that the assay identified a molecular finding within its tested scope.

It does not automatically mean:

- the finding explains every aspect of the cancer;
- the finding is definitely actionable;
- a particular drug must be used;
- treatment will necessarily work.

The clinical meaning requires interpretation in context.

---

## Knowledge Block 12 — What Does a Negative NGS Result Mean?

A negative result means that no relevant finding was identified by the assay within its analytical and interpretive scope.

It does **not** necessarily mean:

> “There are no genetic changes in the cancer.”

A molecular alteration may be outside the panel, outside the assay's detectable alteration classes, insufficiently represented in the tested specimen, or below the test's ability to detect.

---

## Knowledge Block 13 — What Does “No Actionable Alteration Identified” Mean?

This phrase should not be confused with “no mutation found.”

It means that no alteration considered clinically actionable under the relevant testing and interpretation framework was identified.

The distinction is:

**No alteration detected**
≠
**No actionable alteration detected**
≠
**No molecular changes exist.**

Detailed actionability and treatment matching belong to downstream packages.

---

## Knowledge Block 14 — What Does an Uncertain Finding Mean?

Broad molecular testing can identify findings whose clinical significance is not immediately clear.

Such findings require appropriate interpretation.

This package does not teach detailed variant classification, VUS interpretation, or laboratory-tiering systems.

---

## Knowledge Block 15 — Does NGS Replace Pathology, Endoscopy, Biopsy or Imaging?

No.

Gastric-cancer diagnosis and staging remain based on clinical evaluation, endoscopy, biopsy/pathology and appropriate imaging/staging assessment.

NGS provides molecular information.

It is therefore best understood as:

> **one component of comprehensive cancer assessment, not a replacement for diagnosis or staging.**

---

## Knowledge Block 16 — Does NGS Automatically Determine Treatment?

No.

A genomic finding may provide information that is potentially relevant to treatment, but treatment decisions require:

- the patient's diagnosis;
- disease extent;
- biomarker result;
- clinical condition;
- available evidence;
- treatment eligibility;
- guideline context;
- patient preferences and goals.

The specific treatment-selection layer belongs downstream.

---

## Knowledge Block 17 — Is Tumor NGS the Same as Germline Genetic Testing?

No.

Tumor/somatic NGS evaluates molecular changes in the cancer.

Germline testing evaluates inherited genetic variants.

A tumor NGS result should not automatically be interpreted as evidence of an inherited cancer syndrome.

---

## Knowledge Block 18 — Major Limitations

Important limitations include:

1. Not every genomic alteration is covered by every panel.
2. Not every alteration class is detectable by every assay.
3. Adequate tumor material may not always be available.
4. Blood-based testing may not detect every alteration present in tumor tissue.
5. A detected alteration may have uncertain significance.
6. A detected alteration may not be clinically actionable.
7. Clinical interpretation may change as evidence evolves.
8. NGS does not replace pathology, staging or clinical assessment.

# Clinical Importance

NGS can provide a broader molecular view of gastric cancer than a test designed to answer only one biomarker question.

Its clinical value is greatest when the clinical question requires broader genomic characterization and when an appropriately validated assay can be performed on an adequate specimen.

The key patient-facing principle is:

> **NGS provides molecular information; it does not independently make the clinical decision.**

# Key Concepts

- NGS is a testing technology, not a single biomarker.
- Multigene panels examine multiple predefined molecular targets.
- NGS can evaluate multiple molecular events simultaneously.
- NGS may identify mutations, amplifications, deletions, gene fusions, MSI and TMB depending on assay design.
- IHC/ISH/targeted PCR remain important approaches and are not universally replaced by NGS.
- NGS may be considered through validated testing in selected clinical contexts.
- Adequate tumor material matters.
- Selected blood-based NGS can be considered when tissue is limited or biopsy is not feasible in appropriate advanced/metastatic settings.
- A positive NGS finding requires interpretation.
- A negative NGS result does not prove that no genomic alteration exists.
- “No actionable alteration identified” is not the same as “no mutation exists.”
- Tumor NGS is not the same as germline testing.
- NGS does not automatically select a drug.

# Common Misconceptions

## Myth 1
**“NGS is one biomarker.”**

**Fact:** NGS is a molecular testing technology that can assess multiple molecular features.

## Myth 2
**“NGS is always better than IHC or ISH.”**

**Fact:** Different tests answer different molecular questions. NCCN identifies IHC/ISH/targeted PCR as preferred initial approaches for biomarker assessment.

## Myth 3
**“Every gastric-cancer patient must have NGS first.”**

**Fact:** The supplied NCCN framework does not establish universal first-line comprehensive NGS for every patient.

## Myth 4
**“A negative NGS means there are no mutations.”**

**Fact:** A negative result is limited by the assay, panel, specimen and detectable alteration classes.

## Myth 5
**“A mutation found by NGS tells the doctor which drug to give.”**

**Fact:** A molecular finding may be clinically relevant, but treatment selection requires additional interpretation and clinical context.

## Myth 6
**“Tumor NGS is the same as inherited genetic testing.”**

**Fact:** Tumor/somatic testing and germline testing answer different questions.

## Myth 7
**“NGS replaces biopsy and pathology.”**

**Fact:** NGS adds molecular information; it does not replace the diagnostic and staging framework.

## Myth 8
**“NGS detects everything in a tumor.”**

**Fact:** Detection depends on assay design, specimen quality and the molecular alterations the test is designed to identify.

# Key Messages

1. **NGS is a platform for broader molecular testing, not a single biomarker.**
2. **NGS can evaluate multiple genes and molecular events in one testing approach.**
3. **NGS does not replace IHC, ISH, pathology or other clinical assessment.**
4. **Validated NGS may be considered in selected gastric-cancer clinical contexts.**
5. **Adequate tumor material is important for tissue-based NGS.**
6. **Selected blood-based genomic testing can be useful when tissue is limited or biopsy is not feasible in appropriate advanced/metastatic settings.**
7. **A molecular finding is not automatically an actionable treatment target.**
8. **A negative NGS result does not prove that no genomic alteration exists.**
9. **Detailed genomic-report and variant interpretation belong to downstream knowledge packages.**
10. **The final clinical meaning of an NGS result must be determined in context.**
