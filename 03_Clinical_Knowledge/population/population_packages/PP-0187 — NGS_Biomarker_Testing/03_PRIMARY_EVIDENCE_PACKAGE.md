# Clinical Question

## Primary Educational Question

> **What is NGS biomarker testing, what can it test for in gastric cancer, and when may it be useful?**

## Secondary Questions

1. What is NGS?
2. How does NGS differ from an individual biomarker?
3. What molecular events can NGS assess?
4. When may NGS be considered in gastric cancer?
5. Why do tissue availability and assay validation matter?
6. Can NGS be used in blood-based genomic testing?
7. What do positive and negative results mean?
8. Why does NGS not automatically determine treatment?
9. What are the major limitations?

---

# Educational Intent

Provide a patient-facing, evidence-grounded explanation of NGS as a clinical molecular-testing platform.

The package is designed to prevent several common misconceptions:

- that NGS is itself a biomarker;
- that NGS should replace all other biomarker tests;
- that every gastric-cancer patient necessarily needs comprehensive NGS;
- that a mutation automatically determines treatment;
- that a negative NGS means no genomic alterations exist;
- that tumor NGS is equivalent to germline testing.

# Evidence Hierarchy

## Level 1 — Direct Disease-Specific Guideline Evidence

**NCCN Gastric Cancer Version 2.2026**

This is the principal source for PP-0187's gastric-cancer-specific clinical claims.

The NCCN source states that:

- IHC/ISH/targeted gene PCR is the preferred initial approach to biomarker assessment;
- validated NGS through a CLIA-approved laboratory may be considered later in the clinical course when sufficient tumor tissue is available;
- NGS can assess numerous mutations simultaneously and other molecular events such as amplification, deletions, TMB and MSI;
- MSI may be assessed by PCR/NGS;
- selected blood-based comprehensive genomic profiling/MGPT using validated NGS may be considered in appropriate circumstances when tissue is limited or biopsy is not feasible.

# Detailed Evidence Notes

## Evidence Note 1 — NGS as a broad molecular platform

NCCN describes NGS as providing an opportunity to assess numerous mutations simultaneously together with molecular events including amplification, deletions, TMB and MSI.

This directly supports positioning NGS as a broad molecular-testing platform.

---

## Evidence Note 2 — NGS versus initial biomarker testing

The NCCN framework explicitly retains IHC/ISH/targeted gene PCR as preferred approaches to assess biomarkers initially.

Therefore the package must not claim that NGS replaces those approaches.

---

## Evidence Note 3 — Validated NGS

NCCN refers to NGS testing through a CLIA-approved laboratory and a validated assay in the relevant gastric-cancer context.

The package therefore includes:

- validated testing;
- laboratory quality context;

but excludes technical validation parameters.

---

## Evidence Note 4 — Tissue availability

NCCN conditions the relevant NGS consideration on sufficient tumor tissue being available.

This supports a patient-facing explanation that tissue quantity and suitability matter.

---

## Evidence Note 5 — Blood-based genomic testing

NCCN describes ctDNA as a blood-based source of genomic information and states that when tissue is limited, or when patients with advanced/metastatic gastric cancer cannot undergo traditional biopsy, validated NGS-based comprehensive genomic profiling/MGPT may be considered in a CLIA-approved laboratory.

This supports inclusion of blood-based NGS as a selected context, not as a universal replacement for tissue.

---

## Evidence Note 6 — Targetable alterations

NCCN states that genomic alterations detected from DNA shed by gastric carcinomas can identify targetable alterations and may provide information about evolution of clones with altered treatment-response profiles.

PP-0187 may therefore introduce potential clinical relevance.

It must stop short of drug-specific treatment matching.

---

# Clinical Claims Summary

The evidence supports the following major messages:

1. NGS is a broad molecular-testing approach capable of assessing multiple molecular events.
2. NGS can provide information about mutations and other genomic events.
3. NGS can include assessment of TMB and MSI.
4. NGS does not replace all other biomarker-testing methods.
5. Validated NGS may be considered in selected clinical contexts.
6. Adequate tumor material is important for tissue-based testing.
7. Selected blood-based genomic testing may be useful when tissue is limited or biopsy is not feasible in appropriate advanced/metastatic settings.
8. Molecular findings may have potential clinical relevance but require interpretation.
9. NGS does not automatically prescribe treatment.
10. The scope and limitations of the assay must be considered when interpreting results.

# Evidence Gaps

The supplied project source set does not provide sufficient disease-specific evidence to establish:

- one universal NGS panel;
- a universal gene list;
- a universal sequencing platform;
- exact analytical sensitivity/specificity;
- universal limit-of-detection thresholds;
- one universal tissue percentage threshold;
- universal blood-versus-tissue substitution rules;
- universal repeat-NGS timing;
- a single genomic-report interpretation algorithm.

These details must not be invented.
