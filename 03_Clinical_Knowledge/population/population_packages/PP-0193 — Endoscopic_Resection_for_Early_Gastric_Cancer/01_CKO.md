# Educational Objectives

After reading this Population Package, the reader should be able to:

1. Explain what endoscopic resection (ER) means in early gastric cancer.
2. Understand why ER can sometimes treat an early gastric cancer without removing part or all of the stomach.
3. Understand why depth of tumor invasion and the risk of lymph-node spread are central to deciding whether endoscopic treatment is appropriate.
4. Understand the roles of lesion size, histologic differentiation, ulcerative findings, lymphovascular invasion, and resection margins in assessing suitability and curative status.
5. Understand the difference between endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR) and endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) at a strategic level.
6. Understand why ESD is preferred in the NCCN endoscopic-resection framework while still requiring appropriate expertise and patient selection.
7. Understand why the resection specimen must undergo careful pathology review after ER.
8. Understand the difference between a curative and a non-curative endoscopic resection.
9. Understand why endoscopic resection can provide important staging/pathologic information as well as treatment.
10. Understand why some patients may need additional treatment after ER.
11. Understand why follow-up endoscopy remains important after apparently curative endoscopic treatment.
12. Know which questions to ask the cancer care team when ER is being considered.

# Clinical Knowledge Blocks

## Block 1 — What Is Endoscopic Resection?

### Definition

Endoscopic resection is a treatment in which a gastric lesion is removed through an endoscope rather than by surgically removing part or all of the stomach.

In gastric cancer, the term **endoscopic resection (ER)** is used as an umbrella concept that includes:

- **Endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR)**.
- **Endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD)**.

The important point for patients is that ER is not simply a diagnostic biopsy. It is a therapeutic procedure intended to remove a selected cancer completely while providing a larger specimen for detailed pathologic assessment.

### Patient Explanation

If a gastric cancer is very early and has features suggesting that it has a low risk of having spread beyond the area that can be removed endoscopically, doctors may be able to remove it through an endoscope.

This can avoid removing part or all of the stomach in appropriately selected patients.

However, **not every early gastric cancer is suitable for ER**. The decision depends on several tumor and pathology features.

---

## Block 2 — Why Can Endoscopic Resection Treat Some Early Gastric Cancers?

The central clinical idea is:

> **Local treatment is appropriate only when the risk of cancer remaining locally or having spread to lymph nodes is sufficiently low.**

Early gastric cancer is often described in terms of how deeply the tumor has invaded the stomach wall.

A tumor confined to the mucosa has a different risk profile from a tumor that has penetrated more deeply into the submucosa or beyond.

The deeper the tumor extends, the greater the concern that cancer cells may have reached lymphatic channels or lymph nodes. Once that risk becomes clinically important, simply removing the visible lesion through the endoscope may no longer provide adequate oncologic treatment.

Therefore ER is not selected simply because a lesion looks small.

It is selected because the **overall tumor profile** is compatible with local endoscopic treatment.

---

## Block 3 — Which Early Gastric Cancers Are Central to the ER Strategy?

The current NCCN framework identifies ER as a primary treatment option for selected early-stage **Tis or T1a** gastric tumors.

NCI materials similarly describe EMR experience in carefully selected early-stage tumors, including Tis/T1a tumors with favorable features.

This does **not** mean that every Tis or T1a tumor automatically qualifies for ER.

The tumor still needs to be assessed for features such as:

- depth of invasion;
- histologic differentiation;
- lesion size;
- ulcerative findings;
- lymphovascular invasion;
- ability to achieve complete resection;
- final margin status.

Some carefully selected superficial submucosal lesions may also enter an expanded endoscopic-resection framework. These situations require particularly careful assessment and should not be reduced to a simple patient-applied checklist.

---

## Block 4 — Why Does Depth of Invasion Matter?

Depth is one of the most important concepts in deciding whether local endoscopic treatment is sufficient.

A superficial tumor that remains within the mucosa has a lower likelihood of lymph-node spread than a tumor that has invaded more deeply.

This distinction explains why an early lesion can sometimes be treated endoscopically while a deeper lesion may require surgery.

Patients may therefore hear terms such as:

- **Tis** — carcinoma in situ.
- **T1a** — tumor invading the lamina propria or muscularis mucosae.
- **T1b** — tumor invading the submucosa.

The terminology can be confusing. The practical concept is:

> **The deeper the cancer has invaded, the more carefully the care team must consider whether local endoscopic removal alone is oncologically adequate.**

---

## Block 5 — Why Does Lymph-Node Risk Matter?

Endoscopic resection removes the visible/local lesion.

It does not provide the same regional lymph-node removal that accompanies gastrectomy with lymph-node assessment.

Therefore the endoscopic strategy is most appropriate when the probability of clinically important lymph-node disease is sufficiently low.

NCI specifically notes that intramucosal tumors have a lower risk of nodal metastasis than submucosal tumors.

This is one of the reasons that depth of invasion is central to ER selection.

The correct clinical reasoning is:

**Depth / tumor features**

→ **estimated risk of lymphatic or nodal spread**

→ **whether local endoscopic treatment is sufficient**

→ **whether surgery should instead be considered**

---

## Block 6 — What Other Tumor Features Matter?

Depth is not the only consideration.

### Histologic differentiation

The pathology report may describe how differentiated the cancer cells are.

Certain favorable histologic patterns are incorporated into endoscopic-resection criteria.

### Lesion size

Smaller lesions are generally more compatible with the classic favorable-risk ER pattern.

NCI describes EMR experience in selected early-stage lesions of **≤2 cm** with other favorable characteristics.

NCCN also incorporates size into its curative-resection framework and expanded indications.

### Ulcerative findings

Ulceration changes the risk profile and is therefore part of ER eligibility and curative assessment.

### Lymphovascular invasion

Lymphovascular invasion means that cancer cells are identified within lymphatic or blood-vessel spaces.

Its presence is an important adverse feature in assessing whether endoscopic removal alone is sufficient.

### Margins

The resection specimen is evaluated to determine whether the tumor has been completely removed at the relevant margins.

These features work together. No single feature should be treated as a universal patient-applied rule.

---

## Block 7 — Why Does Pre-ER Assessment Matter?

Before ER, the clinical team needs the best possible estimate of:

- tumor location;
- tumor extent;
- depth of invasion;
- lesion morphology;
- other features that may influence suitability.

NCCN emphasizes that accurate clinical staging is especially important when ER is being considered.

Endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) may be used in selected early-stage cases when the team needs additional information about the depth of invasion.

However, no pre-treatment test can replace the information obtained from the final resection specimen when ER is performed.

Therefore the pathway is not:

**endoscopy → ER → finished**

It is:

**pre-ER assessment**

→ **ER**

→ **definitive specimen pathology**

→ **curative assessment**

---

## Block 8 — Why Is ER Also Important for Staging?

ER has an unusual dual role.

### It can be therapeutic

The lesion can be removed.

### It can also be diagnostically and pathologically informative

The larger resection specimen allows the pathologist to evaluate features that may be difficult to determine from a small biopsy alone, including:

- depth of invasion;
- differentiation;
- margins;
- lymphovascular invasion;
- other features relevant to curative assessment.

NCCN specifically identifies endoscopic resection as important for accurate staging of early-stage cancers.

This is why ER should not be thought of simply as “removing a visible spot.”

The resection specimen can determine whether the original assumption of an appropriately superficial cancer was correct and whether the endoscopic treatment was actually curative.

---

## Block 9 — EMR and ESD: What Is the Strategic Difference?

Both are endoscopic resection techniques, but they are not identical.

### EMR

Endoscopic mucosal resection is an established approach for selected superficial lesions.

It is particularly associated with small, favorable-risk lesions.

### ESD

Endoscopic submucosal dissection is designed to allow more extensive and often en-bloc removal of appropriate lesions.

The NCCN framework currently lists **ESD as preferred** within the endoscopic-resection approach.

NCCN also notes that ESD requires greater skill and instrumentation and carries important procedural risks, including perforation.

### What patients should take away

> **ESD is not simply “a better EMR.” It is a different endoscopic technique with broader resection capability in appropriate lesions, greater technical complexity, and its own risk profile.**

The detailed technical ownership belongs to the dedicated EMR and ESD Population Packages.

---

## Block 10 — Why Is Complete or En-Bloc Resection Important?

The goal of ER is not merely to remove part of a lesion.

The clinical objective is to achieve a resection that permits reliable assessment and, when appropriate, definitive local treatment.

An **en-bloc resection** means the lesion is removed as one piece.

This can make assessment of:

- tumor size;
- depth;
- margins;
- other pathologic features

more reliable than fragmented removal.

The importance of complete resection therefore extends beyond procedural technique. It directly affects the confidence with which the pathology can determine whether the treatment was curative.

---

## Block 11 — What Happens to the Resection Specimen?

After ER, the specimen should be carefully examined by an experienced gastrointestinal pathologist.

The pathology assessment is central to the next decision.

The team may need to establish:

- the final depth of invasion;
- histologic differentiation;
- margin status;
- lymphovascular invasion;
- whether the observed features fit a curative endoscopic-resection pattern.

This is why **the final pathology report after ER is not a formality**.

It is the bridge between the procedure and the next treatment decision.

---

## Block 12 — What Does “Curative Resection” Mean?

A curative endoscopic resection means, in broad terms, that the pathology findings indicate that the tumor has been removed in a way considered oncologically adequate for endoscopic treatment.

NCCN's curative framework incorporates features including:

- small lesion size in the relevant setting;
- superficial invasion;
- appropriate histologic differentiation;
- complete excision;
- negative lateral and deep margins;
- absence of lymphovascular invasion in the relevant curative framework.

The exact combination of criteria depends on the lesion and guideline framework.

The patient-facing message is:

> **Curative status is determined from the complete picture of the resected specimen, not from the appearance of the lesion alone.**

---

## Block 13 — What Does “Non-Curative” Mean?

A non-curative endoscopic resection does **not** necessarily mean that the procedure was a failure.

It means that the final pathology shows features indicating that endoscopic treatment alone may not provide sufficient oncologic control.

Examples can include findings such as:

- deeper-than-expected invasion;
- unfavorable histologic features;
- lymphovascular invasion;
- incomplete or positive margins;
- other features outside the accepted curative criteria.

The next step depends on the complete pathology and clinical context.

The care team may consider:

- additional local treatment;
- surgical treatment;
- other appropriate cancer treatment;
- or, in selected situations, surveillance.

The exact management is outside the ownership of this PP.

---

## Block 14 — When Might Surgery Be Needed After ER?

The conceptual reason is straightforward:

> **If the cancer's risk of residual disease or lymph-node spread is higher than can safely be managed by local endoscopic treatment, surgery may be needed.**

Gastrectomy can remove the involved portion of the stomach or the stomach more extensively, together with regional lymph-node assessment.

The decision is not based solely on the fact that ER was performed.

It is based on the **final pathology and overall clinical situation**.

Therefore:

**ER**

↓

**pathology**

↓

**curative or non-curative**

↓

**additional management if required**

is the correct conceptual pathway.

Detailed gastrectomy selection and surgical technique belong to the relevant surgical Population Packages.

---

## Block 15 — ER Versus Gastrectomy

| Feature | Endoscopic Resection | Gastrectomy |
|---|---|---|
| Main concept | Local endoscopic removal | Surgical removal of part/all of stomach |
| Typical role | Selected early cancers with favorable risk profile | Disease where local endoscopic treatment is insufficient or inappropriate |
| Lymph-node assessment | Does not provide surgical regional lymph-node dissection | Includes regional lymph-node assessment as part of oncologic surgery |
| Stomach preservation | Generally preserves the stomach | Removes part or all of the stomach depending on operation |
| Pathology | Provides a resection specimen | Provides surgical specimen and nodes |
| Main selection logic | Low enough risk for local treatment | Higher risk or unsuitable for endoscopic treatment |
| Technical ownership | PP-0193 strategy; EMR/ESD in dedicated PPs | Surgical Population Packages |

The important message is:

> **ER is not simply a smaller operation. It is an oncologic strategy that is appropriate only when the tumor's risk profile makes local treatment reasonable.**

---

## Block 16 — Why Does Experience of the Endoscopist and Center Matter?

ER is a technically specialized treatment.

NCI emphasizes careful patient selection and treatment by an experienced endoscopist.

NCCN similarly emphasizes appropriate expertise and center experience for endoscopic procedures.

This matters because successful ER depends on more than identifying a small lesion.

The team needs to:

- assess whether the lesion is appropriate;
- choose the appropriate endoscopic approach;
- perform the procedure safely;
- obtain an adequate specimen;
- interpret the pathology correctly;
- coordinate additional treatment if the resection is non-curative.

Therefore patients should feel comfortable asking about the team's experience with therapeutic gastric endoscopy.

---

## Block 17 — What Are the Main Risks?

Endoscopic resection avoids the larger operation associated with gastrectomy, but it is still an invasive therapeutic procedure.

Potential procedural complications include:

- bleeding;
- perforation;
- other procedure-related complications.

The exact risk depends on the lesion, procedure, operator expertise, and clinical context.

This PP does not provide detailed complication-management instructions.

Patients should instead follow the specific post-procedure instructions provided by their treating team and know how to contact the team if concerning symptoms occur.

---

## Block 18 — What Happens After a Curative ER?

A curative ER does not eliminate the need for follow-up.

NCCN recommends structured EGD surveillance after ER for Tis/T1a disease.

The reason is that follow-up can help detect:

- local recurrence;
- metachronous gastric lesions;
- other clinically relevant changes.

The surveillance plan should be understood as part of the treatment pathway rather than as evidence that the ER was unsuccessful.

Detailed surveillance schedules and recurrence-management algorithms belong to dedicated follow-up and recurrence Population Packages.

---

## Block 19 — What Happens After a Non-Curative ER?

The first step is to understand **why** the resection was classified as non-curative.

Different findings imply different clinical concerns.

For example:

- positive margins raise concern for residual local disease;
- deeper invasion raises concern for more advanced local disease;
- lymphovascular invasion raises concern for lymphatic or vascular spread;
- unfavorable histology may change the risk assessment.

The next decision is therefore individualized and multidisciplinary.

This package deliberately stops at the decision bridge:

> **non-curative pathology → reassessment of oncologic treatment needs**

rather than prescribing a specific treatment for an individual patient.

---

## Block 20 — Why Is ER Particularly Important in High-Incidence Settings?

Much of the historical experience with early detection and endoscopic treatment comes from countries with high gastric-cancer incidence and established early-detection programs, particularly Japan and other parts of Asia.

NCI notes the extensive Asian experience with EMR in selected early-stage tumors.

The clinical principle is not that ER is relevant only in Asia.

Rather, ER works best as part of a system in which:

- early gastric cancer is detected;
- appropriate staging is available;
- expert therapeutic endoscopy is available;
- gastrointestinal pathology expertise is available;
- structured surveillance is feasible.

This helps explain why the practical role of ER can differ between healthcare systems.

# Patient Explanation

## A Simple Way to Understand the ER Pathway

A useful way to think about endoscopic treatment is:

**1. Find a very early gastric cancer**

↓

**2. Assess whether it appears suitable for local treatment**

↓

**3. Remove it through an endoscope**

↓

**4. Examine the entire resection specimen**

↓

**5. Decide whether the removal was curative**

↓

**6. Continue surveillance or consider additional treatment when needed**

The critical step is number 4.

A lesion can look favorable before treatment but have a different final pathology after removal.

That is why the final pathology report matters so much.

---

# Clinical Importance

ER can provide several benefits for appropriately selected early gastric cancers:

- avoids or reduces the need for gastric surgery;
- preserves the stomach when oncologically appropriate;
- provides a substantial specimen for pathologic assessment;
- can establish whether the tumor remains within a curative endoscopic pattern;
- can identify patients who may need additional treatment.

But ER is only appropriate when the cancer's biological and pathologic risk is compatible with local treatment.

The package therefore emphasizes **selection + complete resection + pathology + follow-up**, rather than the procedure alone.

# Key Concepts

## Concept 1 — Early does not automatically mean endoscopically treatable

“Early gastric cancer” is a staging concept, not a guarantee that ER is appropriate.

## Concept 2 — Depth matters

Deeper invasion can increase concern for lymphatic and nodal spread.

## Concept 3 — The lesion is more than its size

Size, histology, ulceration, LVI, margins, and depth must be considered together.

## Concept 4 — ER can be treatment and staging

The resection specimen can provide information that a small biopsy cannot fully provide.

## Concept 5 — ESD and EMR are different

ESD is currently preferred in the NCCN ER framework, but it is technically more demanding.

## Concept 6 — Pathology determines curative status

The procedure itself does not establish that the cancer has been completely treated.

## Concept 7 — Non-curative does not mean “failed”

It means additional oncologic management may need to be considered.

## Concept 8 — Follow-up remains necessary

Curative ER is followed by surveillance because recurrence or metachronous lesions can still occur.

# Common Misconceptions

### Myth 1

**“If the cancer is early, endoscopic resection is always appropriate.”**

**Fact:**  
Early stage is important but not sufficient. Depth, histology, size, ulceration, lymphovascular invasion, margins, and other findings influence suitability.

### Myth 2

**“Endoscopic resection means the cancer was not serious.”**

**Fact:**  
ER is a cancer treatment used for selected early gastric cancers. It is an oncologic treatment, not simply a biopsy.

### Myth 3

**“EMR and ESD are the same procedure.”**

**Fact:**  
They are different endoscopic techniques. ESD generally provides greater en-bloc resection capability but requires greater technical expertise.

### Myth 4

**“ESD is always better than EMR.”**

**Fact:**  
NCCN prefers ESD within the ER framework, but the appropriate approach depends on lesion characteristics, expertise, and clinical context.

### Myth 5

**“Once the lesion is removed, treatment is finished.”**

**Fact:**  
The resection specimen must be evaluated to determine whether the treatment was curative.

### Myth 6

**“Negative margins automatically mean the resection was curative.”**

**Fact:**  
Curative assessment also considers depth, differentiation, lymphovascular invasion, and other relevant pathologic features.

### Myth 7

**“A biopsy showing early cancer means I definitely qualify for ER.”**

**Fact:**  
Pre-treatment biopsy is only part of the assessment. Final eligibility and curative status require integration of additional tumor and pathology information.

### Myth 8

**“A non-curative ER means the procedure was useless.”**

**Fact:**  
The procedure may provide essential pathologic information showing that additional treatment is needed.

### Myth 9

**“Endoscopic treatment means I never need surgery.”**

**Fact:**  
Some patients need additional surgery if final pathology shows that endoscopic treatment alone is not oncologically adequate.

### Myth 10

**“After a curative ER, I do not need follow-up.”**

**Fact:**  
Structured endoscopic surveillance remains part of care after ER.

### Myth 11

**“Any endoscopy center can perform therapeutic ER.”**

**Fact:**  
Guideline and patient-facing sources emphasize experienced endoscopists and appropriate centers.

# Key Messages

1. **Endoscopic resection is a real cancer treatment for selected early gastric cancers.**
2. **The decision is based on more than lesion size.**
3. **Depth of invasion and lymph-node risk are central to the decision.**
4. **Histology, ulceration, lymphovascular invasion, and margins also matter.**
5. **ER includes EMR and ESD; ESD is preferred in the current NCCN framework.**
6. **The resection specimen is essential for determining whether treatment was curative.**
7. **A non-curative result does not mean the procedure was useless; it can identify the need for additional management.**
8. **ER can preserve the stomach when it is oncologically appropriate.**
9. **Expertise and center experience matter.**
10. **Follow-up remains necessary after ER.**

# Patient Questions to Ask the Care Team

1. Is my cancer considered early gastric cancer?
2. How deeply has the tumor invaded?
3. What features make my lesion suitable or unsuitable for endoscopic resection?
4. Is EMR or ESD being considered, and why?
5. How much experience does the endoscopist or center have with this procedure?
6. What will the pathology team need to determine after the lesion is removed?
7. What would make the resection curative?
8. What findings would mean that additional treatment is needed?
9. If surgery is recommended after ER, what finding led to that recommendation?
10. What follow-up endoscopy will I need?
11. What symptoms after the procedure should prompt me to contact the care team?
12. Will my case be reviewed by a multidisciplinary team if the pathology is not straightforward?
