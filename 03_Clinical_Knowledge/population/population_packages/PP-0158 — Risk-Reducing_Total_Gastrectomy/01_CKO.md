## Educational Objective

Explain what risk-reducing total gastrectomy (RRTG) means in hereditary diffuse gastric cancer (HDGC), why it is considered particularly for people with CDH1 pathogenic variants, why surgery may be considered even without symptoms, how timing is individualized, how RRTG compares conceptually with continued endoscopic surveillance, and what patients should understand about the immediate and lifelong consequences of surgery.

## Clinical Knowledge Blocks

### KB1 — What Is Risk-Reducing Total Gastrectomy?

RRTG is removal of the stomach to substantially reduce gastric-cancer risk in people with hereditary gastric-cancer susceptibility. In HDGC, RRTG with esophagojejunostomy is described by NCI as the treatment of choice for individuals with CDH1 pathogenic variants.

### KB2 — Why Consider Surgery When a Person Has No Symptoms?

HDGC may contain multifocal, microscopic SRCC lesions that are not reliably visible during endoscopy. Therefore, absence of symptoms or a normal-appearing stomach does not reliably exclude early disease in a CDH1 carrier.

### KB3 — Who Is the Main Population?

The core population is people with a confirmed CDH1 pathogenic variant in the HDGC context who are considering gastric-cancer risk reduction.

RRTG should not automatically be extrapolated to HDGC-like families with negative CDH1/CTNNA1 testing, where gastric-cancer risk is more uncertain.

### KB4 — Why Is Microscopic Disease Important?

NCI summarizes RRTG series in which occult gastric cancer and microscopic SRCC were frequently identified. A systematic review of 224 RRTGs found gastric cancer in 85.4% of specimens, with at least one SRCC focus in 72.6% and invasive tumor in 12.8%.

A retrospective review of 101 RRTG specimens from asymptomatic CDH1 carriers identified early-stage pT1a SRCC in 85%.

These figures demonstrate the rationale for risk-reducing surgery; they are not an individual's personal predicted probability.

### KB5 — When Is RRTG Considered?

Timing is individualized. NCI notes that younger individuals (<18 years) and older individuals (>70 years) require a more individualized approach because of surgical morbidity. The decision should consider age, clinical context, surgical fitness, preferences, psychosocial readiness, and the feasibility and acceptability of alternatives.

### KB6 — RRTG Versus Endoscopic Surveillance

RRTG is the gold-standard risk-reduction approach in HDGC, while endoscopic surveillance is an alternative for selected people who decline or defer surgery.

These options should not be presented as identical: RRTG removes high-risk gastric tissue, whereas surveillance retains the stomach and depends on detecting lesions that may be microscopic and difficult to identify.

Detailed surveillance belongs to PP-0159.

### KB7 — What Does the Operation Involve?

At a conceptual level, RRTG removes the stomach and restores gastrointestinal continuity, typically through esophagojejunostomy.

The operation may be performed through minimally invasive or open approaches depending on clinical and surgical circumstances.

Detailed operative technique is outside this PP.

### KB8 — What Are the Major Burdens and Risks?

Important consequences include:

- substantial weight loss;
- nutritional deficiencies;
- anastomotic leak;
- intra-abdominal infection;
- diarrhea;
- reflux and other gastrointestinal symptoms;
- perioperative mortality.

NCI reports that weight loss is universal and cites one series with average postoperative weight loss of 15%–20%; cited mortality estimates range from 3%–6%.

### KB9 — What Changes After Surgery?

The patient must adapt to life without a stomach. Most patients eventually establish a new functional routine, but nutritional status requires close monitoring.

### KB10 — Quality of Life

Long-term quality of life can be good after RRTG. NCI cites a series of 54 patients in which median quality of life was very good after a median follow-up of 4.6 years.

This should be balanced against the irreversible nature of surgery and chronic nutritional/gastrointestinal consequences.

### KB11 — Lifelong Follow-up

Post-RRTG follow-up remains necessary. NCI describes multidisciplinary follow-up addressing diet, weight, symptoms, general health, nutrition, bone health, cancer risk, and consideration of vitamin/mineral supplementation.

### KB12 — What If Surgery Is Deferred or Declined?

Deferring or declining RRTG does not mean abandoning risk management. Selected CDH1 carriers may undergo endoscopic surveillance, with the surveillance pathway owned by PP-0159.

### KB13 — Why Multidisciplinary Decision-Making Matters

NCI favors multidisciplinary discussion because the decision balances:

- high hereditary gastric-cancer risk;
- limitations of endoscopic detection;
- uncertainty about progression of microscopic lesions;
- surgical morbidity;
- patient preferences and readiness.

### KB14 — What Does RRTG Not Mean?

RRTG is not a guarantee that every future cancer risk is eliminated. It specifically reduces gastric-cancer risk by removing the stomach. Other hereditary risks, including breast-cancer risk associated with CDH1, require separate management.

## Patient Explanation

> If you carry a CDH1 pathogenic variant, your doctors may discuss removing the stomach before gastric cancer develops. This is called risk-reducing total gastrectomy. The reason surgery can be considered even when you feel well is that early HDGC can exist as tiny areas of signet-ring cell cancer that may not be visible during routine endoscopy.
>
> Surgery is a major and irreversible decision. It can greatly reduce gastric-cancer risk, but it also changes how you eat and digest food and requires long-term nutritional and medical follow-up. Some people may defer surgery and undergo specialist endoscopic surveillance instead. The choice should be discussed with an experienced multidisciplinary team.

## Clinical Importance

The package addresses a high-stakes preventive decision in which:

- the disease can be microscopic and difficult to detect;
- the preventive intervention is irreversible;
- surgery has substantial morbidity;
- surveillance is possible for selected patients but has limitations.

Therefore balanced decision-support is essential.

## Common Misconceptions

### Myth 1
A normal endoscopy means a CDH1 carrier has no early gastric disease.

**Fact:** Microscopic SRCC can be missed by endoscopy and biopsy.

### Myth 2
RRTG is only considered after symptoms develop.

**Fact:** It is a risk-reducing intervention and may be considered before symptoms or clinically apparent cancer.

### Myth 3
RRTG means no further medical follow-up is needed.

**Fact:** Lifelong multidisciplinary follow-up remains necessary.

### Myth 4
A CDH1 pathogenic variant automatically means surgery must occur immediately.

**Fact:** Timing and management require individualized multidisciplinary discussion.

### Myth 5
RRTG and endoscopic surveillance are equivalent.

**Fact:** They address risk differently: surgery removes the high-risk tissue; surveillance attempts to detect disease while the stomach remains.

## Key Messages

- RRTG is the principal risk-reducing surgical strategy for CDH1-associated HDGC.
- Microscopic SRCC can be present even without symptoms or obvious endoscopic abnormalities.
- Normal endoscopy does not reliably exclude microscopic HDGC.
- Timing of surgery is individualized.
- RRTG is irreversible and has substantial short- and long-term consequences.
- Weight loss and nutritional monitoring are expected parts of post-RRTG care.
- Quality of life can remain good after adaptation, but lifelong follow-up is needed.
- Selected patients who defer or decline surgery may undergo endoscopic surveillance.
- Detailed surveillance, genetic counseling, testing criteria, and gene interpretation belong to adjacent packages.
