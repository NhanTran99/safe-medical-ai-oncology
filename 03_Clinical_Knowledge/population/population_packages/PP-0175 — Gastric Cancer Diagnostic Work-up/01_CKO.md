## Educational Objectives

After reading this Population Package, the reader should be able to:

1. Explain what a gastric-cancer diagnostic work-up is.
2. Understand the difference between clinical suspicion, diagnosis and staging.
3. Understand why upper endoscopy is central to the initial diagnostic pathway.
4. Understand why biopsy and pathology are needed for tissue confirmation.
5. Understand why imaging is needed to assess disease extent.
6. Explain the conceptual roles of CT, PET/CT and EUS.
7. Understand why staging laparoscopy is used selectively.
8. Understand why early gastric cancer can require specialized staging assessment.
9. Recognize the role of pathology and biomarker testing as downstream characterization.
10. Understand why several tests may be needed after cancer is suspected or confirmed.
11. Recognize that the work-up is not a rigid universal checklist.
12. Understand what the diagnostic work-up does and does not decide.

# Clinical Knowledge Blocks

## Knowledge Block 1 — What Is a Gastric Cancer Diagnostic Work-up?

### Patient Explanation

A gastric cancer diagnostic work-up is the structured process used after gastric cancer becomes suspected or a concerning finding is identified. It brings together clinical assessment, endoscopy, tissue examination and selected imaging or other tests to answer different clinical questions.

### Clinical Importance

The work-up is broader than a single test. Its purpose is to move from suspicion toward a confirmed diagnosis and then toward an understanding of disease extent and characteristics.

### Key Concepts

- clinical suspicion
- diagnostic confirmation
- disease characterization
- initial staging

## Knowledge Block 2 — Why Is the Work-up More Than One Test?

### Patient Explanation

Different tests answer different questions. Endoscopy can show where an abnormality is in the stomach, biopsy can provide tissue for confirmation, and imaging can show whether disease has spread beyond the primary lesion.

### Clinical Importance

A patient may therefore need several tests without this automatically meaning that the cancer is more advanced. The tests are complementary rather than interchangeable.

### Key Concepts

- complementary tests
- different clinical questions
- integrated evaluation

## Knowledge Block 3 — What Starts a Diagnostic Work-up?

### Patient Explanation

The work-up can begin when symptoms, examination findings, an abnormal screening result, or another clinical finding raises concern for gastric cancer.

### Clinical Importance

The starting point is suspicion, not confirmation. PP-0175 begins at this transition and does not replace the separate symptom or screening packages.

### Key Concepts

- symptoms
- abnormal screening
- clinical suspicion

## Knowledge Block 4 — What Happens During the Initial Clinical Assessment?

### Patient Explanation

The clinician reviews symptoms, medical history, relevant risk factors and physical findings. Blood tests may also be used to look for anemia or to assess general organ function.

### Clinical Importance

This first assessment helps determine how strongly gastric cancer is suspected and what additional evaluation is appropriate.

### Key Concepts

- history
- physical examination
- risk context
- laboratory assessment

## Knowledge Block 5 — Why Is Upper Endoscopy Central to Diagnosis?

### Patient Explanation

Upper endoscopy, also called esophagogastroduodenoscopy or EGD, lets the clinician directly examine the lining of the esophagus, stomach and first part of the small intestine. If an abnormal area is seen, tissue can be sampled.

### Clinical Importance

The supplied NCCN and ACS sources identify diagnostic endoscopy as a central component of the gastric-cancer work-up because it can identify and locate suspicious gastric neoplasia.

### Key Concepts

- EGD
- direct visualization
- lesion location
- tissue sampling

## Knowledge Block 6 — Can Endoscopy Alone Confirm Gastric Cancer?

### Patient Explanation

No. An abnormal-looking area can strongly suggest cancer, but visual suspicion is not the same as tissue confirmation. A biopsy provides tissue that can be examined by pathology.

### Clinical Importance

This distinction protects against treating a suspicious endoscopic appearance as a confirmed diagnosis.

### Key Concepts

- suspicion versus confirmation
- biopsy
- pathology

## Knowledge Block 7 — Why Is Biopsy Important?

### Patient Explanation

A biopsy removes a small amount of tissue from a suspicious area so that it can be examined in the laboratory. The supplied ACS source states that biopsy is needed to be sure that an abnormal area contains cancer.

### Clinical Importance

Biopsy is therefore the bridge between seeing a suspicious lesion and establishing a tissue-based diagnosis.

### Key Concepts

- tissue confirmation
- histopathology
- diagnostic certainty

## Knowledge Block 8 — What Does Pathology Contribute to the Work-up?

### Patient Explanation

Pathology determines what the sampled tissue represents and provides information needed for subsequent classification and clinical planning.

### Clinical Importance

The Vietnam Ministry of Health guideline identifies histopathology as the gold standard for definitive diagnosis. Detailed histopathologic classification is delegated to PP-0178 and later pathology packages.

### Key Concepts

- definitive diagnosis
- histopathology
- downstream classification

## Knowledge Block 9 — Why Is Location Important?

### Patient Explanation

The work-up records where the tumor is located in the stomach. For tumors near the gastroesophageal junction, the clinical team may also assess the relevant anatomical/Siewert category.

### Clinical Importance

Location affects how the disease is characterized and can influence the subsequent staging and management pathway.

### Key Concepts

- tumor location
- gastroesophageal junction
- Siewert context

## Knowledge Block 10 — Why Are Imaging Tests Needed After or Alongside Endoscopy?

### Patient Explanation

Imaging looks beyond the inner surface of the stomach. It can help show the size and extent of disease, nearby lymph nodes, and possible spread to other organs.

### Clinical Importance

Imaging complements endoscopy and pathology because a tissue sample alone does not show the full distribution of disease in the body.

### Key Concepts

- disease extent
- cross-sectional imaging
- complementary evidence

## Knowledge Block 11 — What Is the Role of CT?

### Patient Explanation

CT uses X-rays to create detailed cross-sectional images. The supplied ACS source states that CT can show the stomach, nearby lymph nodes and organs such as the liver, helping clinicians assess the extent or stage of gastric cancer.

### Clinical Importance

NCCN includes CT of the chest, abdomen and pelvis as a core work-up/staging component in the relevant pathway.

### Key Concepts

- CT chest/abdomen/pelvis
- extent
- staging

## Knowledge Block 12 — What Is the Role of FDG-PET/CT?

### Patient Explanation

FDG-PET/CT can provide additional information about possible disease elsewhere in the body. It is not required in exactly the same way for every patient.

### Clinical Importance

NCCN describes PET/CT as a selected test, particularly in locally advanced or metastatic disease or when clinically indicated. Some gastric cancers may show limited tracer uptake, so PET/CT is not a universal replacement for other imaging.

### Key Concepts

- selected use
- distant disease
- limitations

## Knowledge Block 13 — What Is the Role of EUS?

### Patient Explanation

Endoscopic ultrasound combines endoscopy with ultrasound to examine the layers of the stomach wall and nearby lymph nodes or structures.

### Clinical Importance

EUS can be particularly useful when the depth of local disease needs clarification, including selected patients with suspected early-stage disease. Its detailed interpretation belongs downstream.

### Key Concepts

- local extent
- depth of invasion
- regional nodes

## Knowledge Block 14 — When Might Diagnostic Laparoscopy Be Used?

### Patient Explanation

In selected patients, a surgeon may look directly inside the abdomen and obtain peritoneal washings or tissue to look for disease that is not adequately characterized by routine imaging.

### Clinical Importance

NCCN and ACS describe staging laparoscopy as a selective component of the work-up rather than a test required for everyone.

### Key Concepts

- selected staging
- peritoneal disease
- cytology

## Knowledge Block 15 — Why Is Early Gastric Cancer a Special Situation?

### Patient Explanation

Early gastric cancers can require particularly careful assessment of how deeply the tumor has entered the stomach wall. In selected cases, endoscopic resection can provide additional information for accurate staging.

### Clinical Importance

NCCN identifies endoscopic resection as important for accurate staging of selected early-stage cancers. The procedure itself is outside PP-0175.

### Key Concepts

- early gastric cancer
- local staging
- endoscopic resection

## Knowledge Block 16 — What Is the Role of Biomarker and Molecular Testing?

### Patient Explanation

Once gastric cancer is established or strongly characterized, additional laboratory testing may be performed to identify molecular features that are relevant to classification or later clinical decision-making.

### Clinical Importance

NCCN integrates biomarker testing into the gastric-cancer work-up, but the individual biomarkers and their interpretation are separate Population Packages.

### Key Concepts

- biomarker interface
- molecular characterization
- downstream precision oncology

## Knowledge Block 17 — Are Blood Tests Part of the Work-up?

### Patient Explanation

Blood tests can assess blood counts, liver and kidney function and other general measures. They may also identify anemia or help determine whether a person is medically ready for subsequent procedures or treatment.

### Clinical Importance

These tests support the broader clinical assessment but do not by themselves confirm gastric cancer.

### Key Concepts

- CBC
- organ function
- anemia
- readiness

## Knowledge Block 18 — Why Might Family History, H. pylori, Nutrition and Distress Be Assessed?

### Patient Explanation

The broader initial evaluation may include family history, H. pylori status, nutritional assessment and other health or psychosocial considerations.

### Clinical Importance

These factors can affect risk interpretation and readiness for care, but they are supporting components rather than the central diagnostic question of PP-0175.

### Key Concepts

- family history
- H. pylori
- nutrition
- distress

## Knowledge Block 19 — What Is the Difference Between Diagnosis and Staging?

### Patient Explanation

Diagnosis asks what the abnormality is and whether it is cancer. Staging asks how extensive the cancer is and whether it has spread.

### Clinical Importance

The two processes are closely connected but are not the same. This distinction should remain explicit throughout the package.

### Key Concepts

- diagnosis
- staging
- extent

## Knowledge Block 20 — Does a Negative Test Always End the Work-up?

### Patient Explanation

Not necessarily. A test can be limited or nondiagnostic, and some gastric cancers can be difficult to see with endoscopy. If clinical concern remains, additional evaluation may be needed.

### Clinical Importance

A negative or incomplete result should be interpreted in context rather than automatically treated as proof that cancer is absent.

### Key Concepts

- diagnostic uncertainty
- false-negative possibility
- context

## Knowledge Block 21 — Does Every Patient Need Every Test?

### Patient Explanation

No. The work-up is adapted to the clinical situation. CT is a major staging tool, while PET/CT, EUS and staging laparoscopy are used selectively according to the questions that remain unanswered.

### Clinical Importance

This prevents the work-up from being misunderstood as a rigid checklist that applies identically to every patient.

### Key Concepts

- individualized work-up
- selective testing
- clinical context

## Knowledge Block 22 — Why Can More Tests Be Needed After Cancer Is Confirmed?

### Patient Explanation

Confirmation tells clinicians what the disease is, but additional tests may still be needed to determine its location, extent, local invasion, distant spread and relevant molecular features.

### Clinical Importance

More tests after diagnosis are therefore not automatically evidence that the initial diagnosis was uncertain; they may be needed to characterize the cancer sufficiently for staging and planning.

### Key Concepts

- post-confirmation characterization
- staging
- treatment readiness

## Knowledge Block 23 — When Is the Diagnostic Work-up Considered Sufficient?

### Patient Explanation

The work-up is sufficiently informative when the clinical team has the information needed to establish the diagnosis and understand the disease's relevant extent and characteristics for the next clinical decision.

### Clinical Importance

There is no single universal checklist that is identical for every patient. The required information depends on the clinical presentation and disease context.

### Key Concepts

- decision readiness
- adequate characterization
- context-dependent completion

## Knowledge Block 24 — What Happens After the Work-up?

### Patient Explanation

The results are brought together to establish the clinical diagnosis and stage and to prepare for the next phase of care.

### Clinical Importance

PP-0175 ends at this transition. It does not decide which surgery, medicine, radiation treatment or targeted/immunotherapy should be given.

### Key Concepts

- clinical synthesis
- stage
- treatment-planning transition

# Common Misconceptions

### Myth 1

**“A suspicious endoscopy means cancer is already confirmed.”**

**Fact:** False. Suspicious appearance prompts tissue evaluation; pathology provides the basis for definitive diagnosis.

### Myth 2

**“A biopsy is unnecessary if the CT looks like cancer.”**

**Fact:** False. Imaging can show a suspicious lesion or spread, but it does not replace tissue confirmation when tissue diagnosis is required.

### Myth 3

**“CT is the test that diagnoses gastric cancer.”**

**Fact:** False. CT is primarily used to assess disease extent/staging and complements endoscopy and pathology.

### Myth 4

**“Everyone with gastric cancer needs PET/CT.”**

**Fact:** False. PET/CT is used selectively according to disease context and clinical indication.

### Myth 5

**“Everyone with gastric cancer needs EUS.”**

**Fact:** False. EUS is particularly useful for selected local-staging questions, including suspected early-stage disease.

### Myth 6

**“If the biopsy confirms cancer, no more tests are needed.”**

**Fact:** False. Additional tests may be needed to determine stage, spread and clinically relevant tumor characteristics.

### Myth 7

**“More tests after diagnosis mean the doctors are unsure whether I have cancer.”**

**Fact:** Not necessarily. Different tests answer different questions after the diagnosis is established.

### Myth 8

**“A normal endoscopy always rules out gastric cancer.”**

**Fact:** False. Some gastric cancers can be difficult to see endoscopically.

### Myth 9

**“Every patient follows exactly the same diagnostic sequence.”**

**Fact:** False. The work-up is adapted to the clinical situation.

### Myth 10

**“Biomarker testing is what proves that the tumor is cancer.”**

**Fact:** False. Biomarker testing adds molecular information; it is not a substitute for establishing the diagnosis.

### Myth 11

**“Staging and diagnosis mean the same thing.”**

**Fact:** False. Diagnosis establishes what the disease is; staging describes how extensive it is.

### Myth 12

**“A staging laparoscopy is routine for every patient.”**

**Fact:** False. It is a selected staging procedure used when clinically appropriate.

### Myth 13

**“If CT shows no spread, microscopic peritoneal disease is impossible.”**

**Fact:** False. Selected patients may undergo laparoscopy/cytology because imaging may not answer every staging question.

### Myth 14

**“All diagnostic tests should be completed before any pathology is reviewed.”**

**Fact:** False. The work-up is integrated; different components can be performed according to the clinical situation.

### Myth 15

**“A negative test always means the work-up is finished.”**

**Fact:** False. If a result is nondiagnostic or clinical suspicion remains, further evaluation may be appropriate.

# Key Messages

1. Gastric-cancer diagnosis is a process, not a single test.
2. Clinical suspicion is different from confirmed diagnosis.
3. Upper endoscopy is central because it can directly identify and localize suspicious gastric lesions.
4. Biopsy and pathology provide tissue-based confirmation.
5. CT and other imaging complement pathology by showing disease extent.
6. EUS, PET/CT and staging laparoscopy are selected according to the clinical question.
7. Early gastric cancer may require specialized endoscopic/pathologic assessment for accurate local staging.
8. Biomarker testing adds molecular information and does not replace diagnostic confirmation.
9. Not every patient needs every test.
10. The purpose of the work-up is to establish diagnosis and clinically relevant extent, then provide information needed for the next stage of care.
11. PP-0175 does not prescribe individualized treatment.
