# Educational Objective

Help the reader understand:

> **How is hereditary gastric-cancer risk assessed from a person's personal and family history, and how does that assessment guide genetic evaluation and risk management?**

This package is the **clinical assessment layer** between a patient's history and syndrome-specific testing or management.

# Knowledge Block 1 — What Is Hereditary Gastric Cancer Risk Assessment?

Hereditary gastric-cancer risk assessment is the structured process of determining whether a person's personal and family history suggests an inherited susceptibility to gastric cancer or another hereditary cancer syndrome.

It is broader than genetic testing.

The sequence is:

**History**

→ **pedigree**

→ **pathology/histology**

→ **pattern recognition**

→ **hereditary-risk assessment**

→ **genetic counseling/testing when appropriate**

→ **syndrome-specific management**

---

# Knowledge Block 2 — What Information Is Needed?

Important information includes:

- current age;
- personal cancer history;
- age at cancer diagnosis;
- tumor type and histology;
- previous biopsies and pathology;
- gastric polyps or precursor lesions;
- prior genetic testing;
- prior tumor testing;
- family cancer history;
- cancer types in relatives;
- age at diagnosis in relatives;
- degree of relationship;
- maternal and paternal lineage;
- relevant ancestry.

The quality of the assessment depends on the quality of the information available.

---

# Knowledge Block 3 — Why Build a Three-Generation Family History?

A three-generation pedigree helps identify:

- clustering of cancers;
- patterns across generations;
- maternal versus paternal transmission;
- affected first- and second-degree relatives;
- possible hereditary syndromes.

The family history should not be limited to one side of the family.

Hereditary cancer susceptibility can be inherited through either the maternal or paternal lineage.

---

# Knowledge Block 4 — Why Does Histology Matter?

“Stomach cancer” is not a sufficiently precise hereditary-risk descriptor by itself.

Important details may include:

- diffuse gastric cancer;
- signet-ring cell carcinoma;
- poorly differentiated gastric cancer;
- intestinal-type gastric cancer;
- gastric polyposis patterns.

Histology can change the hereditary differential diagnosis and determine whether a syndrome-specific pathway should be considered.

---

# Knowledge Block 5 — What Are Hereditary-Cancer Red Flags?

Features that can increase suspicion include:

- unusually young cancer diagnosis;
- multiple primary cancers;
- bilateral cancers;
- several close relatives with cancer;
- cancers occurring across multiple generations;
- unusual tumor types or histology;
- characteristic combinations of cancers;
- relevant ancestry/founder populations;
- congenital anomalies or precursor lesions.

The overall pattern is more informative than any single family-history item.

---

# Knowledge Block 6 — Why Verify Family Cancer History?

Family-history reports can be incomplete or inaccurate.

When possible, important diagnoses should be verified using:

- pathology reports;
- medical records;
- cancer registries;
- death records when appropriate.

Verification is especially important when the distinction between gastric-cancer histologies affects hereditary-risk assessment.

---

# Knowledge Block 7 — Can Family History Be Negative Even When Hereditary Risk Exists?

Yes.

An apparently negative family history may reflect:

- small family size;
- missing information;
- adoption;
- early deaths;
- limited knowledge of relatives;
- variable penetrance;
- genetic heterogeneity;
- de novo variants;
- other limitations in pedigree information.

Therefore:

> **A negative or incomplete family history does not always exclude hereditary susceptibility.**

---

# Knowledge Block 8 — Are All Familial Gastric Cancers the Same?

No.

Different personal/family-history patterns can suggest different hereditary syndromes.

Examples include:

- diffuse/signet-ring gastric-cancer patterns → HDGC/CDH1/CTNNA1 pathway;
- gastric polyposis patterns → APC, STK11, SMAD4 and other hereditary pathways;
- gastric cancer with colorectal/endometrial clustering → Lynch-syndrome differential;
- other characteristic cancer combinations → other hereditary cancer syndromes.

These are high-level recognition cues only.

Gene-specific packages own detailed interpretation.

---

# Knowledge Block 9 — What Exactly Is Being Estimated?

Two different questions should be separated:

### Question A
**How likely is it that this person/family carries a pathogenic germline variant?**

### Question B
**How likely is the person to develop cancer?**

These are related but not identical.

A person can have an elevated hereditary-susceptibility probability without a precise individualized cancer-risk estimate being available.

---

# Knowledge Block 10 — How Is Risk Estimated?

Risk assessment may combine:

- personal history;
- family history;
- pedigree structure;
- age at diagnosis;
- tumor characteristics;
- known pathogenic variants;
- ancestry;
- empirical cancer-risk data;
- penetrance information;
- statistical or Bayesian models when appropriate;
- clinical judgment.

Risk models can help refine estimates, but they have limitations and should not replace appropriate clinical judgment.

---

# Knowledge Block 11 — Does Risk Assessment Automatically Mean Genetic Testing?

No.

The conceptual pathway is:

**Hereditary red flags**

↓

**risk assessment**

↓

**genetic counseling/testing when appropriate**

The decision to test depends on the clinical context, the suspected syndrome, whether the result is likely to be interpretable, and whether it can affect management or clarify family risk.

Exact syndrome-specific testing criteria belong to the relevant downstream PP.

---

# Knowledge Block 12 — Why Might Tumor Testing Matter?

Prior tumor genomic testing can sometimes provide clues that raise suspicion for a germline susceptibility.

However:

> **a somatic tumor finding is not automatically a germline diagnosis.**

Tumor findings should be interpreted in the appropriate molecular-testing and germline-testing context.

Detailed somatic and germline interpretation belongs to the relevant molecular/genetic packages.

---

# Knowledge Block 13 — What Happens After Risk Assessment?

If the assessment suggests hereditary susceptibility, the next step may be:

- genetic counseling;
- syndrome-specific genetic-testing criteria;
- germline testing;
- syndrome-specific risk management.

The correct downstream pathway depends on the pattern identified.

For example:

**suspected HDGC pattern**
→ PP-0152

**CDH1 result**
→ PP-0150

**CTNNA1 result**
→ PP-0151

**HDGC-like post-testing state**
→ PP-0153

# Common Misconceptions

### Myth 1
**A family history of gastric cancer automatically means a hereditary syndrome.**

**Fact:** Family clustering increases suspicion but does not by itself establish a hereditary syndrome.

### Myth 2
**Only the mother's side of the family matters.**

**Fact:** Hereditary susceptibility can come through either maternal or paternal lineage.

### Myth 3
**“Stomach cancer” is enough information for genetic risk assessment.**

**Fact:** Age, histology, cancer type and relationship are important.

### Myth 4
**A negative family history means hereditary risk is impossible.**

**Fact:** Family histories can be incomplete or misleading.

### Myth 5
**High hereditary risk means a pathogenic variant has been found.**

**Fact:** Risk assessment estimates suspicion; it does not itself establish a molecular diagnosis.

### Myth 6
**Genetic risk and cancer risk are the same thing.**

**Fact:** Probability of carrying a pathogenic variant and probability of developing cancer are distinct questions.

### Myth 7
**Every person with a strong family history should automatically receive the same genetic test.**

**Fact:** Testing should follow an appropriate clinical assessment and suspected hereditary syndrome.

---

# Key Messages

- Hereditary gastric-cancer risk assessment is broader than genetic testing.
- Personal history and a detailed three-generation family history are central.
- Both maternal and paternal sides should be assessed.
- Age at diagnosis, cancer type and histology matter.
- Important family cancer diagnoses should be verified whenever possible.
- Different cancer patterns can point toward different hereditary syndromes.
- Incomplete family history does not necessarily exclude hereditary susceptibility.
- Probability of a pathogenic variant and probability of developing cancer are different questions.
- Risk models can support assessment but do not replace clinical judgment.
- Risk assessment may lead to genetic counseling/testing when appropriate.
- Syndrome-specific criteria, gene interpretation and management should be handled by downstream packages.
