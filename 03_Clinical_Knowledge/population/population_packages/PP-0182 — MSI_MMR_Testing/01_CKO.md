# 3. Clinical Knowledge Blocks

## CKB-01 — What Is Mismatch Repair?

Mismatch repair (MMR) is a DNA-repair function that helps correct certain errors that can occur when DNA is copied.

Several proteins participate in this repair system. In clinical gastric-cancer testing, the four commonly assessed MMR proteins are:

- MLH1;
- MSH2;
- MSH6;
- PMS2.

The patient-facing concept is simple:

> MMR is one of the systems cells use to repair DNA-copying errors.

Detailed molecular mechanisms belong to the dedicated DNA Mismatch Repair package.

---

## CKB-02 — What Happens When MMR Function Is Lost?

When mismatch-repair function is deficient, DNA replication errors can accumulate.

Microsatellites are repetitive DNA regions that can be particularly susceptible to these errors.

This can produce a recognizable pattern called:

> **microsatellite instability (MSI).**

Therefore the conceptual pathway is:

**MMR dysfunction**

→ accumulation of replication errors

→ instability at microsatellite regions

→ **MSI**

This is a conceptual relationship, not a statement that every tumor result must be assessed by the same assay.

---

## CKB-03 — What Is Microsatellite Instability?

Microsatellites are short repeated sequences within DNA.

**Microsatellite instability** means that these repetitive regions show evidence of changes consistent with replication errors.

MSI is therefore a molecular feature of the tumor.

It is not:

- a symptom;
- a stage;
- a histologic subtype;
- a treatment;
- a diagnosis of hereditary cancer by itself.

---

## CKB-04 — What Does MSI-H Mean?

**MSI-H** means:

> **Microsatellite Instability-High.**

It indicates a high level of microsatellite instability according to the applicable testing criteria.

NCCN defines MSI-H as:

- at least 30% of evaluated markers showing instability; or
- at least 2 of the 5 NCI/mononucleotide markers showing instability in the specified framework.

The exact technical assay and marker set are laboratory-level details.

Patient-facing interpretation:

> MSI-H means the tumor shows a high-instability pattern that is clinically meaningful in gastric cancer.

---

## CKB-05 — What Does MSI-L Mean?

**MSI-L** means:

> **Microsatellite Instability-Low.**

NCCN defines MSI-L in its marker framework as:

- 1%–29% of markers showing instability; or
- 1 of the 5 specified NCI/mononucleotide markers showing instability.

MSI-L is a valid category of MSI interpretation, but it is not the central high-instability category used for the major MSI-H/dMMR clinical decisions.

---

## CKB-06 — What Does MSS Mean?

**MSS** means:

> **Microsatellite Stable.**

It indicates that the tumor does not meet the defined criteria for MSI-L or MSI-H in the applicable assay.

MSS does not mean:

- no cancer is present;
- the tumor has no molecular abnormalities;
- no other biomarker is relevant;
- no treatment option exists.

It describes microsatellite status only.

---

## CKB-07 — What Is dMMR?

**dMMR** means:

> **Mismatch Repair Deficient.**

In gastric-cancer pathology, MMR deficiency can be evaluated by IHC.

Loss of nuclear expression of one or more of the relevant MMR proteins supports a dMMR interpretation.

The four proteins assessed are:

- MLH1;
- MSH2;
- MSH6;
- PMS2.

---

## CKB-08 — What Does MMR IHC Test?

MMR IHC uses tissue staining to assess whether the expected nuclear expression of MMR proteins is present.

The clinically relevant proteins are:

| Protein | Role in PP-0182 |
|---|---|
| MLH1 | MMR protein assessed by IHC |
| MSH2 | MMR protein assessed by IHC |
| MSH6 | MMR protein assessed by IHC |
| PMS2 | MMR protein assessed by IHC |

PP-0182 explains the meaning of these results but does not teach detailed staining methodology.

---

## CKB-09 — What Does Loss of MMR Protein Expression Mean?

NCCN describes:

> Loss of nuclear expression of one or more MMR proteins → dMMR.

This means the tested tumor shows a pattern consistent with MMR deficiency.

The result may have clinical implications beyond the tumor itself, including consideration of genetic counseling in the appropriate context.

---

## CKB-10 — What Does No Loss of MMR Protein Expression Mean?

NCCN describes:

> No loss of nuclear expression of MMR proteins → no evidence of dMMR and low probability of MSI-H.

This is a statement about the tested tumor and assay.

It does not mean:

- the tumor has no mutations;
- the tumor has no other biomarkers;
- MSI/MMR is the only relevant molecular test.

---

## CKB-11 — Are MSI and MMR the Same Test?

No.

They are closely related but use different testing approaches.

### MSI testing

Looks for evidence of instability in microsatellite regions.

It can be assessed by:

- PCR;
- appropriate NGS-based methods.

### MMR testing

Looks at the expression of MMR proteins using IHC.

NCCN specifically notes that MSI by PCR/NGS and MMR by IHC measure different biological effects caused by dMMR function.

### Key message

> MSI and MMR testing ask closely related questions from different biological perspectives.

---

## CKB-12 — Why Are Both MSI and MMR Terms Used?

Clinical reports and guidelines may refer to:

- MSI-H;
- dMMR;
- MSS;
- MSI-L;
- MMR-proficient;
- no evidence of dMMR.

These terms describe related but not identical testing results.

The broader clinical concept is:

> Is the tumor showing evidence of the molecular phenotype associated with mismatch-repair deficiency?

---

## CKB-13 — Who Should Have MSI/MMR Testing?

NCCN v2.2026 recommends:

> **Universal testing for MSI by PCR or NGS, or MMR by IHC, for all newly diagnosed gastric cancers.**

This is an important feature of PP-0182.

Testing is not restricted only to:

- metastatic disease;
- recurrent disease;
- older patients;
- intestinal-type tumors;
- patients already being considered for immunotherapy.

The current guideline recommendation is universal testing at diagnosis.

---

## CKB-14 — When Is MSI/MMR Testing Performed?

MSI/MMR testing belongs early in the gastric-cancer diagnostic and biomarker pathway.

The NCCN work-up includes universal MSI/MMR testing for newly diagnosed patients.

This means the result can be available before later treatment decisions are finalized.

---

## CKB-15 — What Tissue Is Used?

NCCN specifies that MSI/MMR testing is performed on:

> **formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue.**

This means the test uses tumor tissue preserved as part of routine pathology processing.

The tissue may originate from a diagnostic or surgical specimen.

---

## CKB-16 — Can a Biopsy Be Used?

Yes.

NCCN's pathology framework includes biopsy specimens in universal MSI/MMR testing.

A diagnostic endoscopic biopsy can therefore provide tumor tissue for biomarker assessment.

Whether a particular specimen is adequate is a pathology/laboratory determination.

---

## CKB-17 — Can an EMR Specimen Be Used?

Yes.

NCCN includes endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR) specimens within the pathology framework and recommends universal MSI/MMR testing if not already performed.

---

## CKB-18 — Can a Gastrectomy Specimen Be Used?

Yes.

NCCN includes gastrectomy specimens and recommends universal MSI/MMR testing if it was not performed previously.

Therefore the biomarker can be assessed using tissue obtained at different points in the diagnostic/surgical pathway.

---

## CKB-19 — How Is MSI Tested?

MSI can be assessed by:

- PCR;
- appropriate NGS-based testing.

PCR evaluates selected microsatellite markers for instability.

The exact marker panel, assay design, amplification conditions and analytical workflow belong to MSI Testing Methodology.

---

## CKB-20 — How Does NGS Assess MSI?

Some validated NGS approaches can determine MSI status while simultaneously providing broader molecular information.

For PP-0182, NGS is presented only as a conceptual MSI-assessment approach.

The package does not define:

- sequencing platforms;
- panel design;
- bioinformatics;
- variant calling;
- quality-control thresholds.

Those topics belong to NGS Biomarker Testing / MSI Testing Methodology.

---

## CKB-21 — What Is the MSI Interpretation Pathway?

Conceptually:

**Tumor tissue**

↓

**MSI testing**

↓

**MSS / MSI-L / MSI-H**

The clinically important high-instability category is:

**MSI-H**

The interpretation must use the validated assay's criteria.

---

## CKB-22 — What Is the MMR Interpretation Pathway?

Conceptually:

**Tumor tissue**

↓

**MMR IHC**

↓

**MMR protein expression pattern**

↓

**No loss of nuclear expression**

→ no evidence of dMMR / low probability of MSI-H

or

↓

**Loss of nuclear expression of one or more MMR proteins**

→ **dMMR**

---

## CKB-23 — Does MSI-H Mean the Same Thing as dMMR?

They are closely related, but the terms describe results from different testing approaches.

A useful patient-facing explanation is:

> **MSI-H is an instability result; dMMR is a mismatch-repair protein-expression result.**

Both can identify a tumor phenotype associated with mismatch-repair dysfunction.

NCCN specifically preserves the distinction between MSI testing and MMR IHC.

---

## CKB-24 — Why Is MSI-H/dMMR Clinically Important?

MSI-H/dMMR is clinically meaningful because it can influence:

- molecular characterization;
- treatment-selection discussions;
- immunotherapy considerations;
- hereditary-risk assessment in appropriate contexts.

It is therefore more than a descriptive laboratory finding.

---

## CKB-25 — Does MSI-H/dMMR Affect Immunotherapy Decisions?

Yes, it can.

NCCN and NCI materials identify MSI-H/dMMR as an important biomarker in the context of immune-checkpoint treatment.

The patient-facing message should remain:

> MSI-H/dMMR can make immunotherapy particularly relevant to treatment discussions.

It does not mean that the biomarker result alone determines the entire treatment plan.

---

## CKB-26 — Does MSI-H/dMMR Mean the Patient Will Definitely Receive Immunotherapy?

No.

Treatment selection depends on:

- disease stage;
- treatment setting;
- prior treatment;
- other biomarkers;
- applicable guideline recommendations;
- patient-specific factors;
- drug availability and suitability.

The dedicated Immune Checkpoint Inhibitors package owns detailed treatment information.

---

## CKB-27 — Is MSI-H/dMMR a Prognostic Marker?

NCCN reports that better prognosis has been observed in MSI-H/dMMR gastric tumors compared with microsatellite-stable/MMR-proficient tumors.

This is useful population-level context.

It should not be converted into:

> “MSI-H means your cancer will have a good outcome.”

Individual prognosis remains dependent on the complete clinical situation.

---

## CKB-28 — Are MSI-H/dMMR Tumors Associated With Particular Clinical Features?

NCCN reports associations with:

- earlier stage;
- older age;
- intestinal histologic subtype;
- distal stomach location.

These are associations, not diagnostic substitutes.

For example:

> An older patient does not automatically have dMMR.

> An intestinal-type tumor does not automatically have MSI-H.

Testing is still required.

---

## CKB-29 — How Common Is MSI-H/dMMR?

NCCN cites approximately:

- 22% MSI-H tumors in one TCGA gastric adenocarcinoma analysis;
- 19% MSI-H/dMMR in another pan-cancer whole-exome analysis.

These are population-level estimates.

They should not be interpreted as an individualized probability for a particular patient.

Prevalence can vary according to:

- age;
- sex;
- stage;
- histologic type;
- tumor location;
- study population;
- testing methodology.

---

## CKB-30 — Why Might Genetic Counseling Be Considered?

NCCN recommends referral to a genetics counselor for patients with MSI-H or dMMR tumors for further assessment in the appropriate clinical context.

The purpose is to determine whether the tumor finding raises concern for an inherited cancer predisposition.

This is a bridge from:

**tumor biomarker testing**

to

**hereditary cancer risk assessment.**

---

## CKB-31 — Does MSI-H/dMMR Automatically Mean Lynch Syndrome?

No.

MSI-H/dMMR is a tumor biomarker result.

Lynch syndrome is an inherited cancer-predisposition condition.

Some dMMR/MSI-H tumors may be associated with hereditary predisposition, but tumor testing alone does not establish that diagnosis.

---

## CKB-32 — Is Tumor Testing the Same as Germline Testing?

No.

### Tumor MSI/MMR testing asks:

> Does this tumor show MSI-H/dMMR characteristics?

### Germline genetic testing asks:

> Is there an inherited pathogenic genetic alteration that increases cancer risk?

A tumor result may provide a reason to consider genetic counseling, but it is not itself a germline diagnosis.

---

## CKB-33 — Does MSI/MMR Testing Replace Other Biomarker Testing?

No.

Gastric adenocarcinoma may require assessment of several biomarkers, depending on clinical setting, including:

- HER2;
- PD-L1;
- CLDN18.2;
- MSI/MMR;
- other molecular alterations.

MSI/MMR testing is one component of precision oncology.

---

## CKB-34 — What Are the Main Limitations?

Important limitations include:

- different assays measure different biological features;
- results depend on adequate tumor tissue;
- testing should use validated methods;
- pathology interpretation requires appropriate expertise;
- tumor biology can be complex;
- MSI-H/dMMR does not identify every molecular feature of a tumor;
- a biomarker result does not independently determine treatment;
- tumor testing does not by itself establish hereditary cancer.

---

## CKB-35 — What Might Appear on a Report?

Common terms include:

- MSI;
- MSI-H;
- MSI-L;
- MSS;
- MMR;
- dMMR;
- MMR-proficient;
- MLH1;
- MSH2;
- MSH6;
- PMS2;
- IHC;
- PCR;
- NGS;
- FFPE.

The central clinical question is:

> **Does this tumor show the MSI-H/dMMR phenotype, and what does that mean for the next clinical decisions?**

# 4. Patient Explanation

MSI and MMR are two related ways of looking for an important molecular feature of gastric cancer.

**MMR** stands for mismatch repair. It is a system that helps repair certain DNA-copying errors.

When MMR function is deficient, DNA errors can accumulate. One consequence can be **microsatellite instability (MSI)**.

Doctors can look for this feature in tumor tissue.

There are two main approaches:

### MSI testing

The tumor can be tested for microsatellite instability using PCR or an appropriate NGS-based method.

Results may be described as:

- MSS;
- MSI-L;
- MSI-H.

### MMR testing

The tumor can also be tested by IHC to see whether MMR proteins such as MLH1, MSH2, MSH6 and PMS2 are present in the expected pattern.

Loss of one or more relevant MMR proteins supports a finding of **dMMR**.

MSI-H and dMMR are closely related, but they are not literally the same test.

NCCN recommends universal MSI/MMR testing for newly diagnosed gastric cancer.

An MSI-H/dMMR result can be clinically important because it may influence treatment discussions, including whether immunotherapy may be relevant. It may also lead to consideration of genetic counseling in the appropriate context.

However:

> **MSI-H/dMMR does not automatically mean that a patient has Lynch syndrome, and it does not by itself determine the entire treatment plan.**

The clinical pathway is:

**New gastric cancer diagnosis**

↓

**Universal MSI/MMR testing**

↓

**MSI-H / MSI-L / MSS**

↓

# 7. Common Misconceptions

### Myth 1

**“MSI and MMR are exactly the same test.”**

**Fact:** They assess related biological features using different approaches.

### Myth 2

**“MSI-H means one specific gene is mutated.”**

**Fact:** MSI-H describes a pattern of microsatellite instability and does not by itself identify one specific gene alteration.

### Myth 3

**“dMMR automatically means Lynch syndrome.”**

**Fact:** dMMR is a tumor finding. Further hereditary assessment may be appropriate, but tumor testing alone does not diagnose Lynch syndrome.

### Myth 4

**“MSS means the tumor has no molecular abnormalities.”**

**Fact:** MSS describes microsatellite status only.

### Myth 5

**“Everyone with MSI-H gastric cancer must receive immunotherapy.”**

**Fact:** MSI-H/dMMR can influence treatment selection, but the complete clinical context determines treatment.

### Myth 6

**“MSI/MMR testing is only needed in metastatic disease.”**

**Fact:** NCCN recommends universal testing for all newly diagnosed gastric cancers.

### Myth 7

**“MSI/MMR testing is a blood test.”**

**Fact:** The NCCN pathway uses tumor tissue, including FFPE tissue.

### Myth 8

**“A biopsy cannot be used for MSI/MMR testing.”**

**Fact:** Biopsy specimens are included in the NCCN pathology framework.

### Myth 9

**“MSI-H proves the cancer is inherited.”**

**Fact:** Tumor MSI-H/dMMR does not by itself establish inherited cancer predisposition.

### Myth 10

**“MSI-H guarantees a good prognosis.”**

**Fact:** MSI-H/dMMR has been associated with better outcomes at the population level, but individual prognosis depends on the complete clinical situation.

### Myth 11

**“Normal MMR IHC means no other molecular testing is needed.”**

**Fact:** MSI/MMR is only one component of gastric-cancer biomarker assessment.

### Myth 12

**“NGS makes all other MSI/MMR testing unnecessary.”**

**Fact:** MSI may be assessed by PCR or appropriate NGS-based approaches, while MMR can be assessed by IHC.

### Myth 13

**“A tumor MSI-H result is the same as a positive germline genetic test.”**

**Fact:** Tumor biomarker testing and germline testing answer different questions.

### Myth 14

**“MSI-L has the same clinical meaning as MSI-H.”**

**Fact:** MSI-H is the high-instability category with the major clinical relevance discussed in this package.

### Myth 15

**“An MSI/MMR result determines the whole treatment plan.”**

**Fact:** Treatment decisions integrate MSI/MMR with stage, other biomarkers, prior treatment, guidelines and patient-specific factors.
