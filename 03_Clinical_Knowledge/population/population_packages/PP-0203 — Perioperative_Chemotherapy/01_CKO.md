# Educational Objectives

After reading this Population Package, the reader should be able to:

1. Explain what perioperative chemotherapy means in gastric cancer.
2. Distinguish perioperative chemotherapy from neoadjuvant therapy and adjuvant therapy.
3. Understand why systemic chemotherapy may be given both before and after curative-intent surgery.
4. Recognize the clinical setting in which perioperative chemotherapy is relevant: appropriately selected patients with localized, resectable gastric or gastroesophageal-junction adenocarcinoma treated with curative intent.
5. Understand the conceptual sequence of preoperative treatment, reassessment, surgery, postoperative treatment, and follow-up.
6. Understand why surgery remains a central component of the curative-intent strategy.
7. Understand the rationale for giving systemic treatment before surgery rather than relying on surgery alone in selected patients.
8. Recognize the evidence foundation for perioperative chemotherapy, including the MAGIC and AIO-FLOT4 randomized trials.
9. Understand the contemporary role of FLOT as a principal regimen example without treating FLOT and perioperative chemotherapy as synonymous.
10. Understand that postoperative treatment is influenced by pathology, margins, nodal findings, extent of lymphadenectomy, prior treatment, and recovery.
11. Understand that treatment completion may differ from the originally planned sequence because of toxicity, postoperative recovery, complications, or changes in disease status.
12. Recognize common treatment-related adverse effects at a high level without attempting to manage them independently.
13. Understand that treatment selection depends on stage, resectability, functional status, comorbidities, biomarkers where relevant, patient preferences, and multidisciplinary assessment.
14. Recognize that perioperative chemotherapy is distinct from palliative systemic therapy for metastatic or unresectable disease.
15. Use appropriate questions when discussing perioperative treatment with the cancer care team.

# Scope

## Primary Educational Question

> **What is perioperative chemotherapy in gastric cancer, why is chemotherapy given before and after surgery, who may be considered for this strategy, how does the treatment sequence work, what evidence supports it, and how should patients understand its benefits, limitations, and relationship to surgery and other treatment strategies?**

This Population Package is a **patient-facing, source-grounded treatment-strategy package**.

It explains perioperative chemotherapy as a multimodality strategy rather than as a single drug regimen.

The package is deliberately positioned between:

**treatment overview / surgical treatment**

and

**specific systemic-therapy packages such as FLOT, adjuvant therapy, neoadjuvant therapy, chemoradiation, targeted therapy, immunotherapy, and palliative systemic therapy.**

The package therefore owns the **strategy and sequence**, while downstream packages own detailed treatment modalities.

# Included

## 1. Definition of Perioperative Chemotherapy

Perioperative chemotherapy refers to systemic chemotherapy delivered as part of a treatment strategy that spans the period around definitive surgery.

In the gastric-cancer setting, the concept generally includes:

**preoperative systemic therapy**

followed by

**surgery**

followed by

**postoperative systemic therapy**

when appropriate.

The defining feature is therefore not one specific drug.

The defining feature is the **relationship of systemic therapy to surgery**.

---

## 2. Clinical Treatment Context

PP-0203 covers perioperative chemotherapy primarily in the setting of:

- localized gastric cancer;
- resectable disease;
- curative-intent treatment;
- appropriately selected patients;
- gastric and, where represented in the supporting evidence, gastroesophageal-junction adenocarcinoma.

The package does not treat perioperative chemotherapy as a universal treatment for every gastric-cancer patient.

Treatment decisions remain dependent on disease extent, resectability, patient fitness, comorbidities, biomarkers where relevant, treatment goals, and multidisciplinary assessment.

---

## 3. Why Systemic Treatment Is Integrated Around Surgery

The package explains the clinical logic at a conceptual level.

Gastric cancer can carry a risk of microscopic disease beyond the visible primary tumor.

Surgery addresses the known local/regional disease that is resectable.

Systemic chemotherapy can address disease risk outside the surgical field.

The perioperative strategy therefore integrates:

**local-regional treatment**

with

**systemic treatment**.

This is the central clinical concept of the package.

---

## 4. The Preoperative Component

The package explains why chemotherapy may be given before surgery.

The preoperative component can:

- begin systemic treatment before definitive surgery;
- provide treatment to disease that may not be visible on imaging;
- allow reassessment after systemic therapy;
- provide information about treatment response;
- form the first component of the planned perioperative sequence.

This is explained conceptually.

Detailed neoadjuvant therapy is delegated to PP-0206.

Detailed FLOT treatment is delegated to PP-0204.

---

## 5. Restaging and Reassessment

The package explains that preoperative treatment is followed by clinical reassessment.

The sequence is conceptually:

**preoperative systemic therapy**

↓

**reassessment / restaging**

↓

**determine whether curative-intent surgery remains appropriate**

↓

**surgery when appropriate**

The package does not prescribe an individualized restaging protocol.

---

## 6. The Surgical Interface

Surgery remains a central component of curative-intent treatment for appropriately selected localized resectable disease.

Perioperative chemotherapy is therefore positioned as treatment **around** surgery.

It does not automatically replace surgery.

The package may mention:

- gastrectomy;
- regional lymphadenectomy;
- R0 resection;
- subtotal versus total gastrectomy

only as interfaces.

Detailed surgical principles are delegated to PP-0196–PP-0198.

Detailed lymphadenectomy is delegated to PP-0199–PP-0201.

---

## 7. The Postoperative Component

The package explains that the postoperative component is part of the perioperative strategy.

After surgery, the clinical team considers:

- pathology;
- tumor stage;
- nodal status;
- surgical margins;
- extent of lymph-node dissection;
- previous treatment;
- postoperative recovery;
- overall patient fitness.

Postoperative treatment is therefore not presented as an automatic mechanical repetition of the preoperative treatment.

Detailed adjuvant therapy belongs to PP-0205.

## 8. Evidence Foundation

The package uses the project Source Materials to explain two major randomized evidence anchors.

### MAGIC

The NCI Gastric Cancer Treatment PDQ reports the randomized phase III MAGIC trial, in which patients with stage II or higher gastric/lower-third esophageal adenocarcinoma received perioperative ECF or surgery alone.

Compared with surgery alone, perioperative chemotherapy improved:

- progression-free survival;
- overall survival.

The reported 5-year overall survival was 36.3% with perioperative chemotherapy versus 23% with surgery alone.

Source: NCI, Gastric Cancer Treatment PDQ, Perioperative Chemotherapy section.

### AIO-FLOT4

The NCI PDQ reports the randomized phase III AIO-FLOT4 trial in 716 patients with stage IB–III resectable gastric or gastroesophageal adenocarcinoma.

FLOT was compared with ECF/ECX.

Reported outcomes included:

- median OS 50 months with FLOT versus 35 months with ECF/ECX;
- HR for death 0.77;
- margin-free resection 85% versus 78%.

Toxicity patterns differed between treatment groups.

Source: NCI, Gastric Cancer Treatment PDQ, Perioperative Chemotherapy section.

NCCN v2.2026 provides the contemporary guideline context and identifies perioperative systemic therapy as a treatment framework for localized resectable disease, with FLOT as a preferred category-1 regimen in the relevant setting.

## 15. Treatment Completion

The planned perioperative sequence may not be identical to the treatment actually delivered.

Treatment may be:

- delayed;
- modified;
- reduced;
- discontinued;

because of:

- toxicity;
- postoperative complications;
- recovery;
- functional decline;
- disease progression;
- other clinical factors.

Failure to deliver every planned cycle is not automatically equivalent to treatment failure.

---

## 16. Treatment Toxicity

The package provides high-level awareness of possible chemotherapy effects.

Depending on regimen, patients may experience:

- nausea;
- diarrhea;
- fatigue;
- low blood counts;
- infection risk;
- anemia;
- neuropathy;
- other regimen-specific adverse effects.

The package does not provide toxicity-management algorithms.

---

## 17. Pathology and Postoperative Findings

Pathology after surgery provides information that may influence postoperative management.

Relevant concepts include:

- pathologic stage;
- lymph-node status;
- margin status;
- response to preoperative therapy;
- extent of lymphadenectomy.

PP-0203 explains these only as postoperative decision interfaces.

Detailed pathology interpretation is delegated.

# Clinical Knowledge Blocks

# Block 1 — What Is Perioperative Chemotherapy?

Perioperative chemotherapy is systemic chemotherapy delivered around surgery as part of a planned multimodality treatment strategy.

The simple sequence is:

**chemotherapy before surgery**

↓

**surgery**

↓

**chemotherapy after surgery**

when appropriate.

The key concept is:

> **Perioperative describes the timing and relationship of systemic treatment to surgery. It does not name a single chemotherapy drug.**

---

# Block 2 — Why Is Chemotherapy Given Around Surgery?

Surgery can remove the known resectable tumor and regional disease.

However, gastric cancer can also carry a risk of microscopic disease elsewhere in the body.

Systemic chemotherapy is intended to address cancer cells that may not be visible or removable through surgery alone.

The rationale is therefore:

**local treatment**

+

**systemic treatment**

rather than relying on one modality alone in patients for whom multimodality treatment is appropriate.

---

# Block 3 — Why Give Chemotherapy Before Surgery?

The preoperative component allows systemic treatment to begin before definitive surgery.

Potential clinical purposes include:

- addressing systemic disease risk early;
- treating the known cancer before surgery;
- potentially producing tumor response;
- assessing disease behavior during treatment;
- completing part of the systemic treatment plan before a major operation.

These are strategic purposes.

They do not mean that every patient will have the same degree of tumor shrinkage or that chemotherapy guarantees a successful operation.

---

# Block 4 — Why Give Chemotherapy After Surgery?

The postoperative component completes the planned perioperative strategy when clinically appropriate.

After surgery, the team has additional information from:

- the surgical findings;
- the pathology report;
- tumor stage;
- lymph-node status;
- margin status;
- treatment response;
- postoperative recovery.

Therefore the postoperative plan is informed by the entire treatment course.

---

# Block 5 — Does Perioperative Chemotherapy Replace Surgery?

No.

For appropriate localized resectable gastric cancer treated with curative intent, surgery remains a central part of the treatment strategy.

Perioperative chemotherapy is positioned around surgery.

It does not automatically substitute for surgery.

---

# Block 6 — How Does the Treatment Sequence Work?

A conceptual sequence is:

**Diagnosis and staging**

↓

**Multidisciplinary treatment planning**

↓

**Preoperative systemic therapy**

↓

**Reassessment / restaging**

↓

**Surgery when appropriate**

↓

**Pathology**

↓

**Postoperative systemic therapy when appropriate**

↓

**Follow-up**

This is a conceptual framework rather than a rigid schedule for every patient.

---

# Block 7 — What Happens During Reassessment?

After preoperative treatment, the clinical team reassesses the disease and the patient's condition.

The purpose is to determine whether the patient remains an appropriate candidate for planned curative-intent surgery.

Reassessment may consider:

- disease status;
- resectability;
- response;
- new evidence of progression;
- overall health.

The exact tests and timing depend on the clinical context and are not specified as a universal algorithm in this PP.

---

# Block 8 — What Evidence Supports Perioperative Chemotherapy?

Two major randomized trials in the project Source Materials provide the principal evidence anchors.

## MAGIC

Perioperative ECF improved progression-free survival and overall survival compared with surgery alone.

The NCI PDQ reports a 5-year OS rate of 36.3% versus 23%.

## FLOT4

FLOT improved median OS compared with ECF/ECX:

**50 months versus 35 months**

with HR 0.77.

FLOT also produced a higher margin-free resection proportion:

**85% versus 78%**.

These results support the strategy and the contemporary evolution of the regimen used within it.

---

# Block 9 — What Is FLOT's Role?

FLOT is a specific perioperative chemotherapy regimen.

The relationship is:

**Perioperative chemotherapy**

↓

**FLOT**

Therefore:

> **FLOT is not synonymous with perioperative chemotherapy.**

A patient can be discussing the perioperative strategy without yet discussing the detailed FLOT regimen.

Detailed FLOT content belongs to PP-0204.

---

# Block 10 — What Does “Curative Intent” Mean?

Curative intent means that treatment is being planned with the goal of eliminating the cancer and achieving long-term disease control or cure.

For PP-0203, this concept is linked to:

- localized disease;
- resectable disease;
- planned definitive surgery.

It does not guarantee cure.

It describes the **goal of treatment**.

---

# Block 11 — Who May Be Considered?

Perioperative chemotherapy is considered for appropriately selected patients.

Selection depends on more than stage.

The treatment team may consider:

- tumor stage;
- resectability;
- functional status;
- comorbidities;
- organ function;
- nutritional condition;
- biomarkers where relevant;
- previous treatment;
- patient preferences;
- multidisciplinary assessment.

Therefore:

> **“Localized and resectable” identifies the broad clinical setting; it does not automatically determine an individual treatment plan.**

---

# Block 12 — Why Does Fitness Matter?

Perioperative chemotherapy can produce substantial treatment effects and adverse effects.

The patient's ability to tolerate treatment matters.

A patient who is medically fit may be considered for an intensive regimen that would not be appropriate for another patient with:

- major comorbidities;
- poor functional status;
- significant organ dysfunction;
- severe nutritional compromise.

The package does not prescribe a regimen based on these factors.

---

# Block 13 — Why Does Pathology Matter After Surgery?

The operation provides tissue for pathological evaluation.

The pathology may provide information about:

- residual tumor;
- tumor stage;
- lymph-node involvement;
- margin status;
- treatment response.

These findings can influence postoperative management.

Therefore the perioperative sequence is not simply:

**preoperative treatment → surgery → repeat exactly the same plan**.

The postoperative phase incorporates the new information.

---

# Block 14 — Why Does Lymphadenectomy Matter?

Regional lymphadenectomy is part of gastric cancer surgery.

The extent of lymph-node dissection can influence postoperative treatment pathways.

PP-0203 only teaches the interface:

> **What was done surgically can influence what postoperative treatment is appropriate.**

It does not teach:

- D1 technique;
- D2 technique;
- lymph-node stations;
- operative steps.

Those belong to PP-0199–PP-0201.

---

# Block 15 — Why Does Margin Status Matter?

A surgical margin indicates whether cancer is identified at the edge of the removed specimen.

Margin status is one of the postoperative factors that can influence treatment planning.

The package does not provide detailed pathology or surgical-margin management.

---

# Block 16 — What If the Patient Cannot Complete the Planned Treatment?

The planned sequence is a target, not a guarantee.

Treatment can be changed because of:

- chemotherapy toxicity;
- postoperative complications;
- delayed recovery;
- reduced functional status;
- progression;
- other medical issues.

The treatment team weighs benefit and harm when deciding whether to continue, modify, delay, or stop therapy.

---

# Block 17 — What Are Common Chemotherapy Side Effects?

Depending on the regimen, patients may experience:

- nausea;
- diarrhea;
- fatigue;
- reduced blood counts;
- infection;
- anemia;
- neuropathy;
- appetite or nutritional problems;
- other treatment-specific effects.

The exact adverse-effect profile depends on the regimen.

FLOT4 showed that the overall rates of treatment-related hospitalization were similar between FLOT and ECF/ECX, but the pattern of adverse effects differed.

Patients should report concerning symptoms promptly rather than attempting to manage serious toxicity alone.

---

# Block 18 — Perioperative Versus Adjuvant Therapy

| Concept | Timing | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Perioperative chemotherapy | Before + after surgery | Systemic treatment integrated around surgery |
| Adjuvant therapy | After surgery | Postoperative treatment |
| Neoadjuvant therapy | Before definitive local therapy | Preoperative treatment |
| Chemoradiation | Chemotherapy + radiation | Separate multimodality strategy |

The terms overlap conceptually but are not interchangeable.

---

# Block 19 — Perioperative Chemotherapy Versus Chemoradiation

Chemoradiation combines systemic treatment with radiation.

It may be relevant in selected clinical settings.

However:

> **PP-0203 owns perioperative chemotherapy, not detailed chemoradiation.**

Detailed chemoradiation belongs to PP-0207.

---

# Block 20 — Perioperative Chemotherapy Versus Palliative Chemotherapy

The treatment intent is different.

### Perioperative chemotherapy

Used around planned curative-intent surgery in appropriate localized/resectable disease.

### Palliative systemic therapy

Used when disease is advanced, metastatic, or otherwise not being treated with curative-intent surgery.

Detailed palliative systemic treatment belongs to PP-0208.

---

# Block 21 — What If Disease Progresses Before Surgery?

Progression during preoperative treatment can change the treatment pathway.

This is one reason reassessment is important.

The package does not prescribe what alternative treatment should be given after progression.

The appropriate next step depends on:

- extent of progression;
- resectability;
- patient condition;
- previous treatment;
- tumor biology;
- multidisciplinary assessment.

---

# Block 22 — What If Surgery Is No Longer Possible?

The perioperative pathway may no longer apply in the same form if curative-intent surgery is no longer feasible.

This does not mean that no treatment is available.

It means that treatment intent and strategy may change.

Detailed management of unresectable/metastatic disease is outside PP-0203.

---

# Block 23 — Why Is Multidisciplinary Care Important?

Perioperative treatment crosses multiple specialties.

The treatment plan can involve:

- gastroenterology;
- surgery;
- medical oncology;
- radiation oncology when relevant;
- pathology;
- radiology;
- nutrition/supportive care;
- other specialists.

ESMO-ASCO's gastric-cancer curriculum specifically emphasizes multidisciplinary treatment planning and treatment sequencing.

---

# Block 24 — What Does “Treatment Sequencing” Mean?

Treatment sequencing means deciding:

**which treatment comes first**

and

**which treatment follows**

within the overall cancer plan.

For PP-0203:

**systemic treatment**

↓

**surgery**

↓

**postoperative systemic treatment**

The sequence is designed around the patient's disease and treatment goal.

---

# Block 25 — Why Is the Postoperative Plan Not Known Completely at Diagnosis?

Some postoperative information is unavailable before surgery.

After surgery, the team obtains:

- final pathology;
- nodal findings;
- margin status;
- treatment response;
- postoperative recovery information.

Therefore postoperative decisions can be refined after surgery.

This is a central concept in patient education.

---

# Block 26 — Can Biomarkers Affect Perioperative Treatment?

Yes, biomarkers can influence treatment selection in gastric cancer.

However, the biomarker-specific interpretation is outside PP-0203.

Current NCCN material includes biomarker-defined treatment contexts, including selected MSI-H/dMMR and other biomarker-related systemic treatment options.

PP-0203 therefore acknowledges biomarkers as a treatment-selection context without becoming a biomarker-testing or immunotherapy package.

Detailed biomarker testing belongs to dedicated packages.

---

# Block 27 — What Is the Relationship With Immunotherapy?

The perioperative treatment landscape is evolving.

Current guideline materials include selected perioperative systemic therapy approaches involving immunotherapy in defined contexts.

However:

> **PP-0203 is not the immunotherapy package.**

Detailed immunotherapy belongs to PP-0213–PP-0216 and related downstream packages.

---

# Block 28 — Why Is FLOT4 Important?

FLOT4 provides evidence for the evolution of perioperative chemotherapy.

The trial compared:

**FLOT**

with

**ECF/ECX**

in resectable gastric/GEJ adenocarcinoma.

The reported survival difference supported FLOT's contemporary role.

The package uses FLOT4 to answer:

> **Why has the preferred perioperative regimen evolved?**

It does not use FLOT4 to teach every practical detail of FLOT.

---

# Block 29 — Why Is MAGIC Still Important?

MAGIC provides the historical randomized evidence for the broader principle:

> **Perioperative chemotherapy can improve outcomes compared with surgery alone.**

This makes MAGIC an evidence anchor for the strategy itself.

FLOT4 then illustrates the evolution of the regimen used within that strategy.

The two trials therefore have different educational roles.

---

# Block 30 — What Does “Margin-Free Resection” Mean?

A margin-free resection means that cancer was not identified at the relevant resection margin.

FLOT4 reported a higher margin-free resection proportion with FLOT than with ECF/ECX.

This is one reason the trial is relevant to the relationship between systemic therapy and surgical outcomes.

The package does not infer that every patient receiving perioperative chemotherapy will achieve an R0/margin-free resection.

---

# Block 31 — What Does Treatment Response Mean?

Treatment response describes how the cancer changes during or after treatment.

Possible concepts include:

- tumor shrinkage;
- stable disease;
- progression;
- pathological response after surgery.

Response is only one part of the treatment assessment.

A patient does not need to have a dramatic radiological shrinkage for treatment to have clinical value.

---

# Block 32 — What Is the Difference Between Response and Cure?

A tumor can respond to treatment without guaranteeing cure.

Likewise, treatment can be clinically useful even when the tumor does not visibly shrink.

Curative intent refers to the overall goal of treatment.

Response is one measure within that broader process.

---

# Block 33 — What Is Treatment Failure?

The package should avoid defining failure solely as:

> “the patient did not complete every cycle.”

Clinical treatment outcomes are more complex.

Failure may involve:

- disease progression;
- recurrence;
- inability to tolerate treatment;
- complications;
- other clinical events.

Treatment completion and treatment efficacy are related but not identical concepts.

# Key Messages

1. **Perioperative chemotherapy is a treatment strategy, not a single drug.**
2. It integrates systemic chemotherapy around surgery.
3. The strategy is primarily relevant to appropriately selected localized, resectable gastric cancer treated with curative intent.
4. Surgery remains a central component of the curative-intent pathway.
5. Preoperative therapy is followed by reassessment before surgery.
6. Postoperative treatment is part of the planned perioperative strategy when appropriate.
7. MAGIC provides randomized evidence supporting perioperative chemotherapy versus surgery alone.
8. FLOT4 supports the contemporary role of FLOT within the perioperative strategy.
9. FLOT and perioperative chemotherapy should not be treated as synonymous.
10. Postoperative treatment depends on pathology, margins, nodal findings, extent of lymphadenectomy, previous treatment, and recovery.
11. Planned treatment and delivered treatment may differ.
12. Toxicity and postoperative recovery can alter the treatment sequence.
13. Biomarkers can influence treatment selection, but biomarker-specific interpretation belongs to dedicated packages.
14. Perioperative chemotherapy is distinct from adjuvant therapy, neoadjuvant therapy, chemoradiation, and palliative systemic therapy.
15. Individual treatment decisions must be made with the multidisciplinary cancer-care team.

# Boundary

**Core =** Perioperative chemotherapy as a curative-intent treatment strategy; localized/resectable disease context; rationale for systemic treatment around surgery; preoperative and postoperative components; treatment sequence; reassessment before surgery; surgery interface; pathology and postoperative-management interface; MAGIC and FLOT4 evidence anchors; FLOT as a principal contemporary regimen example; treatment completion; high-level toxicity awareness; patient-facing terminology, misconceptions, and questions.

**Supporting =** Selected survival and surgical-outcome data from MAGIC/FLOT4; treatment-response concepts; performance status and fitness; nutritional/recovery context; margin and lymphadenectomy interfaces; biomarker/immunotherapy evolution; multidisciplinary treatment planning; clinical-trial context.

**Explicitly Excluded =** Detailed FLOT regimen/dosing/cycle administration; detailed adjuvant therapy; detailed neoadjuvant therapy; detailed chemoradiation; palliative/metastatic systemic therapy; targeted therapy; immunotherapy algorithms; biomarker testing; individualized regimen selection; detailed gastrectomy or lymphadenectomy technique; detailed pathology; detailed response-assessment methodology; detailed toxicity management; dose modification and administration logistics.

**Delegated-to PP =** PP-0196–PP-0202 for surgery/lymphadenectomy; PP-0204 FLOT; PP-0205 Adjuvant Therapy; PP-0206 Neoadjuvant Therapy; PP-0207 Chemoradiation; PP-0208 Palliative Systemic Therapy; PP-0209–PP-0212 targeted-therapy packages; PP-0213–PP-0216 immunotherapy packages; relevant biomarker-testing packages.
