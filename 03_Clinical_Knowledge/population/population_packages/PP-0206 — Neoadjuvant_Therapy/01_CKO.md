# Educational Objectives

After reading this Population Package, the reader should be able to:

1. Explain what neoadjuvant therapy means in gastric cancer.
2. Distinguish neoadjuvant or preoperative therapy from perioperative therapy and adjuvant therapy.
3. Understand why treatment may sometimes be given before definitive surgery.
4. Understand that treatment before surgery can be part of a planned curative-intent pathway and does not automatically mean that cancer is unresectable.
5. Recognize the importance of accurate staging and resectability assessment before selecting a preoperative treatment strategy.
6. Understand the relationship between preoperative treatment, response assessment, and subsequent surgical evaluation.
7. Recognize that not every patient with gastric cancer needs treatment before surgery.
8. Understand the source-supported role of perioperative chemotherapy and its preoperative component without turning this package into a regimen-specific package.
9. Understand that selected patients with MSI-H/dMMR gastric tumors may have a neoadjuvant or perioperative immunotherapy pathway.
10. Understand why FLOT is a regimen, whereas neoadjuvant therapy is a treatment-timing/strategy concept.
11. Understand why treatment response, including pathologic response, can provide clinically relevant information without equating response with cure.
12. Recognize that treatment selection depends on stage, resectability, tumor characteristics, previous treatment, patient fitness, and multidisciplinary assessment.
13. Understand what may happen if disease progresses or becomes unresectable during preoperative treatment.
14. Use patient-facing questions to discuss the proposed preoperative treatment plan with the cancer care team.

> **What is neoadjuvant therapy in gastric cancer, why may treatment be given before definitive surgery, how does it fit within the overall treatment sequence, what evidence supports preoperative treatment, and how are response, surgery, tumor biology, and patient fitness integrated into this strategy?**

It explains the clinical meaning and role of treatment delivered before definitive surgery. It deliberately separates the timing/strategy concept from the details of individual drugs, chemotherapy regimens, immunotherapy regimens, surgery, biomarker testing, and formal response-assessment methodology.

# Clinical Knowledge Blocks

# 1. What Is Neoadjuvant Therapy?

Neoadjuvant therapy means treatment given **before definitive surgery**.

In gastric cancer, the treatment may be systemic therapy or, in selected situations, immunotherapy.

The defining feature is **timing relative to surgery**.

It is not the name of a particular drug.

It is not synonymous with chemotherapy.

It is not synonymous with FLOT.

It is not synonymous with perioperative therapy.

A useful mental model is:

**Neoadjuvant therapy**

→ **reassessment**

→ **definitive surgery when appropriate**

→ **postoperative treatment when part of the planned strategy**

---

# 2. Why Is Treatment Sometimes Given Before Surgery?

Surgery removes the visible/localized cancer.

Systemic treatment given before surgery can address the possibility that cancer cells may already exist outside the main tumor but are too small to be detected by routine staging.

The source materials support a broader treatment strategy in which systemic treatment can be given around surgery.

The goal is therefore not simply:

> “make the tumor smaller.”

Potential goals include:

- treating systemic disease earlier;
- treating the primary tumor before surgery;
- assessing treatment sensitivity;
- potentially improving the surgical situation;
- integrating systemic therapy into the complete curative-intent pathway.

These are strategy-level concepts rather than guarantees for an individual patient.

---

# 3. Does Neoadjuvant Therapy Mean the Cancer Cannot Be Operated On?

No.

Neoadjuvant treatment can be used in a planned pathway for potentially resectable disease.

NCCN describes perioperative systemic therapy as a category 1 recommendation for medically fit patients with resectable T2 or higher tumors, any N, and identifies neoadjuvant/perioperative immunotherapy as an option in certain MSI-H/dMMR tumors.

Therefore:

> **Receiving treatment before surgery does not by itself mean that the cancer is unresectable.**

The purpose of the preoperative treatment is to be part of the planned overall strategy.

---

# 4. Why Accurate Staging Comes First

The decision to use treatment before surgery depends on knowing as accurately as possible:

- where the primary tumor is;
- how deeply it has invaded;
- whether regional lymph nodes are involved;
- whether distant disease is present;
- whether the tumor is considered resectable;
- whether the patient is medically fit for the proposed treatment.

The ACS source emphasizes that accurate staging is important before attempting surgery in potentially resectable gastric cancer and describes CT/PET, EUS, and staging laparoscopy as tools that may be used in appropriate situations.

Therefore:

**staging**

→ **resectability assessment**

→ **treatment-sequence decision**

is a central architecture of PP-0206.

---

# 5. Why Not Every Gastric Cancer Needs Neoadjuvant Therapy

Very early gastric cancers follow different pathways.

NCCN identifies endoscopic resection as the primary treatment option for Tis or T1a tumors.

For medically fit patients with resectable T1b or higher disease, surgery with D2 lymph-node dissection may be used.

For resectable T2 or higher tumors, perioperative systemic therapy is a category 1 recommendation.

This demonstrates that treatment timing is stage-dependent.

Therefore PP-0206 must never teach:

> “All gastric cancers require treatment before surgery.”

---

# 6. Neoadjuvant vs Perioperative Therapy

These terms are related but not interchangeable.

### Neoadjuvant / preoperative therapy

Treatment:

**before surgery**

### Adjuvant therapy

Treatment:

**after surgery**

### Perioperative therapy

Treatment:

**before + after surgery**

Therefore a regimen given before and after surgery is part of a perioperative strategy.

Its preoperative portion is also a neoadjuvant/preoperative treatment phase.

This distinction prevents PP-0206 from duplicating PP-0203.

---

# 7. Why FLOT Is Not the Same as Neoadjuvant Therapy

FLOT is a chemotherapy regimen.

Neoadjuvant therapy describes treatment timing.

FLOT can be delivered before surgery as part of a perioperative strategy.

Therefore:

**FLOT = regimen**

while

**neoadjuvant therapy = treatment phase/strategy concept**.

PP-0204 owns FLOT.

PP-0206 owns the neoadjuvant concept.

---

# 8. Evidence From MAGIC

The MAGIC trial evaluated perioperative chemotherapy in patients with stage II or higher adenocarcinoma of the stomach or lower third of the esophagus.

Patients received three cycles of ECF before and after surgery or surgery alone.

Compared with surgery alone, perioperative chemotherapy improved:

- progression-free survival: HR 0.66;
- overall survival: HR 0.75;
- 5-year overall survival: 36.3% versus 23%.

This is important evidence that a treatment strategy containing a preoperative phase can improve outcomes.

However, MAGIC was **not a pure neoadjuvant-only trial**.

It was a perioperative trial.

Therefore its primary architectural ownership belongs to the perioperative strategy, while PP-0206 uses it to explain why the preoperative phase matters.

---

# 9. Evidence From FLOT4

FLOT4 compared perioperative FLOT with ECF/ECX in 716 patients with stage IB to III resectable gastric or gastroesophageal adenocarcinoma.

Median overall survival was:

- 50 months with FLOT;
- 35 months with ECF/ECX.

HR was 0.77.

Margin-free resection was:

- 85% with FLOT;
- 78% with ECF/ECX.

This provides important evidence for modern perioperative systemic treatment.

Again, it is not a pure neoadjuvant-only trial.

The preoperative component is clinically relevant to PP-0206, but the regimen itself belongs to PP-0204 and the broader perioperative strategy belongs to PP-0203.

---

# 10. Why the Preoperative Component Matters

A perioperative trial cannot be reduced to “chemotherapy after surgery.”

The treatment begins before surgery.

That means the patient receives systemic therapy while the tumor is still present and before definitive local resection.

This creates an opportunity to:

- treat systemic disease earlier;
- observe tumor behavior;
- assess whether the patient remains on a surgical pathway;
- integrate response information into the next decision.

---

# 11. Neoadjuvant Immunotherapy Is a Separate but Important Branch

Current NCCN guidance identifies:

> **Neoadjuvant or perioperative immunotherapy — useful in certain circumstances — for MSI-H/dMMR tumors.**

This is an important update to the treatment landscape.

It means that the neoadjuvant concept cannot be defined solely as chemotherapy.

However, it is also incorrect to generalize neoadjuvant immunotherapy to all gastric cancers.

The evidence is strongest in the specific molecularly defined setting represented by MSI-H/dMMR tumors.

---

# 12. Why MSI-H/dMMR Matters

MSI-H means microsatellite instability-high.

dMMR means deficient mismatch repair.

These tumors have a distinctive molecular biology and can be particularly sensitive to immune checkpoint inhibition.

The current NCCN source therefore identifies selected neoadjuvant/perioperative immunotherapy pathways for MSI-H/dMMR gastric tumors.

PP-0206 owns only the **strategy-level relationship**.

The dedicated biomarker and immunotherapy PPs own detailed testing and treatment.

---

# 13. NEONIPIGA as a Source-Supported Example

NCCN summarizes the GERCOR NEONIPIGA phase II trial.

The study included 32 patients with locally advanced gastric or EGJ adenocarcinoma with confirmed MSI-H/dMMR.

Patients received neoadjuvant nivolumab plus ipilimumab followed by adjuvant nivolumab.

After neoadjuvant therapy:

- 29 patients underwent R0 resection;
- a little more than half, approximately 59%, had a pathologic complete response;
- among 31 patients evaluated for survival, 97% had a survival benefit as described by NCCN.

NCCN recommends nivolumab plus ipilimumab followed by nivolumab as a category 2A option useful in certain circumstances for MSI-H/dMMR gastric tumors in the neoadjuvant or perioperative settings.

The evidence is promising but remains specialized and should not be generalized to unselected gastric cancer.

---

# 14. Neoadjuvant Pembrolizumab Evidence

NCCN also summarizes a phase II study of neoadjuvant pembrolizumab in locally advanced MSI-H/dMMR solid tumors.

Among 33 assessed patients:

- ORR was 82%;
- CR was 30%.

Among 17 patients whose tumors were surgically resected:

- 65% had a pathologic complete response.

The population was not exclusively gastric cancer, because most patients had stage III colorectal cancer.

Therefore the evidence is relevant to the MSI-H/dMMR neoadjuvant concept but should not be presented as a gastric-cancer-specific randomized result.

---

# 15. Why the MSI-H/dMMR Branch Must Stay Specialized

The source evidence does not support:

> “All patients with gastric cancer should receive neoadjuvant immunotherapy.”

The source-supported statement is narrower:

> Selected patients with MSI-H/dMMR gastric tumors may have a neoadjuvant or perioperative immunotherapy option.

That distinction is essential for safe patient education.

---

# 16. Response Assessment After Preoperative Therapy

NCCN includes response assessment after preoperative therapy.

Depending on the pathway, assessment may include:

- CT;
- FDG-PET/CT when clinically indicated;
- EGD and biopsy;
- other assessment according to the treatment pathway.

The purpose is not simply to measure whether the tumor became smaller.

The clinical team may need to determine:

- whether disease has progressed;
- whether disease remains localized;
- whether the patient remains a surgical candidate;
- whether there is new metastatic disease.

PP-0206 introduces this decision point.

Formal response methodology is delegated.

---

# 17. Why Restaging Matters

Preoperative treatment changes the clinical state.

Therefore the team needs to reassess the patient before definitive surgery.

A simplified pathway is:

**Before treatment**

→ baseline staging

→ **neoadjuvant therapy**

→ **post-treatment reassessment**

→ **surgical decision**

This is particularly important if there is:

- persistent local disease;
- no evidence of disease;
- new metastatic disease;
- evidence that the disease is unresectable.

---

# 18. What If There Is No Evidence of Disease After Neoadjuvant Immunotherapy?

This is an important modern uncertainty.

The NCCN source explicitly states that in MSI-H/dMMR tumors, the role of surgery after biopsy-proven and radiologic/metabolic complete response to neoadjuvant immunotherapy is unclear.

Therefore PP-0206 must **not** teach a universal:

> “complete response means no surgery.”

The correct patient-facing message is:

> A complete response can create a complex treatment decision, and the role of surgery may depend on the specific treatment pathway and multidisciplinary assessment.

---

# 19. What If Local Disease Persists?

Persistent local disease after preoperative treatment does not automatically determine the next treatment.

The clinical team reassesses:

- resectability;
- disease distribution;
- treatment response;
- patient fitness;
- available treatment options.

PP-0206 owns the **reassessment concept**, not the complete salvage algorithm.

---

# 20. What If New Metastatic Disease Appears?

New metastatic disease can change treatment intent and pathway.

The patient may no longer be managed according to the original curative-intent surgical pathway.

The package should therefore route this situation to downstream advanced/metastatic treatment knowledge rather than provide a universal salvage regimen.

---

# 21. Pathologic Response

Pathologic response is assessed in the surgical specimen after preoperative therapy.

It can describe how much viable tumor remains and the degree of treatment effect.

NCCN states that pathologic response and histologic tumor regression after neoadjuvant therapy have been shown to be predictors of survival in gastric adenocarcinoma.

This makes pathologic response clinically meaningful.

However:

> **pathologic response is not the same as cure.**

---

# 22. Pathologic Complete Response

A pathologic complete response means that no viable tumor is identified in the examined surgical specimen according to the relevant pathology assessment.

It is a favorable treatment-response finding.

It should not be translated into:

> “The cancer can never return.”

Individual outcomes remain dependent on the complete clinical context.

---

# 23. Why Lack of Major Response Does Not Automatically Mean Treatment Was Useless

Treatment response is not a binary patient-level judgment.

A tumor may:

- shrink;
- remain stable;
- show partial treatment effect;
- show substantial histologic regression;
- show little apparent response.

The interpretation depends on the complete treatment and disease context.

A patient should not be told that only dramatic shrinkage makes preoperative treatment worthwhile.

---

# 24. Neoadjuvant Therapy and Surgical Feasibility

One strategic objective of preoperative therapy may be to improve the situation for definitive local treatment.

But the package must not promise:

> “Neoadjuvant treatment will make the tumor operable.”

The correct statement is:

> In selected patients, preoperative treatment may influence tumor burden and the subsequent surgical assessment.

Surgical feasibility remains a clinical decision.

---

# 25. Why Patient Fitness Matters

Preoperative systemic treatment is itself treatment.

The patient needs to be able to tolerate it.

Relevant considerations include:

- performance status;
- nutritional status;
- organ function;
- comorbidities;
- treatment tolerance;
- postoperative expectations;
- ability to proceed to surgery.

ESMO-ASCO emphasizes treatment sequencing, multidisciplinary discussions, patient treatment planning, and management across the disease continuum.

PP-0206 therefore includes fitness conceptually without prescribing universal thresholds.

---

# 26. Nutritional Status

Gastric cancer can be associated with:

- reduced food intake;
- weight loss;
- nutritional deficiencies;
- fatigue.

Major treatment before surgery can add further physiological burden.

Therefore nutritional assessment and support may be part of treatment readiness.

However:

> nutritional support does not replace indicated anticancer treatment.

---

# 27. Why Multidisciplinary Care Is Important

Neoadjuvant treatment sits between diagnosis and surgery.

Different specialists may contribute to the decision:

- medical oncologist;
- surgeon;
- radiologist;
- pathologist;
- gastroenterologist/endoscopist;
- radiation oncologist when relevant;
- nutrition/supportive-care professionals.

NCCN strongly recommends multidisciplinary management for localized gastric cancer.

---

# 28. Neoadjuvant Therapy Is Not a Standalone Decision

The decision is connected to:

**stage**

+

**resectability**

+

**tumor biology**

+

**planned surgery**

+

**patient fitness**

+

**available evidence**

+

**previous treatment**

Therefore the package should never answer:

> “Should everyone with stage X get neoadjuvant therapy?”

without clinical context.

---

# 29. Previous Treatment Matters

A patient who has not yet received systemic treatment is different from a patient who has already completed part of a perioperative strategy.

The term “neoadjuvant” describes timing, but the treatment plan may be embedded in a larger perioperative sequence.

Therefore the system should always ask:

> **What treatment has already been given, and what treatment was originally planned around surgery?**

---

# 30. Neoadjuvant Therapy vs Adjuvant Therapy

### Neoadjuvant

Before surgery.

### Adjuvant

After surgery.

The two phases can belong to the same overall perioperative plan.

Example:

**preoperative chemotherapy**

→ surgery

→ **postoperative chemotherapy**

This is:

**perioperative therapy**

with:

- a neoadjuvant/preoperative component;
- an adjuvant/postoperative component.

# 38. What Neoadjuvant Therapy Does Not Mean

It does not automatically mean:

- the cancer is metastatic;
- surgery is impossible;
- the tumor must shrink;
- chemotherapy is mandatory;
- immunotherapy is mandatory;
- FLOT is mandatory;
- surgery will definitely occur;
- treatment will definitely cure the cancer.

---

# 39. Patient Question: “Why am I getting treatment before surgery?”

A safe explanation is:

> Your treatment team may be using treatment before surgery as part of the overall plan for your gastric cancer. Depending on your stage and tumor characteristics, treatment before surgery can treat cancer throughout the body earlier, affect the primary tumor, and provide information about how the cancer responds before the operation. The exact reason depends on your individual treatment plan.

---

# 40. Patient Question: “Does this mean my cancer is too advanced for surgery?”

Not necessarily.

Neoadjuvant treatment can be used before planned surgery in potentially resectable disease.

The fact that treatment is given first does not itself mean that the cancer cannot be removed.

---

# 41. Patient Question: “If the tumor shrinks, can I skip surgery?”

Not automatically.

Imaging may show an excellent response without proving that every viable cancer cell has disappeared.

The surgical decision depends on the full clinical assessment.

In selected MSI-H/dMMR cases with complete response to neoadjuvant immunotherapy, NCCN specifically notes uncertainty about the role of surgery.

---

# 42. Patient Question: “What if the tumor does not shrink?”

A lack of obvious shrinkage does not automatically answer the treatment question.

The team may consider:

- disease stability;
- progression;
- resectability;
- treatment effect;
- patient fitness.

The next step requires clinical reassessment.

---

# 43. Patient Question: “Does pathologic complete response mean I am cured?”

No.

It is a very favorable treatment-response finding, but it does not guarantee that cancer can never recur.

---

# 44. Patient Question: “Why do I need scans after treatment before surgery?”

The team needs to know the current extent of disease.

The reassessment can help determine whether:

- disease remains localized;
- disease has responded;
- disease has progressed;
- surgery remains appropriate.

---

# 45. Patient Question: “Why do I need another endoscopy or biopsy?”

Depending on the treatment pathway, the care team may use endoscopic reassessment and biopsy as part of response assessment.

The exact tests depend on the clinical situation.

PP-0206 does not prescribe a universal testing schedule.

---

# 46. Patient Question: “Can immunotherapy be used before surgery?”

In selected MSI-H/dMMR gastric tumors, current NCCN guidance supports neoadjuvant or perioperative immunotherapy as a category 2A option useful in certain circumstances.

This does not apply universally to all gastric cancers.

---

# 47. Patient Question: “Can chemotherapy and immunotherapy both be given before surgery?”

Some current perioperative strategies combine systemic treatment and immunotherapy in selected clinical contexts.

The exact combination depends on:

- tumor biology;
- treatment indication;
- current guideline;
- patient factors.

PP-0206 explains the strategy-level concept but does not prescribe a regimen.

---

# 48. Patient Question: “Why not just have surgery first?”

For some patients, surgery-first is appropriate.

For others, evidence and guidelines support systemic treatment around surgery.

The decision depends on the disease and treatment context.

The goal is not to delay surgery unnecessarily; it is to use the sequence that best fits the evidence-supported treatment plan.

---

# 49. Patient Question: “Does neoadjuvant therapy always make surgery easier?”

No.

Response varies between tumors.

The purpose is to integrate systemic treatment into the overall pathway, not to guarantee an easier operation.

---

# 50. Patient Question: “Can treatment before surgery be stopped?”

Treatment may sometimes be delayed, modified, or discontinued because of:

- toxicity;
- complications;
- progression;
- changes in clinical status;
- other safety considerations.

The decision is individualized.

PP-0206 does not provide dose-modification algorithms.

---

# 51. Common Misconceptions

### Myth 1
“Neoadjuvant therapy means the cancer is unresectable.”

**Fact:** It may be used before planned surgery in potentially resectable disease.

### Myth 2
“Neoadjuvant therapy always means chemotherapy.”

**Fact:** Chemotherapy is one form of preoperative therapy; selected MSI-H/dMMR tumors may have a neoadjuvant immunotherapy pathway.

### Myth 3
“FLOT and neoadjuvant therapy are the same thing.”

**Fact:** FLOT is a regimen; neoadjuvant therapy describes treatment before surgery.

### Myth 4
“Everyone with gastric cancer needs treatment before surgery.”

**Fact:** Treatment sequence depends on stage, resectability, tumor characteristics, and patient factors.

### Myth 5
“If the tumor disappears on the scan, surgery is unnecessary.”

**Fact:** Imaging response does not automatically prove eradication of all viable disease.

### Myth 6
“If the tumor does not shrink, treatment failed.”

**Fact:** Treatment response must be interpreted in the broader clinical context.

### Myth 7
“Pathologic complete response guarantees cure.”

**Fact:** It is a favorable response finding, not a guarantee of individual outcome.

### Myth 8
“Neoadjuvant therapy is just about shrinking the tumor.”

**Fact:** It may also provide earlier systemic treatment and information about treatment sensitivity.

### Myth 9
“If treatment is given before surgery, surgery is being delayed for no reason.”

**Fact:** Preoperative treatment can be an intentional part of an evidence-supported treatment strategy.

### Myth 10
“MSI-H/dMMR means every patient should receive immunotherapy before surgery.”

**Fact:** Current source-supported use is selective and should be considered in the appropriate clinical context.

# Key Messages

1. **Neoadjuvant therapy means treatment before definitive surgery.**
2. It is a treatment-timing concept, not the name of a specific drug.
3. It can be part of a curative-intent pathway for potentially resectable gastric cancer.
4. Not every gastric cancer requires treatment before surgery.
5. Accurate staging and resectability assessment are essential.
6. Perioperative therapy includes treatment before and after surgery; the preoperative part is neoadjuvant/preoperative treatment.
7. FLOT is a regimen, not a synonym for neoadjuvant therapy.
8. Current evidence supports perioperative systemic treatment in appropriate resectable gastric cancer settings.
9. Selected MSI-H/dMMR tumors may have a neoadjuvant/perioperative immunotherapy pathway.
10. Response is reassessed before definitive surgery.
11. Pathologic response can provide important prognostic information, but pathologic complete response does not guarantee cure.
12. Treatment sequence should be determined by the multidisciplinary team using disease characteristics and patient factors.

# Runtime Safety Rules

1. Do not equate neoadjuvant therapy with unresectability.
2. Do not equate neoadjuvant therapy with chemotherapy.
3. Do not equate neoadjuvant therapy with FLOT.
4. Do not tell every patient they need preoperative therapy.
5. Do not tell a patient that response guarantees cure.
6. Do not tell a patient that lack of shrinkage automatically means treatment failure.
7. Do not provide regimen-specific prescriptions from this PP.
8. Do not provide individualized treatment recommendations.
9. Do not use MSI-H/dMMR immunotherapy evidence as a universal gastric-cancer rule.
10. Do not interpret a complete response as automatic permission to omit surgery.
11. Do not replace formal response-assessment methodology with this PP.
12. Route advanced/recurrent disease questions to the appropriate downstream package.

# Boundary

## Core

Neoadjuvant/preoperative therapy as a treatment-timing and strategy concept; rationale for treatment before surgery; relationship to staging and resectability; preoperative systemic therapy at strategy level; selected MSI-H/dMMR neoadjuvant immunotherapy; relationship to response assessment and surgery; patient-facing interpretation; misconceptions; multidisciplinary decision-making.

## Supporting

MAGIC and FLOT4 as perioperative evidence with preoperative components; pathologic response; tumor regression; nutritional/functional readiness; treatment-to-surgery transition; progression/reassessment concepts; selected response-assessment context.

## Explicitly Excluded

Regimen-level chemotherapy; FLOT details; full perioperative chemotherapy; adjuvant therapy; detailed immunotherapy; biomarker testing; surgery/lymphadenectomy technique; formal RECIST/imaging methodology; detailed pathology methodology; individualized prescription; recurrent/metastatic treatment; surveillance algorithms.

## Delegated-to PP

PP-0203 Perioperative Chemotherapy; PP-0204 FLOT; PP-0205 Adjuvant Therapy; PP-0195–0201 surgical PPs; biomarker-testing PPs; PP-0191 Biomarker Testing for Immunotherapy; dedicated immunotherapy PPs; response-assessment/imaging PPs; downstream advanced/recurrent-treatment PPs.
