# Educational Objectives

After reading this Population Package, the reader should be able to:

1. Explain what hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy (HIPEC) is.
2. Understand why HIPEC is usually discussed together with cytoreductive surgery for selected gastric cancer with peritoneal disease.
3. Understand why HIPEC is not an automatic treatment for every patient with peritoneal metastases.
4. Understand the role of systemic therapy, restaging, peritoneal cancer index (PCI), absence of extraperitoneal disease, and predicted complete cytoreduction in the current selection pathway.
5. Understand that HIPEC is a local-regional treatment modality rather than a replacement for systemic therapy.
6. Understand the major evidence supporting and challenging the use of HIPEC in gastric cancer.
7. Interpret the different clinical meanings of overall survival, progression-free survival, and recurrence-related outcomes in HIPEC studies.
8. Understand why observational evidence such as CYTO-CHIP cannot be interpreted in the same way as randomized evidence.
9. Recognize that GASTRIPEC-I did not demonstrate a statistically significant overall-survival benefit despite improvements in progression-free survival and other distant-metastasis-free survival.
10. Understand why HIPEC requires multidisciplinary selection and careful discussion of expected benefit, treatment burden, and uncertainty.
11. Distinguish therapeutic HIPEC from prophylactic HIPEC and from PIPAC.
12. Recognize common misconceptions about PCI, eligibility, cure, toxicity, and the role of HIPEC.
13. Identify questions to discuss with the treating multidisciplinary team.

# Clinical Knowledge Blocks

## 1. What Is HIPEC?

Hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy (HIPEC) is a local-regional treatment in which a heated, chemotherapy-containing sterile solution is circulated through the peritoneal cavity, generally after cytoreductive surgery.

The supplied NCCN Gastric Cancer v2.2026 source describes HIPEC as continuous circulation of a heated sterile chemotherapy-containing solution throughout the peritoneal cavity following cytoreductive surgery. It explains that the approach permits high concentrations of chemotherapy to be delivered directly into the abdominal cavity, an area in which systemic treatment can face limitations related to the blood-peritoneal barrier.

The key concept is therefore:

**cytoreductive surgery removes visible disease**

followed by

**HIPEC delivers heated chemotherapy directly within the peritoneal cavity.**

HIPEC should not be reduced to the phrase “chemotherapy that is hot.” It is a treatment strategy integrated into a selected surgical pathway.

---

## 2. Why Is HIPEC Considered in Gastric Cancer?

Peritoneal metastases are clinically important in gastric cancer and can be difficult to treat effectively.

The peritoneal cavity is a distinct compartment. Systemic chemotherapy circulates through the bloodstream, whereas HIPEC is intended to expose the peritoneal surfaces directly to chemotherapy after surgical removal of visible disease.

This creates a conceptual distinction:

| Systemic therapy | HIPEC |
|---|---|
| Delivered through the bloodstream | Delivered directly into the peritoneal cavity |
| Treats disease throughout the body | Focuses on the peritoneal compartment |
| Important for systemic microscopic/metastatic disease | Used as a local-regional component |
| Can be given repeatedly as systemic treatment | Usually linked to a major operative treatment |
| Does not require cytoreductive surgery | Commonly follows cytoreductive surgery in the selected pathway |

These approaches should not be presented as interchangeable.

---

## 3. HIPEC Is Usually Considered With Cytoreductive Surgery

A central principle of the package is:

> HIPEC is generally considered as part of a cytoreductive strategy rather than as an isolated replacement for surgery or systemic therapy.

Cytoreductive surgery aims to remove visible peritoneal tumor deposits.

HIPEC then exposes the peritoneal cavity to heated chemotherapy.

The current NCCN pathway links IC/HIPEC with cytoreductive surgery in selected patients who are candidates for complete cytoreduction.

Therefore:

**Peritoneal disease**

→ **systemic therapy**

→ **restaging**

→ **selection**

→ **cytoreductive surgery + HIPEC in selected patients**

This is a pathway concept, not an individualized treatment prescription.

---

## 4. Why Complete Cytoreduction Matters

The current NCCN framework specifically identifies patients likely to undergo complete cytoreduction as candidates for the HIPEC option in the low-PCI setting.

Complete cytoreduction means that the surgical team expects to remove all visible peritoneal tumor nodules/plaques.

This matters because HIPEC is not intended to compensate automatically for large amounts of visible tumor that cannot be removed.

A useful conceptual distinction is:

- **Visible disease** → addressed primarily by cytoreductive surgery.
- **Residual microscopic/peritoneal disease** → the setting in which intraperitoneal chemotherapy is intended to contribute.

Therefore:

> A patient should not interpret a favorable PCI value alone as proof that HIPEC is appropriate.

---

## 5. Why Systemic Therapy Comes Before Selected HIPEC Consideration

The NCCN v2.2026 pathway states that selected patients with documented peritoneal metastatic disease should receive systemic therapy for a minimum of 3 months, followed by restaging before further therapy is considered.

This means that the HIPEC decision is dynamic.

The clinical sequence is approximately:

**Documented peritoneal disease**

↓

**pretreatment workup**

↓

**systemic therapy**

↓

**restaging**

↓

**disease remains stable or improves**

↓

**reassess peritoneal burden**

↓

**assess extraperitoneal disease**

↓

**assess feasibility of complete cytoreduction**

↓

**multidisciplinary decision**

↓

**selected HIPEC pathway**

The exact systemic regimen belongs to systemic-treatment packages and is intentionally not reproduced here.

---

## 6. What Is PCI?

PCI stands for **Peritoneal Cancer Index**.

It is a measure used to describe the burden and distribution of peritoneal disease.

For PP-0226, PCI is important because it helps the multidisciplinary team judge whether a patient has a limited enough peritoneal disease burden for a potential cytoreductive strategy.

The current NCCN v2.2026 guidance identifies:

- **PCI ≤10** as a setting in which selected patients who are candidates for complete cytoreduction may have IC/HIPEC as an option.
- **PCI >10** as a setting in which IC/HIPEC may be considered in a clinical trial.

This is a selection framework, not a universal eligibility calculator.

Detailed PCI scoring methodology is outside PP-0226.

---

## 7. PCI ≤10 Does Not Mean “You Qualify for HIPEC”

This is a mandatory safety message.

The pathway requires more than a number.

The current NCCN framework combines low PCI with:

- improved or stable disease;
- no metastatic progression;
- no extraperitoneal disease;
- predicted complete cytoreduction;
- multidisciplinary assessment.

Therefore:

**PCI ≤10**

does not mean:

**automatic HIPEC.**

Instead:

**PCI ≤10**

+

**controlled disease**

+

**appropriate distribution**

+

**no relevant extraperitoneal disease**

+

**complete cytoreduction feasible**

+

**patient fitness and multidisciplinary judgment**

→ **HIPEC may be considered in selected circumstances.**

---

## 8. Why Restaging Matters

HIPEC selection should not be based only on the initial presentation.

Disease can progress during systemic treatment.

A patient may therefore move between pathways after restaging.

The current NCCN algorithm explicitly incorporates treatment-response assessment before the specialized peritoneal-only treatment pathway.

Detailed response methodology is delegated to PP-0217 and PP-0218.

PP-0226 only needs to preserve the principle:

> HIPEC consideration occurs after the disease has been reassessed and the clinical team has determined that the disease remains appropriate for a local-regional strategy.

---

## 9. Why Absence of Extraperitoneal Disease Matters

HIPEC targets the peritoneal compartment.

If a patient has uncontrolled metastatic disease outside the peritoneum, treating the peritoneal compartment alone may not address the dominant systemic disease burden.

The NCCN pathway therefore identifies absence of extraperitoneal disease as part of the selected pathway.

This is one reason why HIPEC should not be described as a general treatment for all stage IV gastric cancer.

---

## 10. Why a Multidisciplinary Team Is Essential

HIPEC selection requires integration of several clinical questions:

- Is the disease truly confined to the relevant peritoneal compartment?
- Has the disease responded to systemic treatment?
- What is the PCI?
- Is complete cytoreduction realistically achievable?
- Is the patient fit enough for major surgery?
- What is the expected morbidity?
- What is the quality-of-life impact?
- What does the current evidence actually show?
- What are the patient's goals?

The current NCCN guideline explicitly recommends multidisciplinary discussion and tumor-board decision-making for IC/HIPEC.

PP-0226 owns the principle and rationale.

PP-0232 owns the broader multidisciplinary-management framework.

---

## 11. What Does the HIPEC Procedure Conceptually Involve?

At a high level:

1. The patient undergoes an operative assessment and selected cytoreductive surgery.
2. Visible peritoneal disease is surgically removed as completely as possible.
3. A heated chemotherapy-containing solution is circulated through the peritoneal cavity.
4. The treatment is then completed and the patient enters postoperative recovery.

This package intentionally stops at the conceptual level.

It does not provide a technical surgical protocol.

---

## 12. Why Is the Chemotherapy Heated?

The term “hyperthermic” means that the intraperitoneal chemotherapy solution is heated.

The project sources support the procedural definition and the rationale for direct intraperitoneal exposure.

The package should not overstate this into a universal mechanistic promise such as “heat always makes chemotherapy substantially more effective.”

The clinically safe statement is:

> HIPEC combines direct intraperitoneal chemotherapy delivery with hyperthermia as part of a local-regional treatment strategy.

Detailed pharmacologic and technical mechanisms are outside the approved scope.

---

## 13. HIPEC Is Not the Same as PIPAC

PIPAC stands for **pressurized intraperitoneal aerosolized chemotherapy**.

It is a different intraperitoneal treatment approach.

NCCN v2.2026 distinguishes the two:

- HIPEC uses a heated chemotherapy-containing solution circulated through the peritoneal cavity.
- PIPAC introduces chemotherapy into the abdomen as an aerosol.

The supplied NCCN source states that PIPAC is investigational and should only be performed in the context of a clinical trial.

Therefore:

**PIPAC ≠ HIPEC.**

PIPAC is included here only to prevent conceptual confusion.

---

## 14. What Does the Current NCCN Position Mean?

The current NCCN v2.2026 position is deliberately selective.

It does not say:

> “HIPEC should be used for every patient with peritoneal metastases.”

It states that, because evidence remains limited, IC/HIPEC should be pursued only in selected circumstances and after multidisciplinary discussion.

For certain patients with PCI ≤10 who are candidates for complete cytoreduction, IC/HIPEC with cytoreductive surgery is an option.

For certain patients with PCI >10, IC/HIPEC may be considered in a clinical trial.

This is a **selected-option** position, not a universal-standard position.

# Evidence Knowledge Blocks

## 15. CYTO-CHIP

The NCCN v2.2026 evidence discussion cites CYTO-CHIP as an important comparative study.

The study included:

- 277 patients with gastric-cancer peritoneal metastases;
- 180 received cytoreductive surgery plus HIPEC;
- 97 received cytoreductive surgery alone.

NCCN reports that adding HIPEC was associated with improved overall survival and recurrence-free survival without increased morbidity or mortality in the analysis.

However, the median PCI remained higher in the HIPEC group following treatment.

The NCCN conclusion is appropriately cautious:

> cytoreductive surgery with HIPEC may be effective for strictly selected patients with limited peritoneal metastases.

### Evidence interpretation

CYTO-CHIP is important evidence, but it is not equivalent to a randomized trial.

A propensity-score analysis can reduce measured baseline differences but cannot eliminate every possible unmeasured confounder.

Therefore:

**CYTO-CHIP supports potential benefit in selected patients.**

It does not prove:

**HIPEC benefits every patient with gastric-cancer peritoneal metastases.**

---

## 16. Badgwell Phase II Study

The NCCN source describes a phase II study of 20 patients with gastric adenocarcinoma and positive peritoneal cytology or carcinomatosis.

Patients had completed systemic chemotherapy and underwent laparoscopic HIPEC followed by cytoreduction, gastrectomy, and HIPEC as described in the guideline evidence summary.

Reported outcomes included:

- 90-day morbidity: **70%**
- 90-day mortality: **0%**
- median OS from cytoreduction/gastrectomy/HIPEC: **16.1 months**
- 1-year OS from diagnosis of metastatic disease: **90%**
- 2-year OS: **50%**
- 3-year OS: **28%**

These figures are study-specific.

They should not be interpreted as universal HIPEC complication rates or expected survival for an individual patient.

The high morbidity figure also demonstrates why HIPEC must be understood as a major treatment strategy rather than a minor add-on procedure.

---

## 17. Yang Randomized Phase III Trial

The NCCN v2.2026 source describes a phase III randomized trial involving 68 patients with gastric cancer and peritoneal carcinomatosis.

Patients received:

- cytoreductive surgery alone, or
- cytoreductive surgery plus HIPEC.

At a median follow-up of 32 months:

- death occurred in 97% of the surgery-alone group;
- death occurred in 85% of the surgery-plus-HIPEC group;
- median survival was **6.5 months vs 11 months**;
- **P = .046**.

Serious adverse events occurred in:

- **11.7%** of the surgery-alone group;
- **14.7%** of the HIPEC group;
- **P = .839**.

The NCCN source also notes that multivariate analysis found addition of HIPEC to cytoreductive surgery to be an independent predictor of better survival.

### Interpretation

This randomized evidence provides an important survival signal.

However, it is a relatively small trial and comes from an earlier treatment era.

Results from older studies must therefore be interpreted alongside modern systemic treatment and contemporary randomized evidence.

---

## 18. GASTRIPEC-I

GASTRIPEC-I is particularly important because it prevents an overly positive interpretation of HIPEC.

The NCCN v2.2026 source describes this phase III trial as comparing cytoreductive surgery with and without HIPEC after preoperative chemotherapy in patients with gastric or EGJ cancers and peritoneal metastasis.

### Overall survival

No statistically significant difference was observed:

**HR 0.72; P = .1647**

### Progression-free survival

Improved with HIPEC:

**7.1 vs 3.5 months; P = .0472**

### Other distant metastasis-free survival

Improved:

**10.2 vs 9.2 months; P = .0286**

The guideline also notes that some patients did not proceed to further treatment and complete cytoreduction after preoperative chemotherapy because of disease progression and other causes, and almost half had PCI ≥7.

### Clinical meaning

GASTRIPEC-I demonstrates why endpoints matter.

A treatment can improve some disease-control outcomes without demonstrating a statistically significant overall-survival advantage.

Therefore:

> “HIPEC improves PFS in this trial” is not the same claim as “HIPEC improves OS.”

Both statements must remain separate.

---

## 19. PERISCOPE II

PERISCOPE II is an ongoing Dutch study investigating:

- gastrectomy;
- cytoreductive surgery;
- HIPEC;

versus

- systemic treatment

in resectable cT3–cT4 gastric cancer with limited peritoneal carcinoma.

The primary endpoint is OS.

Secondary endpoints include:

- PFS;
- toxicity;
- quality of life.

The study is therefore part of the continuing evidence-generation process.

It should not be presented as evidence of established benefit because its definitive results are not yet available in the supplied NCCN material.

---

## 20. What the Evidence Says as a Whole

The evidence should be understood as a progression:

### Earlier studies

→ feasibility and potential survival signal.

### CYTO-CHIP

→ favorable comparative association in a large propensity-score analysis.

### Yang randomized trial

→ survival signal in a small randomized population.

### GASTRIPEC-I

→ no statistically significant OS improvement, despite PFS and other distant-metastasis-free survival improvements.

### PERISCOPE II

→ ongoing attempt to clarify the role of HIPEC in a contemporary treatment setting.

Therefore:

> The current evidence is supportive enough to justify selected use, but not strong enough to justify universal use.

This is consistent with the current NCCN recommendation.

# Safety and Treatment-Burden Knowledge Blocks

## 21. HIPEC Is a Major Treatment

HIPEC is generally integrated with major abdominal surgery.

The overall burden can therefore reflect:

- cytoreductive surgery;
- gastrectomy when performed;
- intraperitoneal chemotherapy;
- prolonged operative treatment;
- postoperative recovery.

A complication occurring after CRS + HIPEC cannot automatically be labeled as a chemotherapy-specific HIPEC toxicity.

This distinction is essential for interpreting studies.

---

## 22. Morbidity Must Be Interpreted in Context

The Badgwell phase II study reported 70% 90-day morbidity in 20 patients.

That number is clinically important but should not be presented as:

> “HIPEC has a 70% complication rate.”

The correct interpretation is:

> In that small phase II cohort undergoing cytoreduction, gastrectomy and HIPEC, 70% experienced morbidity within 90 days.

The study population, procedure combination, follow-up window, and sample size all matter.

---

## 23. Mortality Is Also Study-Specific

The same phase II study reported 0% 90-day mortality.

This does not mean HIPEC has zero mortality risk.

It means:

> No 90-day deaths were observed in that particular 20-patient study cohort.

Patients should therefore avoid translating a single study's rate into an individualized prediction.

---

## 24. Quality of Life Matters

HIPEC can only be understood properly by considering more than tumor outcomes.

Important patient-centered endpoints include:

- recovery time;
- postoperative burden;
- complications;
- nutritional consequences;
- functional recovery;
- quality of life;
- ability to receive subsequent treatment.

PERISCOPE II explicitly includes quality of life among its secondary endpoints.

This reinforces the principle that survival outcomes are not the only clinically meaningful outcomes.

# Evidence Maturity

## 25. Established / Guideline-Supported

The supplied evidence supports:

- HIPEC as a defined intraperitoneal treatment modality.
- HIPEC being used in conjunction with cytoreductive surgery.
- Selected use after appropriate pretreatment evaluation.
- Multidisciplinary discussion.
- The importance of PCI and complete-cytoreduction feasibility.
- Selected patients with PCI ≤10 being potential candidates.
- PCI >10 being a clinical-trial context in the current NCCN pathway.
- PIPAC being investigational.
- Prophylactic IC/HIPEC/PIPAC in non-metastatic disease being investigational.

---

## 26. Context-Dependent

These require patient and disease context:

- whether cytoreduction is technically feasible;
- whether complete cytoreduction is likely;
- whether systemic therapy has controlled disease;
- whether there is clinically relevant extraperitoneal disease;
- whether expected treatment burden is acceptable;
- whether the patient is medically fit;
- whether a specialized experienced center is available.

---

## 27. Emerging / Investigational

The evidence remains evolving for:

- the magnitude of OS benefit from HIPEC;
- optimal patient selection beyond broad guideline criteria;
- contemporary integration with modern systemic treatment;
- broader use in higher-PCI disease;
- prophylactic HIPEC;
- PIPAC.

---

## 28. Not Established / Excluded

The supplied sources do not support:

- universal HIPEC for all peritoneal metastases;
- guaranteed survival benefit;
- guaranteed cure;
- PCI alone as a sufficient eligibility rule;
- HIPEC as a replacement for systemic therapy;
- prophylactic HIPEC as routine standard care;
- PIPAC as established routine care.

# Patient Explanation

## 29. A Simple Explanation

If cancer has spread to the lining of the abdomen, doctors may sometimes consider a specialized treatment called HIPEC.

First, surgery may be used to remove visible tumor deposits.

Then a heated chemotherapy solution is circulated inside the abdominal cavity.

The goal is to give chemotherapy directly to the area where the peritoneal cancer has been located.

But this approach is not suitable for everyone.

Doctors consider how much disease is present, whether the disease has remained controlled after systemic treatment, whether cancer is present elsewhere, whether all visible disease can probably be removed, and whether the patient can safely undergo major treatment.

---

## 30. What a Patient Should Not Infer

A patient should not infer:

- “My PCI is below 10, so I definitely qualify.”
- “HIPEC will cure my cancer.”
- “HIPEC means I no longer need systemic therapy.”
- “A HIPEC study showed benefit, so I will receive the same benefit.”
- “All complications after surgery are caused by HIPEC.”
- “PIPAC is another name for HIPEC.”

Each of these interpretations is too simplistic.

# Common Misconceptions

## Myth 1

**“HIPEC is simply heated chemotherapy.”**

**Fact:**  
It is a local-regional treatment strategy integrated with cytoreductive surgery in selected patients.

## Myth 2

**“Everyone with peritoneal metastases should receive HIPEC.”**

**Fact:**  
The current guideline supports selected use only.

## Myth 3

**“PCI ≤10 guarantees HIPEC.”**

**Fact:**  
PCI is one selection factor among several.

## Myth 4

**“HIPEC replaces systemic chemotherapy.”**

**Fact:**  
The current pathway uses systemic therapy before restaging and possible HIPEC selection.

## Myth 5

**“HIPEC always improves overall survival.”**

**Fact:**  
Evidence is mixed. GASTRIPEC-I did not show a statistically significant OS difference.

## Myth 6

**“CYTO-CHIP proved HIPEC works for everyone.”**

**Fact:**  
CYTO-CHIP was a propensity-score analysis in a selected population.

## Myth 7

**“A 70% morbidity rate means every HIPEC patient has a 70% chance of complications.”**

**Fact:**  
The 70% figure came from a specific 20-patient phase II cohort and should not be generalized.

## Myth 8

**“If the patient has PCI ≤10, visible disease can be left behind because HIPEC will treat it.”**

**Fact:**  
The current pathway specifically links the option to predicted complete cytoreduction.

## Myth 9

**“PIPAC and HIPEC are the same.”**

**Fact:**  
They are different intraperitoneal treatment approaches. PIPAC is investigational in the supplied NCCN framework.

## Myth 10

**“HIPEC is either standard treatment or useless.”**

**Fact:**  
The evidence is more nuanced: selected use is supported, but the magnitude and circumstances of benefit remain an active evidence question.

# Key Messages

1. HIPEC is a specialized intraperitoneal treatment modality.
2. It is usually considered together with cytoreductive surgery.
3. It is not an automatic treatment for peritoneal metastases.
4. Systemic therapy and restaging are important parts of the selected pathway.
5. PCI helps describe peritoneal disease burden but is not a standalone eligibility rule.
6. The current NCCN pathway gives a selected option for certain patients with PCI ≤10 who are candidates for complete cytoreduction.
7. Higher PCI moves HIPEC consideration toward a clinical-trial setting.
8. Absence of relevant extraperitoneal disease and controlled disease are important selection concepts.
9. Evidence includes favorable observational data and randomized studies with mixed results.
10. GASTRIPEC-I did not show a statistically significant OS benefit.
11. HIPEC carries substantial treatment burden because it is linked to major surgery.
12. Patient goals, quality of life, fitness, and uncertainty must be part of the decision.
13. Prophylactic HIPEC and PIPAC should not be treated as routine established therapy.

# Patient Questions

Before discussing HIPEC with the treatment team, useful questions include:

1. Is my peritoneal disease the only metastatic site?
2. What is my current PCI?
3. Has the disease remained stable or improved after systemic treatment?
4. Is complete cytoreduction realistically possible?
5. What parts of the planned operation are included?
6. What evidence supports HIPEC in patients with my disease characteristics?
7. What are the expected benefits and what are the uncertainties?
8. What are the major short-term risks of the combined surgery and HIPEC?
9. How might treatment affect nutrition, recovery, and quality of life?
10. Would treatment be performed as routine selected care or within a clinical trial?
11. What alternatives exist if HIPEC is not chosen?
12. What would happen if the disease progresses during treatment or surgery?
13. Which parts of my treatment are systemic and which are local-regional?
14. Is PIPAC being discussed, and if so, is it only within a clinical trial?
15. How will follow-up and recurrence surveillance be handled?

# Clinical Safety Boundary

This package provides a governed educational explanation of HIPEC.

It does not determine whether an individual patient should receive HIPEC.

Individual treatment decisions require assessment of:

- disease distribution;
- pathology;
- imaging;
- PCI;
- treatment response;
- extraperitoneal disease;
- predicted cytoreduction;
- medical fitness;
- operative feasibility;
- expected risks and benefits;
- patient goals;
- local expertise;
- clinical-trial availability.

No single factor, including PCI, should be used in isolation to determine treatment eligibility.
