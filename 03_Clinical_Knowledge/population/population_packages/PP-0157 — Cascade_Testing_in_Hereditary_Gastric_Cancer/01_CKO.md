# Educational Question

> **What is cascade genetic testing in hereditary gastric cancer, who may be offered testing after a familial pathogenic variant is identified, how does testing move through a family, and what factors can affect whether relatives complete testing?**

---

# Educational Objective

Help patients and families understand cascade genetic testing as a **family-level testing pathway** that begins after a familial pathogenic variant has been identified.

The package explains:

- who may be at risk;
- how relatives may be informed;
- how relatives enter counseling/testing;
- what is tested;
- what positive and negative results mean for the cascade;
- how testing can extend across generations;
- why communication, emotional, access and family barriers can affect uptake.

# Knowledge Block 1 — What Is Cascade Genetic Testing?

Cascade genetic testing is the process of offering genetic testing to biological relatives who may have inherited a **familial pathogenic variant already identified in a relative**.

When another relative is found to carry the familial pathogenic variant, that person's at-risk biological relatives may then be offered testing.

This creates a family-level testing pathway:

**Known familial pathogenic variant**

→ **at-risk relatives**

→ **familial-variant testing**

→ **additional carriers**

→ **their at-risk relatives**

---

# Knowledge Block 2 — When Does Cascade Testing Begin?

Cascade testing is distinct from the initial search for a hereditary cancer syndrome.

The usual starting point is:

> **A pathogenic germline variant has already been identified in a family.**

Before that point, the clinical pathway may involve:

- family-history assessment;
- hereditary-risk assessment;
- genetic counseling;
- syndrome-specific testing criteria;
- initial genetic testing.

These are handled by upstream Population Packages.

---

# Knowledge Block 3 — Who Are the Relatives at Risk?

The relevant group is **biological family members who may have inherited the familial pathogenic variant**.

The degree of biological relatedness helps determine who may be considered at risk.

In practice, close biological relatives are often an important initial focus, and the process can extend through additional generations as carriers are identified.

Exact syndrome-specific testing criteria are not reproduced in this package.

---

# Knowledge Block 4 — What Is the Role of the Proband?

The proband or index patient is the person through whom the familial pathogenic variant was first identified for the family testing pathway.

The proband may help initiate cascade testing by:

- sharing the result with relatives;
- encouraging relatives to seek genetic counseling;
- providing relatives with relevant information;
- using family letters or educational materials when available.

Other communication models may also be used depending on the clinical setting.

---

# Knowledge Block 5 — How Does Genetic Information Reach Relatives?

Communication is a central part of cascade testing.

Possible approaches include:

- discussion between the patient and relatives;
- written family letters;
- educational materials;
- referral/contact information;
- support from genetics professionals;
- facilitated communication or direct provider-to-family approaches in selected settings.

There is no single communication model that applies universally.

---

# Knowledge Block 6 — Does Every Relative Need the Same Genetic Test?

When a familial pathogenic variant is already known, cascade testing generally focuses on determining whether the relative carries **that known familial variant**.

This is different from the broader genetic testing used when a hereditary cause has not yet been established.

Detailed laboratory testing methodology is outside PP-0157.

---

# Knowledge Block 7 — What Happens If a Relative Tests Positive?

A positive result for the known familial pathogenic variant identifies the relative as a carrier of that familial variant.

That person may then enter the appropriate hereditary-cancer risk-management pathway.

The person may also become a new node in the cascade:

**Carrier**

→ **their at-risk biological relatives**

→ **familial-variant testing**

The specific syndrome, gene interpretation, surveillance and risk-reduction pathway belongs to downstream packages.

---

# Knowledge Block 8 — What Happens If a Relative Tests Negative?

If a relative does not carry the known familial pathogenic variant, that person does not become a carrier node for that specific familial variant.

Therefore, that person generally does not continue the cascade for that known familial variant.

However:

> **A negative result for a known familial variant should not automatically be interpreted as zero cancer risk from every possible cause.**

Other personal or family risk factors may require separate assessment.

---

# Knowledge Block 9 — How Does Cascade Testing Continue Across Generations?

Cascade testing can extend through several generations.

For example:

**Index patient**

↓

**Sibling / parent / child**

↓

**Carrier identified**

↓

**That carrier's at-risk biological relatives**

↓

**Additional carrier identified**

↓

**Next generation**

This repeated process is the reason it is called a **cascade**.

---

# Knowledge Block 10 — Why Do Some Relatives Not Complete Testing?

Cascade testing can be interrupted by:

- loss of contact information;
- weak or distant family relationships;
- anxiety or fear;
- guilt;
- concern about causing distress;
- difficulty understanding genetic information;
- family conflict;
- financial or access barriers;
- uncertainty about whether testing is worthwhile.

Therefore, identifying a familial pathogenic variant does not automatically mean that every at-risk relative will be tested.

---

# Knowledge Block 11 — Why Does Information Comprehension Matter?

Relatives need enough understandable information to make an informed decision about whether to seek counseling and testing.

Information may be difficult to understand, especially when:

- the genetic terminology is unfamiliar;
- family relationships are complex;
- the result is emotionally distressing;
- the relative has limited health literacy or communication difficulties.

Clear communication and access to appropriate genetic counseling can help.

---

# Knowledge Block 12 — What Is the Role of Genetic Counseling?

Genetic counseling remains important for relatives entering cascade testing.

It can help them understand:

- why they may be at risk;
- what the familial variant means at a high level;
- what testing involves;
- possible outcomes;
- medical and family implications;
- privacy and psychosocial considerations.

However:

> **PP-0156 owns the genetic-counseling process; PP-0157 owns the cascade-testing pathway.**

---

# Knowledge Block 13 — What About Privacy and Family Communication?

Genetic information can have implications for biological relatives while remaining sensitive personal health information.

Cascade testing therefore involves balancing:

- family notification;
- informed decision-making;
- privacy;
- confidentiality;
- local legal/ethical requirements.

The exact legal duties concerning disclosure to relatives vary by jurisdiction and are not defined by this package.

---

# Knowledge Block 14 — What Happens After a Positive Cascade Test?

A confirmed familial pathogenic variant may create an opportunity for:

- hereditary-risk management;
- cancer surveillance;
- risk reduction;
- further family testing.

The appropriate next step depends on the specific hereditary syndrome and gene.

PP-0157 ends at the **handoff** to those downstream pathways.

---

# Knowledge Block 15 — Does Cascade Testing Prevent Cancer?

Cascade testing does not directly prevent cancer.

Its value is that it can identify people who carry a familial pathogenic variant **before cancer develops**, creating an opportunity for appropriate prevention, early detection or risk-reduction measures.

The downstream management itself is not part of PP-0157.

---

# Knowledge Block 16 — Does Cascade Testing Work the Same Way Everywhere?

No.

Cascade-testing protocols and delivery models can vary internationally.

Differences may involve:

- who contacts relatives;
- whether written letters are used;
- whether genetics professionals contact relatives directly;
- how counseling is delivered;
- access to testing;
- privacy and legal requirements.

The underlying clinical principle remains:

> **Identify at-risk relatives and offer appropriate testing for the known familial pathogenic variant.**

---

# Common Misconceptions

## Myth 1
**Cascade testing means testing the whole family for every cancer gene.**

**Fact:** When a familial pathogenic variant is already known, cascade testing generally focuses on that known familial variant.

## Myth 2
**Cascade testing begins as soon as someone has a family history of gastric cancer.**

**Fact:** Cascade testing normally follows identification of a familial pathogenic variant.

## Myth 3
**If a relative tests negative, that relative has zero cancer risk.**

**Fact:** A negative result means the known familial variant was not identified; other risk factors may still exist.

## Myth 4
**One person testing positive means every relative will eventually test.**

**Fact:** Communication, emotional, family and access barriers can reduce cascade uptake.

## Myth 5
**Cascade testing is the same as genetic counseling.**

**Fact:** Counseling supports informed decisions; cascade testing is the family-level testing pathway.

---

# Key Messages

- Cascade testing begins after a familial pathogenic variant has been identified.
- It focuses on biological relatives who may have inherited that variant.
- Family communication is central to successful cascade testing.
- Testing generally focuses on the known familial pathogenic variant.
- A positive relative can become a new starting point for the next cascade.
- A negative relative does not continue the cascade for that known familial variant.
- Cascade testing can extend across generations.
- Communication, emotional, comprehension and access barriers can reduce uptake.
- Genetic counseling remains important but belongs to PP-0156.
- Positive carriers may need downstream hereditary-risk management, surveillance or risk reduction.
