## Educational Objectives

After reading this Population Package, the reader should be able to:

1. Explain why gastric-cancer screening is not completely risk-free.
2. Distinguish a screening result from a cancer diagnosis.
3. Explain false-positive and false-negative results.
4. Understand why a positive result can lead to additional testing even when cancer is not confirmed.
5. Understand why a negative result cannot guarantee that cancer is absent.
6. Understand positive predictive value at a patient-facing level.
7. Recognize important physical harms associated with screening procedures.
8. Understand the radiation and premedication issues associated with some screening approaches.
9. Understand overdiagnosis and how it can lead to overtreatment.
10. Understand why screening harms and benefits depend on population and setting.

# Clinical Knowledge Blocks

## Knowledge Block 1 — Screening Can Help, but It Is Not Risk-Free

### Patient Explanation

Screening looks for cancer before symptoms appear. The goal is to find important disease earlier, but screening itself can also create problems.

A person may receive a result that needs further evaluation, undergo a procedure that carries a small risk, or have an abnormality detected that would never have caused important harm.

### Clinical Importance

The value of screening depends on both its possible benefits and its possible harms.

### Key Concepts

- Benefit–harm balance
- Screening limitation
- False results
- Procedure-related harm

## Knowledge Block 2 — What Is a False-Positive Result?

### Patient Explanation

A false-positive result occurs when a screening test suggests that cancer or another important abnormality may be present, but cancer is not ultimately confirmed.

A positive screening result is therefore not the same as a diagnosis.

### Clinical Importance

False positives are identified by the NCI as the most frequent reported harm of routine gastric-cancer screening.

### Key Concepts

- Positive screening result
- False positive
- Additional evaluation
- Not a diagnosis

## Knowledge Block 3 — What Is a False-Negative Result?

### Patient Explanation

A false-negative result occurs when the screening test does not identify cancer that is actually present.

A negative result can be reassuring, but it cannot make cancer impossible.

### Clinical Importance

No screening method detects every cancer. A later cancer diagnosis after a negative screen does not necessarily mean that the earlier screening process was inappropriate.

### Key Concepts

- False negative
- Missed cancer
- False reassurance
- Screening limitation

## Knowledge Block 4 — Why Is a Positive Screening Result Not a Diagnosis?

### Patient Explanation

Screening is designed to identify people who may need further evaluation. It is not the same as diagnostic testing.

If a screening result is abnormal, additional assessment may be needed to determine whether cancer is actually present.

### Clinical Importance

This distinction prevents unnecessary fear and preserves the boundary with PP-0175, which owns the diagnostic work-up.

### Key Concepts

- Screening
- Diagnosis
- Confirmation
- Downstream work-up

## Knowledge Block 5 — What Is Positive Predictive Value?

### Patient Explanation

Positive predictive value, or PPV, describes how often a positive screening result actually represents the disease being looked for in a particular population and testing setting.

PPV is not a universal property of a test. It depends partly on the population being screened.

### Clinical Importance

A screening program may generate many positive results even though relatively few people are ultimately found to have gastric cancer.

### Key Concepts

- PPV
- Population context
- Screening yield

## Knowledge Block 6 — What Does the Japanese Screening Example Show?

### Patient Explanation

The NCI describes a Japanese screening program involving 17,647 men aged 40 to 60 years. Combined serum pepsinogen and barium digital radiography had a PPV of 0.85% over seven years. The cancer detection rate was 0.28%.

This means that a positive screening pathway did not usually translate into detection of gastric cancer.

### Clinical Importance

This is a study-specific example, not a universal PPV for gastric-cancer screening.

### Key Concepts

- Study-specific PPV
- Cancer detection
- Screening burden

## Knowledge Block 7 — Why Can False Positives Cause Harm?

### Patient Explanation

A positive screening result may lead to repeat testing, endoscopy, biopsy, or other evaluation.

Even when cancer is not ultimately confirmed, the person may experience:

- worry and uncertainty;
- additional visits;
- invasive procedures;
- procedure-related risks;
- time and resource burden.

### Clinical Importance

The harm is not limited to the initial test. It can extend through the downstream consequences of an abnormal result.

### Key Concepts

- Downstream testing
- Uncertainty
- Procedure burden

## Knowledge Block 8 — Can a Negative Result Miss Cancer?

### Patient Explanation

Yes. A screening test can fail to identify a cancer that is present.

This is why a negative screening result should not be described as proof that cancer is absent.

### Clinical Importance

The package deliberately avoids universal false-negative rates because the supplied evidence does not establish one number that applies to every modality and population.

### Key Concepts

- False negative
- Detection limits
- Interval cancer

## Knowledge Block 9 — What Is an Interval or Missed Cancer?

### Patient Explanation

A cancer may sometimes be diagnosed after a negative screening test and before another screening episode would have occurred.

This can happen because screening does not detect every cancer.

### Clinical Importance

The concept is useful for patient understanding, but PP-0174 does not assign universal interval-cancer rates.

### Key Concepts

- Interval cancer
- Missed cancer
- Screening interval

## Knowledge Block 10 — What Harms Can Occur With Endoscopic Screening?

### Patient Explanation

Endoscopy is commonly used to examine the stomach, but it is a procedure and is not completely risk-free.

The NCI describes uncommon but serious complications that may include:

- perforation;
- cardiopulmonary events;
- aspiration pneumonia;
- bleeding requiring hospitalization.

### Clinical Importance

These are potential harms rather than expected outcomes for every patient.

The NCI also notes that complications such as bleeding and perforation can vary with the experience of the screening center.

### Key Concepts

- Procedure-related harm
- Perforation
- Bleeding
- Screening-center experience

## Knowledge Block 11 — What Are the Radiation-Related Concerns With Photofluorography?

### Patient Explanation

Barium-meal photofluorography uses X-rays and therefore exposes a person to a low dose of radiation.

The NCI describes an exposure of about 0.6 mSv and characterizes the possible carcinogenesis risk as theoretical and poorly quantified.

### Clinical Importance

The evidence does not support describing this as either completely harmless or as a proven cause of cancer.

### Key Concepts

- Radiation exposure
- Low dose
- Theoretical risk
- Uncertainty

## Knowledge Block 12 — Can Premedication Cause Problems?

### Patient Explanation

Some screening procedures use medicines before the examination. These medicines can themselves cause adverse effects.

### Clinical Importance

Premedication is one of the additional categories of harm identified by the NCI. Detailed medication safety is outside this package.

### Key Concepts

- Premedication
- Adverse effects
- Procedure preparation

## Knowledge Block 13 — What Is Overdiagnosis?

### Patient Explanation

Overdiagnosis means that screening detects a disease or abnormality that would not have caused important health problems during a person's lifetime.

The problem is not that the finding is necessarily false. The problem is that finding it may not improve the person's health.

### Clinical Importance

Overdiagnosis is a distinct screening harm and should not be confused with a false-positive result.

### Key Concepts

- Overdiagnosis
- Clinically important disease
- Screening harm

## Knowledge Block 14 — How Can Overdiagnosis Lead to Overtreatment?

### Patient Explanation

Once an abnormality is found, it may be treated even when it would never have caused important harm.

That treatment can expose a person to risks without a corresponding benefit.

### Clinical Importance

The NCI specifically identifies overdiagnosis with attendant overtreatment as a possible harm of gastric-cancer screening.

### Key Concepts

- Overtreatment
- Treatment burden
- Benefit–harm balance

## Knowledge Block 15 — Does Screening Safety Depend on Where It Is Performed?

### Patient Explanation

Yes. Some procedural risks can vary according to the experience of the screening center.

### Clinical Importance

This is important when comparing screening programs across different settings. A complication rate from one highly experienced program should not automatically be assumed to apply everywhere.

### Key Concepts

- Screening center
- Operator/center experience
- Program quality

## Knowledge Block 16 — Why Can Harms Matter Differently in Different Populations?

### Patient Explanation

The chance of finding cancer is not the same in every population.

In a population with lower gastric-cancer incidence, fewer screened people will have cancer. The potential benefit may therefore be smaller, while false-positive results and procedure-related burdens can still occur.

### Clinical Importance

The NCI notes that evidence from high-risk areas may not apply to low-risk populations and that some harms may be higher in low-risk populations than in Japanese mass-screening programs.

### Key Concepts

- Incidence
- Generalizability
- Population context
- Benefit–harm balance

## Knowledge Block 17 — Why Is the Benefit–Harm Balance Important?

### Patient Explanation

Screening should be considered as an intervention with both possible benefits and possible harms.

Potential benefits include earlier detection. Potential harms include false-positive results, false-negative results, procedures, radiation exposure, overdiagnosis, and overtreatment.

### Clinical Importance

The balance depends on the disease frequency, screening method, population, and quality of the screening setting.

### Key Concepts

- Benefit
- Harm
- Population strategy
- Screening effectiveness

## Knowledge Block 18 — What Does an Abnormal Screening Result Mean for the Next Step?

### Patient Explanation

An abnormal screening result generally means that further evaluation may be needed. It does not by itself establish cancer.

### Clinical Importance

PP-0174 owns the meaning and potential consequences of the abnormal result. PP-0175 owns the detailed diagnostic work-up.

### Key Concepts

- Abnormal screening
- Diagnostic confirmation
- Downstream work-up

## Knowledge Block 19 — What Does a Negative Screening Result Mean?

### Patient Explanation

A negative screening result means that the screening test did not identify an abnormality at that time.

It is reassuring, but it is not a guarantee that cancer is absent.

### Clinical Importance

This is particularly important when symptoms or other clinical concerns later arise.

### Key Concepts

- Negative result
- Residual risk
- False reassurance

## Knowledge Block 20 — What Evidence Is Still Uncertain?

### Patient Explanation

The available evidence does not give one universal number for every screening test's false-positive rate, false-negative rate, complication rate, or overdiagnosis rate.

The evidence also does not establish randomized proof that population gastric-cancer screening reduces mortality.

### Clinical Importance

These uncertainties should remain visible rather than being replaced with unsupported numerical thresholds.

### Key Concepts

- Evidence quality
- Uncertainty
- Generalizability

# Common Misconceptions

### Myth 1

**“A positive screening test means I have gastric cancer.”**

**Fact:** A positive screening result means that further evaluation may be needed. It is not itself a cancer diagnosis.

### Myth 2

**“A negative screening test means I definitely do not have gastric cancer.”**

**Fact:** Screening tests can miss cancer.

### Myth 3

**“False positives mean screening is useless.”**

**Fact:** False positives are an expected limitation of screening. The relevant question is whether the overall benefit–harm balance is favorable in the population being screened.

### Myth 4

**“Every positive screening result leads to a cancer diagnosis.”**

**Fact:** The Japanese example described by the NCI had a PPV of only 0.85%, illustrating that many positive screening pathways do not result in detection of gastric cancer.

### Myth 5

**“Endoscopy is completely risk-free.”**

**Fact:** Rare but serious complications can occur, including bleeding, perforation, cardiopulmonary events, and aspiration pneumonia.

### Myth 6

**“Radiation from photofluorography is either completely harmless or definitely dangerous.”**

**Fact:** The NCI describes the exposure as low dose and the possible carcinogenesis risk as theoretical and poorly quantified.

### Myth 7

**“Every abnormality found by screening needs treatment.”**

**Fact:** Screening can lead to overdiagnosis and overtreatment.

### Myth 8

**“If no cancer is found after a positive result, the screening episode caused no harm.”**

**Fact:** Additional testing, procedures, uncertainty, and other burdens can occur even when cancer is ultimately not confirmed.

### Myth 9

**“A later cancer after a negative screening test proves that the screening was wrong.”**

**Fact:** Screening tests cannot detect every cancer. A later diagnosis can occur after a negative result.

### Myth 10

**“A screening complication rate from one center applies everywhere.”**

**Fact:** The NCI notes that some complications can vary with screening-center experience.

# Key Messages

1. Screening is potentially useful but is not risk-free.
2. A positive screening result is not a cancer diagnosis.
3. A negative screening result does not guarantee that cancer is absent.
4. False positives can lead to additional testing and procedures.
5. False negatives can create false reassurance.
6. Endoscopy has rare but serious potential complications.
7. Photofluorography involves low-dose radiation exposure with a theoretical, poorly quantified carcinogenesis risk.
8. Premedication can cause adverse effects.
9. Overdiagnosis can lead to overtreatment.
10. Screening harms depend partly on population and screening-center context.
11. A study-specific PPV should not be treated as a universal value.
12. Detailed diagnostic evaluation after an abnormal result belongs to downstream packages.
