## Clinical Question

> **What can go wrong with gastric-cancer screening, and what do false-positive or false-negative results mean?**

## Educational Intent

Provide a patient-facing, evidence-grounded explanation of the limitations and potential harms of gastric-cancer screening.

The package must make four distinctions clear:

1. **Screening result ≠ diagnosis**
2. **Positive result ≠ confirmed cancer**
3. **Negative result ≠ guaranteed absence of cancer**
4. **Screening benefit must be weighed against screening harm**

# Primary Evidence Sources

## 1. NCI — Stomach (Gastric) Cancer Screening PDQ

This is the principal evidence source.

The NCI screening review states that harms of routine gastric-cancer screening are poorly quantified or reported and derive chiefly from screening experience in very high-risk areas such as Japan.

The NCI identifies false-positive testing as the most frequent harm.

It also identifies:

- low-dose radiation exposure from photofluorography;
- adverse effects of premedication;
- bleeding or perforation from endoscopy;
- overdiagnosis with attendant overtreatment.

The NCI further notes that some procedural harms can vary with screening-center experience.

# Evidence Notes

## 1. False Positives Are the Central Harm Concept

The NCI explicitly identifies false-positive tests as the most frequent harm of routine gastric-cancer screening.

The patient-facing implication is:

**Positive screening result**

→ **not automatically cancer**

→ **may require additional evaluation**

→ **may create burden or procedure-related harm**

This is the core causal chain for PP-0174.

## 2. The Japanese PPV Example Must Remain Study-Specific

The NCI reports:

- 17,647 men;
- age 40–60 years;
- combined serum pepsinogen and barium digital radiography;
- PPV 0.85%;
- positive rate 19.5% for serum pepsinogen;
- positive rate 22.5% for radiography;
- cancer detection rate 0.28%;
- no reduction in gastric-cancer mortality over seven years versus an age-matched surrounding population.

These numbers illustrate screening burden and limitations.

They are **not universal performance estimates**.

## 3. Endoscopic Harms

The NCI grades the evidence for uncommon but serious endoscopic side effects as solid, based on screening programs and case series.

Potential complications include:

- perforation;
- cardiopulmonary events;
- aspiration pneumonia;
- bleeding requiring hospitalization.

The package therefore uses the phrase:

> **rare but serious**

rather than presenting these as common outcomes.

## 4. Radiation Exposure

The NCI reports about 0.6 mSv from photofluorography in the cited evidence.

The possible carcinogenesis risk is described as:

> **theoretical but poorly quantified.**

This uncertainty must remain explicit.

## 5. Premedication

Premedication used for endoscopy and sometimes photofluorography may produce adverse effects.

PP-0174 owns this as a screening-related harm category.

Detailed drug-specific safety is outside scope.

## 6. Overdiagnosis

Overdiagnosis differs from a false-positive result.

### False positive

The screening result suggests disease, but the disease is not ultimately confirmed.

### Overdiagnosis

The disease or abnormality may actually be present, but it would not have caused important harm during the person's lifetime.

This distinction is essential.

## 7. Overtreatment

Overdiagnosis can lead to overtreatment.

The patient may undergo treatment for a condition that would not have become clinically important.

PP-0174 owns the screening-level concept, not treatment-specific adverse effects.

## 8. Screening-Center Experience

The NCI notes that harms such as perforation and bleeding may vary with screening-center experience.

Therefore:

**same screening modality ≠ identical risk in every setting**

The package does not create an operator-volume threshold because the supplied sources do not establish one.

## 9. Population Context

The NCI notes that evidence from high-risk areas may not apply to low-risk areas and that some harms may be higher in low-risk populations than in Japanese mass-screening programs.

This creates the bridge:

**PP-0173 — population context**

→ **PP-0174 — harm/benefit consequences**

# Evidence Strength Summary

## Established / directly supported

- False positives are the most frequent reported screening harm in the NCI review.
- Endoscopy has rare but serious potential complications.
- Photofluorography exposes patients to low-dose radiation.
- Premedication can cause adverse effects.
- Overdiagnosis can lead to overtreatment.
- Screening-center experience can affect some procedural risks.
- Study-specific PPV can be low even in high-risk populations.

## Supported but context-dependent

- Downstream burden after an abnormal result.
- Population-dependent harm/benefit.
- Generalizability limitations.
- False reassurance after a negative screen.

## Not established universally

- Universal false-positive rate.
- Universal false-negative rate.
- Universal sensitivity/specificity.
- Universal PPV/NPV.
- Universal complication rate.
- Universal overdiagnosis rate.
- Universal cost burden.
- Universal benefit–harm threshold.

# Clinical Claims Summary

1. Screening is not risk-free.
2. False-positive results are the most frequently reported harm in the NCI gastric-screening evidence review.
3. A positive screening result is not a diagnosis.
4. A negative screening result does not guarantee that cancer is absent.
5. Abnormal results can lead to further evaluation and associated burden.
6. Endoscopic screening can produce rare but serious complications.
7. Photofluorography produces low-dose radiation exposure.
8. Premedication can cause adverse effects.
9. Screening can cause overdiagnosis and overtreatment.
10. The magnitude of harm can depend on population and screening-center experience.
11. Study-specific performance metrics must not be converted into universal thresholds.
12. The overall value of screening depends on benefit–harm balance.

# Evidence Consistency Review

The supplied sources are internally coherent:

**Screening benefit is possible**

but

**screening is not risk-free**

and

**evidence is population- and modality-dependent**.

The NCI evidence specifically combines:

- inadequate mortality evidence for some screening approaches;
- observational evidence for endoscopy;
- recognized screening harms;
- low PPV in a high-risk program;
- concerns about generalizability.

No contradiction affecting PP-0174 ownership was identified.
