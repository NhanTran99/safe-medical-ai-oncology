# 1. Educational Objectives

After reading this Population Package, the reader should be able to:

1. Explain what multidisciplinary management means in gastric cancer.
2. Understand why gastric cancer may require input from several medical disciplines.
3. Recognize the major specialist roles involved in multidisciplinary gastric-cancer care.
4. Understand how pathology, imaging, staging, biomarkers, clinical condition, nutrition, and patient goals are integrated.
5. Understand the role of a multidisciplinary tumor board or equivalent multidisciplinary review.
6. Understand that multidisciplinary management is an integration process rather than a collection of unrelated specialist opinions.
7. Understand how multidisciplinary review can influence treatment planning and coordination without replacing the detailed treatment packages owned by individual specialties.
8. Understand why multidisciplinary review may need to be repeated when new information, treatment response, progression, toxicity, or patient circumstances change.
9. Recognize the role of supportive, nutritional, psychosocial, and palliative expertise within a coordinated care model.
10. Know what multidisciplinary management does and does not mean for an individual patient.

---

# 2. Atomic Clinical Question

> **How are the different medical and supportive-care perspectives brought together to form and reassess a coordinated management strategy for gastric cancer?**

This PP is intentionally an **integration-layer package**.

It is not a replacement for the detailed packages covering surgery, systemic therapy, radiotherapy, biomarkers, response assessment, toxicity, palliative care, surveillance, recurrence, or survivorship.

# 4. Clinical Knowledge Blocks

## Block 1 — What Is Multidisciplinary Management?

Multidisciplinary management means that relevant clinical information and expertise are brought together rather than allowing each clinical problem to be considered in isolation.

For gastric cancer, this can involve specialists such as surgical oncology, medical oncology, radiation oncology, gastroenterology, radiology, pathology, and supportive disciplines. NCCN's gastric-cancer guidance contains a dedicated section on the Principles of Multidisciplinary Team Approach for Esophagogastric Cancers and places multidisciplinary review directly within the treatment pathway.

The important idea is not simply that several specialists are present. The important idea is that their information is **integrated into one coordinated clinical strategy**.

---

## Block 2 — Why Can Gastric Cancer Need Several Specialists?

Gastric cancer management may require simultaneous consideration of:

- where the tumor is located;
- histologic diagnosis;
- depth and extent of disease;
- regional and distant spread;
- resectability;
- biomarker status;
- previous treatment;
- performance status;
- comorbidities;
- nutritional condition;
- expected treatment burden;
- patient preferences and goals.

These questions often cross specialty boundaries.

For example, a surgeon may need imaging and pathology information to assess an operation; the medical oncologist may need biomarker and pathology information to consider systemic treatment; and nutrition or supportive-care specialists may identify factors that affect whether a planned strategy is feasible.

---

## Block 3 — The Core MDT Disciplines

NCCN's multidisciplinary framework identifies core disciplines that may include:

- surgical oncology;
- medical oncology;
- gastroenterology;
- radiation oncology;
- radiology;
- pathology.

Additional supporting disciplines can include:

- nutrition services;
- nursing;
- social work;
- palliative care;
- other supportive disciplines as clinically appropriate.

The exact composition can vary by institution and case complexity.

---

## Block 4 — What Does the Surgeon Contribute?

The surgical team contributes information about:

- technical and medical feasibility of surgery;
- local disease extent;
- resectability;
- expected operative strategy;
- surgical risks and consequences;
- reconstruction considerations;
- the relationship between surgery and other treatments.

This PP explains the **integration role** of surgery.

Detailed gastrectomy, lymphadenectomy, operative technique, and surgical algorithms remain owned by dedicated surgery packages.

---

## Block 5 — What Does Medical Oncology Contribute?

Medical oncology contributes expertise about:

- systemic treatment options;
- biomarker-directed treatment context;
- treatment setting and sequence;
- prior systemic therapy;
- feasibility and tolerance;
- systemic treatment response and progression.

PP-0232 does not reproduce detailed systemic-treatment regimens.

Its role is to explain how systemic therapy is considered together with surgery, radiation, pathology, imaging, biomarkers, and patient factors.

---

## Block 6 — What Does Radiation Oncology Contribute?

Radiation oncology contributes expertise about:

- whether radiation may have a role;
- local/regional disease control;
- relationship between radiation and surgery;
- relationship between radiation and systemic treatment;
- timing and feasibility.

Detailed radiation planning and treatment technique are delegated to radiation-specific packages.

---

## Block 7 — What Does Gastroenterology / Endoscopy Contribute?

Gastroenterology and endoscopy may contribute:

- diagnostic endoscopy;
- biopsy and tissue acquisition;
- endoscopic staging information;
- selected endoscopic treatment;
- assessment of luminal problems;
- selected palliation or surveillance information.

The MDT package does not teach endoscopy technique. It explains why endoscopic findings may become important inputs into the multidisciplinary decision.

---

## Block 8 — What Does Pathology Contribute?

Pathology can provide:

- diagnostic confirmation;
- histologic information;
- pathologic staging information where applicable;
- margin information after surgery;
- biomarker-related information;
- clarification of pathologic uncertainty.

Pathology is therefore not simply a report-generating service. It is one of the information sources that can materially influence the integrated clinical strategy.

---

## Block 9 — What Does Radiology Contribute?

Radiology contributes interpretation of:

- tumor extent;
- anatomical relationships;
- regional disease;
- distant disease;
- treatment response;
- progression;
- resectability context.

NCCN's multidisciplinary principles emphasize joint review of actual clinical data rather than relying only on isolated reports. The purpose is to improve the quality of treatment decisions by allowing relevant specialists to interpret the information together.

Detailed CT, PET/CT, MRI, or RECIST methodology is delegated.

---

## Block 10 — What Do Nutrition and Supportive Services Contribute?

Gastric cancer and its treatments can affect:

- food intake;
- weight;
- hydration;
- nutritional status;
- functional status;
- symptoms;
- treatment tolerance.

Nutrition services can therefore contribute to treatment readiness and ongoing management.

Nursing, social work, rehabilitation, psychosocial services, and other supportive disciplines may become relevant depending on patient needs.

These contributions do not mean that every patient needs every service.

---

## Block 11 — What Does Palliative Care Contribute?

Palliative care may contribute to:

- symptom management;
- quality of life;
- communication about goals;
- support during complex treatment;
- management of advanced or recurrent disease;
- coordination when treatment goals change.

Palliative care is not synonymous with stopping anticancer treatment.

In an MDT model, palliative expertise can be introduced according to need and may coexist with active cancer treatment.

Detailed palliative-care content belongs to PP-0227.

# 5. How the MDT Integrates Information

A useful mental model is:

**Patient**

↓

**Clinical question**

↓

**Diagnosis + pathology + imaging + staging + biomarkers**

↓

**Patient factors**

- fitness;
- comorbidity;
- nutrition;
- organ function;
- preferences.

↓

**Specialist input**

↓

**MDT synthesis**

↓

**Integrated management strategy**

↓

**Patient discussion**

↓

**Implementation**

↓

**Reassessment**

↓

**MDT re-review when necessary**

The MDT therefore functions as an **integration hub**.

# 6. Multidisciplinary Management Across the Disease Continuum

## Newly Diagnosed / Localized Disease

The team may need to integrate:

- diagnosis;
- staging;
- pathology;
- endoscopic findings;
- resectability;
- biomarker information;
- fitness for major treatment;
- nutrition;
- treatment options.

NCCN's gastric-cancer algorithm places multidisciplinary review within the pathway to primary treatment.

## Locally Advanced Disease

The MDT may need to coordinate:

- systemic therapy;
- surgery;
- radiation where relevant;
- response/reassessment;
- nutritional and functional readiness.

## Unresectable Disease

The team may consider:

- systemic therapy;
- selected radiation;
- symptom management;
- nutritional support;
- palliative care;
- patient goals.

## Recurrent Disease

The MDT may need to reassess:

- location of recurrence;
- resectability;
- prior treatment;
- systemic options;
- local treatment possibilities;
- supportive and palliative needs.

## Metastatic Disease

The team may integrate:

- disease distribution;
- biomarker results;
- systemic-treatment options;
- performance status;
- prior therapy;
- symptom burden;
- patient goals.

## Peritoneal-only Disease

Complex peritoneal cases provide a particularly clear example of MDT value. NCCN includes multidisciplinary consultation in the work-up and treatment pathway and states that treatment decisions should be made in the context of a multidisciplinary tumor board in selected peritoneal/HIPEC circumstances.

# 7. Treatment Sequencing: What This PP Owns

PP-0232 owns **sequencing as a multidisciplinary coordination concept**.

For example:

> Should surgery, systemic therapy, and/or radiation be considered first, together, or at different points in the care pathway?

However, PP-0232 does not provide a universal stage-by-stage treatment algorithm.

Detailed sequencing remains delegated to treatment-specific packages.

The distinction is:

**PP-0232 = how specialists coordinate sequencing decisions**

versus

**Treatment PPs = what the detailed treatment strategy is.**

---

# 8. Shared Decision-Making

MDT discussion is not a substitute for discussion with the patient.

The final individualized plan must take into account:

- medical evidence;
- disease characteristics;
- treatment feasibility;
- expected benefits and burdens;
- comorbidities;
- nutritional/functional status;
- patient values;
- patient preferences;
- goals of care.

A multidisciplinary recommendation is therefore best understood as an **integrated clinical recommendation**, not an automatic treatment order.

---

# 9. Dynamic MDT Reassessment

Multidisciplinary management is not necessarily a one-time event.

Re-review may become appropriate when:

- new pathology information appears;
- biomarkers become available;
- staging changes;
- imaging changes;
- disease progresses;
- response is unexpected;
- toxicity limits treatment;
- resectability changes;
- a recurrence develops;
- the patient's functional status changes;
- patient goals change.

This creates an important cycle:

**Initial MDT**

→ **Treatment**

→ **New information**

→ **Reassessment**

→ **Updated MDT strategy**

# 10. Common Misconceptions

### Myth 1 — “MDT means several doctors giving separate treatment plans.”

**Fact:** The purpose is to integrate different perspectives into a coordinated strategy.

### Myth 2 — “The surgeon decides everything if surgery is involved.”

**Fact:** Surgical feasibility is considered alongside pathology, imaging, systemic treatment, radiation, patient condition, and other relevant expertise.

### Myth 3 — “Tumor board is only for terminal cancer.”

**Fact:** Multidisciplinary review can be part of primary treatment planning as well as complex advanced-disease management.

### Myth 4 — “More specialists automatically means more treatment.”

**Fact:** MDT can identify that a less intensive or different strategy is more appropriate.

### Myth 5 — “MDT guarantees there is one correct answer.”

**Fact:** Some cases remain uncertain even after multidisciplinary review.

### Myth 6 — “Palliative care means anticancer treatment has stopped.”

**Fact:** Palliative care can be integrated alongside active treatment when symptom or quality-of-life needs warrant it.

### Myth 7 — “MDT removes the need to talk with the patient.”

**Fact:** The patient remains central to individualized decision-making.

# 11. Patient Explanation

A patient can understand MDT as follows:

> **“Gastric cancer can involve several different medical questions. Different specialists may understand different parts of the problem. The multidisciplinary team brings these perspectives together so that the overall treatment plan is coordinated rather than fragmented.”**

The patient may encounter:

- surgeon;
- medical oncologist;
- radiation oncologist;
- gastroenterologist/endoscopist;
- radiologist;
- pathologist;
- nutrition specialist;
- nurse;
- social worker;
- palliative-care specialist.

Not every patient needs every specialist.

The team is assembled according to the patient's disease and needs.

---

# 12. What the Patient Should Ask

Useful questions include:

1. Who is involved in my multidisciplinary team?
2. What information was reviewed?
3. How do my pathology and imaging affect the plan?
4. Were my biomarkers considered?
5. Is surgery being considered, and what does the surgical team think?
6. Is systemic treatment or radiation part of the plan?
7. Do I need nutritional or supportive-care input?
8. Has my case been discussed by a tumor board or equivalent MDT?
9. What uncertainties remain?
10. What would cause the team to reassess the plan?
11. How do my preferences affect the final plan?
12. Should I ask for a second opinion if there is uncertainty?

---

# 13. Clinical Safety Boundary

This package explains multidisciplinary management but does not make an individualized MDT recommendation.

It must not:

- determine whether a patient is resectable;
- select a chemotherapy regimen;
- prescribe immunotherapy;
- determine radiation dose;
- interpret a biomarker result for an individual;
- determine RECIST response;
- diagnose recurrence;
- decide whether HIPEC is appropriate;
- provide an individualized prognosis.

Those decisions require the patient's complete clinical record and the treating multidisciplinary team.

# 15. Key Messages

1. Multidisciplinary management means integrating different areas of expertise into one coordinated strategy.
2. Gastric cancer can require multiple specialties because diagnosis, staging, biomarkers, surgery, systemic therapy, radiation, nutrition, and supportive needs can interact.
3. The core MDT may include surgical oncology, medical oncology, radiation oncology, gastroenterology, radiology, and pathology, with additional supportive disciplines according to need.
4. MDT is not simply a list of specialists; it is a process of joint review and coordinated decision-making.
5. Treatment sequencing can be discussed at MDT level, but detailed treatment algorithms belong to dedicated treatment packages.
6. Patient goals and preferences remain central.
7. MDT review can be repeated when new clinical information or changing circumstances alter the decision context.
8. Palliative and supportive care can be part of multidisciplinary care even while active treatment continues.
9. MDT does not eliminate uncertainty.
10. MDT does not replace individualized discussion with the treating clinical team.
