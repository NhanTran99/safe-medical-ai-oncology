# Educational Question

> **What is total gastrectomy, when and why might the entire stomach need to be removed for gastric cancer, and what should a patient expect after living without a stomach?**

## Clinical-Educational Intent

Explain total gastrectomy as a specific gastric-cancer operation without reducing it to a definition or presenting it as automatically more effective than subtotal gastrectomy. The package connects the oncologic reason for removing the entire stomach with tumor location and extent, adequate cancer resection, regional lymph-node management, reconstruction, surgical approach, curative versus selected palliative intent, and the long-term functional and nutritional consequences of having no stomach.

The package is deliberately positioned after general gastrectomy principles and subtotal gastrectomy, and before dedicated lymphadenectomy and downstream treatment packages.

---

# Educational Objectives

After reading this Population Package, the reader should be able to:

1. Explain what total gastrectomy means in plain language.
2. Understand why the entire stomach may be removed even when a smaller operation might otherwise be possible.
3. Understand the importance of tumor location, tumor extent, and adequate oncologic margins.
4. Understand why diffuse gastric involvement is a particularly important context for total gastrectomy.
5. Understand why selected cardia/proximal tumors may require total gastrectomy while other proximal tumors may be treated with a different operation.
6. Understand that regional lymph-node management is part of oncologic gastric surgery without needing to learn D1/D2 technical anatomy in this PP.
7. Understand that routine spleen removal is not part of every total gastrectomy.
8. Understand, at a conceptual level, how digestive continuity is restored after the stomach is removed.
9. Understand the distinction between open and minimally invasive total gastrectomy.
10. Understand that total gastrectomy can be performed with curative intent and that palliative surgery has a different purpose.
11. Understand how eating, weight, digestion, vitamin/mineral status and quality of life can change after total gastrectomy.
12. Recognize why lifelong nutritional monitoring may be required.
13. Identify common misconceptions about total gastrectomy.
14. Prepare practical questions for the surgical and oncology team.

# Clinical Knowledge Blocks

## 1. What Is Total Gastrectomy?

Total gastrectomy means removal of the entire stomach. It is a major operation used in selected gastric-cancer situations. The word “total” describes the extent of stomach removal; it does not by itself tell the reader the cancer stage or whether the operation is curative.

## 2. Why Would the Entire Stomach Need to Be Removed?

The central reason is oncologic adequacy. When a tumor's location or extent makes it difficult to preserve an adequate gastric remnant while achieving appropriate cancer removal and negative microscopic margins, total gastrectomy may be selected. The goal is not to remove as much stomach as possible; the goal is to remove the cancer appropriately.

## 3. Diffuse Gastric Involvement

Diffuse involvement is a particularly important setting. NCI describes total gastrectomy as required when a lesion diffusely involves the stomach. In this situation, preserving only a small portion of stomach may not provide an oncologically appropriate resection.

## 4. Cardia and Proximal Tumors

For tumors involving the cardia, NCI describes proximal subtotal gastrectomy or total gastrectomy as possible curative operations. Therefore, a cardia tumor does not automatically mean total gastrectomy. The choice depends on tumor anatomy and the ability to achieve adequate resection.

## 5. Body Tumor Extending Toward the Cardia

NCI describes total gastrectomy in selected body tumors extending close to the cardia. This is a location-and-extent example rather than a universal margin rule. The exact surgical decision remains patient-specific.

## 6. Why Negative Margins Matter

A negative microscopic margin means that cancer is not identified at the examined surgical edge. NCCN identifies adequate gastric resection to achieve negative microscopic margins as a core goal of resectable surgery. This is why preservation of stomach cannot be considered separately from cancer-control requirements.

## 7. Total Does Not Mean Automatically Better

Total gastrectomy is not simply a more aggressive version of subtotal gastrectomy that should always be preferred. NCI reports that in selected stage I disease, subtotal gastrectomy has equivalent survival and lower morbidity compared with total gastrectomy. The choice therefore depends on whether adequate oncologic treatment can be achieved with preservation.

## 8. What Is Removed?

The defining component is removal of the entire stomach. Curative gastric resection also includes regional lymph-node management. Omentum and other nearby structures may be involved in the operative specimen according to the disease and surgical plan. Nearby organs are not automatically removed merely because total gastrectomy is performed.

## 9. What About the Spleen?

NCCN states that routine splenectomy is not indicated unless the spleen is involved or there is extensive hilar adenopathy. This is important because patients may otherwise assume that removal of the entire stomach automatically requires removal of the spleen.

## 10. What If the Cancer Involves a Nearby Organ?

For T4b disease, NCCN states that involved structures require en-bloc resection. This means that if a tumor directly invades a neighboring structure, the operation may need to be extended to remove the involved tissue as one oncologic resection. It does not mean that neighboring organs are routinely removed.

## 11. What About the Esophagus?

In selected proximal/cardia or EGJ-related disease, additional esophageal resection may be needed to obtain adequate margins. Detailed EGJ classification and esophageal-cancer treatment are outside this PP.

## 12. What Happens to Digestion After the Stomach Is Removed?

The digestive tract must be reconstructed so that food can pass from the esophagus into the small intestine. The exact reconstructive method is a surgical technical issue and is not taught here.

## 13. Can a Person Eat Without a Stomach?

Yes. Eating remains possible, but the digestive system works differently because the normal gastric reservoir is gone. ACS explains that people who have had the stomach removed can eat only small amounts at a time and therefore need to eat more often.

## 14. Why Are Small Meals Often Needed?

Without a stomach reservoir, food reaches the small intestine differently and the person cannot store a large meal in the usual way. Smaller, more frequent meals are therefore a common practical adaptation.

## 15. Weight Loss After Gastrectomy

Weight loss is a recognized survivorship issue after gastric-cancer treatment. It may relate to reduced meal size, altered digestion, treatment effects, symptoms, or nutritional deficiency. Persistent or substantial weight loss needs clinical assessment rather than self-management alone.

## 16. Vitamin B12

NCCN identifies vitamin B12 deficiency as a long-term issue after gastrectomy and recommends lifelong monitoring and management of nutritional sequelae. This package explains the risk concept; it does not prescribe an individual B12 regimen.

## 17. Iron and Anemia

Iron deficiency and anemia can occur after gastrectomy. NCCN identifies iron monitoring and replacement as part of survivorship care. Individual testing and treatment depend on the patient's clinical situation.

## 18. Vitamin D and Calcium

Vitamin D and calcium deficiencies can occur after gastrectomy and can contribute to long-term bone-health problems. NCCN includes monitoring and supplementation within survivorship care. Exact supplementation is outside this PP.

## 19. Zinc and Other Micronutrients

Zinc and other micronutrient deficiencies may occur. The important patient-facing message is that nutrition after total gastrectomy is a long-term clinical issue rather than only a short postoperative concern.

## 20. Dumping Syndrome

Dumping syndrome can occur after total gastrectomy because food moves rapidly into the small intestine. NCCN describes early and late symptom patterns and includes dietary and follow-up management in survivorship guidance. PP-0198 explains what dumping syndrome means but does not replace the dedicated management guidance.

## 21. Small-Intestinal Bacterial Overgrowth

NCCN identifies small-intestinal bacterial overgrowth as another possible long-term issue after gastrectomy. It is included here to explain why persistent gastrointestinal symptoms may require clinical evaluation.

## 22. Diarrhea, Reflux and Dysphagia

Long-term quality of life after gastrectomy can be affected by diarrhea, dysphagia, reflux and eating restrictions. These symptoms are not inevitable for every patient, but they are important to recognize and discuss with the care team if persistent.

## 23. Why Lifelong Nutritional Follow-up Matters

NCCN recommends lifelong monitoring and management of nutritional sequelae of gastrectomy. The need for follow-up reflects the permanent anatomical change rather than a temporary postoperative complication.

## 24. Open Versus Minimally Invasive Surgery

NCCN allows minimally invasive approaches in selected cases when the surgeon has appropriate laparoscopic or robotic foregut and lymphadenectomy experience. Randomized evidence cited by NCCN supports comparable safety for laparoscopic total gastrectomy with lymphadenectomy in selected early-stage patients. Minimally invasive surgery is generally not recommended for T4b or bulky N2 disease.

## 25. Feeding Tube

NCCN recommends considering a feeding tube in selected patients undergoing total gastrectomy, particularly when postoperative chemoradiation appears likely. A feeding tube is therefore a selective support measure, not an automatic feature of total gastrectomy.

## 26. Curative-Intent Total Gastrectomy

Total gastrectomy can be performed with curative intent in appropriately resectable disease. The operation itself does not mean that disease is metastatic or incurable.

## 27. Palliative Surgical Context

In incurable disease, NCCN states that gastric resection should be reserved for palliation of symptoms such as obstruction or uncontrollable bleeding when nonsurgical options are not feasible. Palliative surgery has a symptom-relief goal and must not be presented as curative treatment.

## 28. Total Gastrectomy and Systemic Treatment

Surgery may be part of a multimodality treatment pathway. Perioperative, adjuvant or other treatment may be considered depending on stage and clinical context. PP-0198 explains this interface only; it does not select a regimen.

## 29. Total Gastrectomy and Endoscopic Resection

Some early cancers can be treated endoscopically when appropriate criteria are met. When endoscopic treatment is not appropriate or does not provide adequate cancer removal, surgical resection may become relevant. PP-0193–0195 own the detailed endoscopic criteria and methods.

## 30. Total Versus Subtotal Gastrectomy

The essential comparison is whether adequate oncologic treatment can be achieved while preserving part of the stomach. Subtotal gastrectomy preserves a gastric remnant; total gastrectomy does not. Total gastrectomy therefore carries a different long-term digestive and nutritional burden.

# Patient-Facing Questions

## Why did my surgeon recommend removing the whole stomach instead of part of it?

The answer usually depends on where the tumor is, how far it extends, and whether an adequate cancer operation can be achieved while safely preserving part of the stomach.

## Does total gastrectomy mean my cancer is very advanced?

Not necessarily. Total gastrectomy can be used in selected resectable disease, including some proximal or diffuse tumors. The operation itself does not define the stage.

## Could a smaller operation give the same cancer control?

In selected patients it can. NCI reports equivalent survival with subtotal gastrectomy compared with total gastrectomy in the cited stage I context, with lower morbidity. The key is whether the smaller operation can still remove the cancer appropriately.

## Will my spleen be removed?

Not routinely. NCCN states that routine splenectomy is not indicated unless the spleen is involved or there is extensive hilar adenopathy.

## Will my esophagus be removed?

Usually the stomach is the organ being removed. In selected proximal or EGJ-related tumors, additional esophageal resection may be necessary to achieve adequate margins.

## Will my intestines be removed?

The small intestine is used to reconnect the digestive tract after the stomach is removed. Additional bowel removal is not a routine consequence of total gastrectomy itself.

## Will I still be able to eat?

Yes. Eating is possible after total gastrectomy, but meal size and frequency usually change because there is no longer a stomach reservoir.

## Will I need to eat small meals?

Small, more frequent meals are a common adaptation after gastrectomy and are specifically included in NCCN survivorship guidance.

## Will I lose weight?

Weight loss is common enough after gastric-cancer treatment to be a recognized survivorship issue. Persistent or substantial weight loss should be discussed with the care team.

## Will I need vitamin B12?

Vitamin B12 deficiency is a recognized long-term issue after gastrectomy. Whether and how you need supplementation should be determined by your clinical team and monitoring results.

## What other deficiencies can occur?

NCCN identifies vitamin B12, vitamin D, calcium, iron and zinc deficiencies among possible long-term issues.

## What is dumping syndrome?

It is a group of symptoms related to food moving rapidly into the small intestine after stomach surgery. It can be discussed and managed as part of survivorship care.

## Can I develop diarrhea or reflux?

Yes. Diarrhea, reflux, dysphagia and eating restrictions are among the long-term issues reported after gastrectomy, although they do not occur in every patient.

## Will I need a feeding tube?

Not necessarily. NCCN recommends considering one in selected total-gastrectomy patients, particularly when postoperative chemoradiation is likely.

## Can total gastrectomy cure gastric cancer?

It can be performed with curative intent in appropriately resectable disease, but no operation guarantees that cancer will never return.

## Can total gastrectomy be done laparoscopically or robotically?

In selected cases, yes. NCCN supports minimally invasive approaches when the surgeon has appropriate experience, while noting that they are generally not recommended for T4b or bulky N2 disease.

## Does total gastrectomy always require lymph-node removal?

Regional lymph-node management is generally part of curative gastric-cancer resection. The detailed extent is a separate lymphadenectomy topic.

## Why are lymph nodes removed if scans do not show cancer there?

Microscopic lymph-node disease may not be visible on imaging. Regional lymph-node management is therefore integrated into oncologic gastric resection.

## What does a negative margin mean?

It means cancer was not found at the examined edge of the removed tissue. It is an important goal of curative surgery but does not guarantee that recurrence is impossible.

## What should I ask my surgeon before surgery?

Ask why total rather than subtotal gastrectomy is recommended, where the tumor is located, whether adequate margins are expected, what lymph-node surgery is planned, how the digestive tract will be reconstructed, what approach will be used, and what nutritional follow-up is expected.

# Common Misconceptions

## “Total gastrectomy is always better than subtotal gastrectomy.”

**Fact:** False. The operation should be selected to achieve appropriate cancer removal. In selected stage I disease, NCI reports equivalent survival for subtotal gastrectomy with lower morbidity.

## “If the whole stomach is removed, the spleen must also be removed.”

**Fact:** False. Routine splenectomy is not indicated unless the spleen is involved or there is extensive hilar adenopathy.

## “Total gastrectomy means there is no way to digest food.”

**Fact:** False. The digestive tract can be reconstructed so food passes from the esophagus to the small intestine, although eating and digestion change.

## “I will never be able to eat normally again.”

**Fact:** The digestive pattern changes substantially, but patients can eat after total gastrectomy. Small, more frequent meals are commonly needed.

## “Vitamin problems are only an early postoperative issue.”

**Fact:** False. NCCN describes nutritional deficiencies and other digestive issues as potentially lifelong.

## “A feeding tube is required for everyone.”

**Fact:** False. It is considered in selected patients.

## “Robotic surgery is always safer.”

**Fact:** Not necessarily. It may be appropriate in selected cases with experienced surgeons; it is not universally recommended.

## “Negative margins mean I am cured.”

**Fact:** No. Negative margins are an important surgical goal but do not eliminate all recurrence risk.

## “Total gastrectomy automatically means the cancer is metastatic.”

**Fact:** False. Total gastrectomy can be used with curative intent in resectable disease.

## “Total gastrectomy is the same as palliative surgery.”

**Fact:** False. Total gastrectomy may be curative-intent; palliative surgery has symptom relief as its goal.

# Extended Clinical Reasoning and Patient Education

## Scenario 1 — Diffuse gastric involvement

A patient has gastric cancer involving a broad or diffuse portion of the stomach. The clinical question is not simply whether the patient can technically have a smaller operation. The key question is whether a meaningful gastric remnant can be preserved while still removing the cancer appropriately and achieving the intended oncologic result.

In the supplied NCI PDQ, diffuse involvement is specifically identified as a setting in which total gastrectomy is required. This makes diffuse disease one of the clearest examples for the patient-facing explanation of why total removal can be necessary.

**Boundary:** This package explains the reason total gastrectomy may be required. It does not define the pathology criteria for diffuse-type histology or the hereditary diffuse gastric cancer algorithm.

## Scenario 2 — Cardia tumor

A patient has a tumor involving the cardia. The correct educational message is conditional: NCI describes proximal subtotal gastrectomy or total gastrectomy as possible curative operations. The choice depends on tumor anatomy and whether adequate resection can be achieved.

The package therefore avoids the unsafe shortcut: “cardia cancer always requires total gastrectomy.”

**Boundary:** Detailed EGJ/Siewert classification and esophageal-cancer treatment are delegated to the relevant EGJ/esophageal knowledge branch.

## Scenario 3 — Body tumor approaching the cardia

NCI identifies a specific body-tumor context in which total gastrectomy may be required when the tumor extends close to the cardia. This is useful for explaining why location is more than a simple label such as “upper” or “lower” stomach.

The patient-facing interpretation is that the surgical team must evaluate whether enough stomach can safely remain while still achieving adequate margins.

## Scenario 4 — Distal tumor with a potentially adequate gastric remnant

A distal tumor may be amenable to subtotal gastrectomy when adequate cancer removal can be achieved while preserving part of the stomach. In this setting, total gastrectomy should not be described as automatically more oncologically effective.

NCI reports equivalent survival and lower morbidity for subtotal gastrectomy compared with total gastrectomy in the cited stage I context. This evidence is retained as a boundary against over-treatment framing.

## Scenario 5 — T1b–T3 resectable disease

NCCN identifies distal gastrectomy, subtotal gastrectomy and total gastrectomy as possible adequate gastric resections for T1b–T3 resectable tumors, with the goal of negative microscopic margins and regional lymphadenectomy.

The implication for PP-0198 is not that every T1b–T3 tumor receives total gastrectomy. Rather, total gastrectomy is one surgical form available when the clinical anatomy makes it appropriate.

## Scenario 6 — T4b disease

For T4b tumors, NCCN states that involved structures require en-bloc resection. The patient-facing explanation can therefore acknowledge that the operation may extend beyond the stomach when direct invasion is present.

This package intentionally stops before explaining multivisceral surgical technique.

## Scenario 7 — Spleen preserved

A patient is worried that removal of the entire stomach automatically means the spleen must also be removed. NCCN directly rejects routine splenectomy unless the spleen is involved or there is extensive hilar adenopathy.

This is an important safety-oriented misconception correction because unnecessary organ removal should never be implied by the word “total.”

## Scenario 8 — Feeding support

A patient asks whether a feeding tube is inevitable. NCCN says to consider a feeding tube in selected patients undergoing total gastrectomy, especially when postoperative chemoradiation is likely.

The package therefore uses “may be considered” rather than “will be required.”

## Scenario 9 — Open surgery

Open total gastrectomy remains an established surgical approach. The package should not frame open surgery as obsolete simply because minimally invasive approaches exist.

The choice depends on disease characteristics, surgical expertise, and the circumstances of the operation.

## Scenario 10 — Laparoscopic total gastrectomy

NCCN cites randomized evidence showing comparable safety for laparoscopic total gastrectomy with lymphadenectomy versus open surgery in selected early-stage patients when performed by experienced surgeons.

This supports a patient-facing explanation that minimally invasive surgery can be appropriate in selected settings without promising that it is always preferable.

## Scenario 11 — Robotic surgery

Robotic surgery belongs to the minimally invasive branch. NCCN allows minimally invasive approaches in selected cases when surgeons have appropriate foregut and lymphadenectomy experience.

PP-0198 should not claim that robotic surgery has better survival or is safer for every patient.

## Scenario 12 — Curative-intent surgery

Total gastrectomy can be performed with curative intent in appropriately resectable gastric cancer. The patient should not interpret “total” as synonymous with metastatic or terminal disease.

Curative intent means the operation is undertaken with the goal of complete cancer removal, not that cure is guaranteed.

## Scenario 13 — Palliative surgery

In incurable disease, NCCN reserves gastric resections for symptom palliation when nonsurgical options are not feasible. Symptoms may include obstruction or uncontrollable bleeding.

The purpose is different from curative total gastrectomy. This distinction must be explicit throughout the package.

## Scenario 14 — Reconstruction

After the stomach is removed, continuity of the digestive tract must be restored. At the patient-facing level, the key concept is that food can travel from the esophagus into the small intestine despite the absence of a stomach.

The exact reconstructive operation is deliberately delegated.

## Scenario 15 — Eating after total gastrectomy

The absence of a gastric reservoir changes meal capacity and eating behavior. ACS states that people who have had the stomach removed can eat only a small amount at a time and therefore need to eat more often.

This is not presented as an exact prescription for every patient. It is an educational expectation that supports preparation for postoperative adaptation.

## Scenario 16 — Postprandial fullness

NCCN identifies postprandial fullness or eating dysfunction among long-term issues after gastrectomy. This means a patient may experience fullness or difficulty managing meal volume even after the acute postoperative period has ended.

The package emphasizes discussion with clinicians rather than self-diagnosis.

## Scenario 17 — Vitamin B12

NCCN identifies vitamin B12 deficiency as a long-term issue after gastrectomy and recommends lifelong monitoring and management of nutritional sequelae.

PP-0198 therefore explains why B12 is important but does not prescribe the route, dose, or laboratory schedule for an individual patient.

## Scenario 18 — Iron deficiency

Iron deficiency and anemia can occur after gastrectomy. The long-term care team may monitor blood counts and iron status and provide replacement when clinically indicated.

The package does not turn this into an individualized anemia-management protocol.

## Scenario 19 — Vitamin D and calcium

NCCN identifies vitamin D and calcium issues as part of long-term survivorship concerns. These deficiencies can contribute to bone-health problems.

The package therefore explains the need for long-term attention without specifying an individualized supplement regimen.

## Scenario 20 — Zinc and other micronutrients

Zinc is among the deficiencies identified in NCCN survivorship guidance. The broader lesson is that total gastrectomy changes nutritional physiology enough that follow-up must remain active over time.

## Scenario 21 — Dumping syndrome

NCCN identifies dumping syndrome as a concern after total gastrectomy and explains it in relation to rapid emptying into the small bowel. The patient-facing package should explain the concept, not attempt to diagnose every episode of dizziness, diarrhea or abdominal symptoms.

## Scenario 22 — Small-intestinal bacterial overgrowth

NCCN also identifies bacterial overgrowth in the small intestine as a possible long-term issue. Persistent gastrointestinal symptoms therefore warrant clinical assessment rather than assuming they are simply part of normal recovery.

## Scenario 23 — Quality of life

NCCN reports long-term quality-of-life effects after gastrectomy, including diarrhea, dysphagia, reflux, eating restrictions and effects on physical and cognitive functioning and fatigue.

These findings support the package's emphasis that total gastrectomy is not only an anatomic operation. It creates a long-term functional transition.

## Scenario 24 — Total gastrectomy after unsuccessful endoscopic treatment

Early gastric cancer may sometimes be treated by EMR or ESD when appropriate criteria are met. If endoscopic resection is not appropriate or does not achieve adequate cancer removal, surgical treatment can become relevant.

PP-0198 only owns the surgical branch; PP-0193–0195 own the detailed endoscopic criteria and methods.

# Clinical Decision Logic

## Question 1 — Is the cancer resectable?

The first educational distinction is whether the disease is considered appropriate for surgical resection. PP-0198 does not perform staging, but it explains that total gastrectomy is a surgical option within resectable disease.

## Question 2 — Where is the tumor?

Location matters because the amount of stomach that can safely remain depends on where the cancer sits and how far it extends.

## Question 3 — Is the stomach diffusely involved?

Diffuse involvement is a particularly strong source-supported context for total gastrectomy.

## Question 4 — Can adequate margins be achieved while preserving part of the stomach?

If yes, subtotal gastrectomy may be appropriate. If not, total gastrectomy may be necessary.

## Question 5 — Are nearby structures directly involved?

T4b disease may require en-bloc resection of involved structures. This is a disease-extent issue, not a routine feature of every total gastrectomy.

## Question 6 — What lymph-node management is needed?

Regional lymphadenectomy is integrated into curative gastric surgery. Detailed D1/D2 decisions belong to the dedicated packages.

## Question 7 — How will the digestive tract be reconstructed?

Continuity must be restored after the stomach is removed. Technical reconstruction is outside PP-0198.

## Question 8 — What happens after surgery?

Pathology and the overall treatment plan determine downstream therapy. Long-term nutritional and functional follow-up is important for gastrectomy survivors.

# Extended Patient FAQ

## Can total gastrectomy be avoided if the tumor is small?

Tumor size alone does not determine the operation. Location, depth, spread and the ability to achieve adequate cancer removal are important.

## If my tumor is in the upper stomach, does that automatically mean total gastrectomy?

No. Selected proximal tumors may be treated with proximal subtotal or total gastrectomy depending on anatomy and margin requirements.

## Why does the surgeon care so much about the margins?

The goal is to remove the cancer without leaving microscopic tumor at the surgical edge. This is why preserving stomach cannot override oncologic adequacy.

## Does a negative margin mean there is no cancer anywhere else in my body?

No. It describes the examined surgical edge. It does not rule out microscopic disease elsewhere or future recurrence.

## Why do they remove lymph nodes if the operation already removes the stomach?

Gastric cancer can spread through regional lymphatic pathways, so regional lymph-node management is part of oncologic resection.

## Does total gastrectomy remove the omentum?

The omentum may be included in the surgical specimen as part of oncologic gastric surgery, but the exact operative specimen depends on the surgical plan.

## Can another organ be removed with the stomach?

If the tumor directly invades an adjacent structure, en-bloc resection may be needed. This is not routine for every patient.

## Will I have a normal stomach-shaped digestive system afterward?

There will be no remaining stomach. The digestive tract is reconstructed so that the esophagus connects into the small intestine.

## Why can I only eat a little after surgery?

Without a stomach reservoir, there is less capacity for storing a meal. Smaller, more frequent meals are a common adaptation.

## Can I drink fluids with meals?

NCCN survivorship guidance discusses avoiding fluid intake with meals in the context of post-gastrectomy eating dysfunction and dumping management. Individual advice should come from the clinical nutrition team.

## Why am I losing weight months after surgery?

Weight loss can occur because meal size is reduced and digestion and nutrition are changed. Persistent weight loss should be evaluated.

## Why do I need long-term blood tests?

Some nutritional deficiencies can develop after gastrectomy, including B12, iron, vitamin D, calcium and zinc deficiencies.

## Can B12 deficiency happen even if I eat a good diet?

Yes. The issue is not simply dietary intake; gastrectomy changes gastrointestinal physiology relevant to nutrient absorption.

## Why can bone health be affected?

NCCN identifies vitamin D and calcium deficiency and osteopenia/osteoporosis as long-term concerns after gastric-cancer treatment.

## Can dumping syndrome happen years later?

Dumping syndrome is a recognized long-term concern after gastrectomy, so persistent compatible symptoms should be discussed with clinicians.

## Are diarrhea and reflux always signs that something went wrong during surgery?

No. They can occur as long-term functional consequences after gastrectomy, but persistent or severe symptoms should still be assessed.

## Is laparoscopic total gastrectomy available everywhere?

Not necessarily. NCCN emphasizes appropriate case selection and surgeon experience.

## Why might open surgery be chosen?

The disease extent, operative complexity and available expertise may make an open approach appropriate.

## Does minimally invasive surgery remove the need for lymphadenectomy?

No. The surgical approach and the oncologic need for regional lymph-node management are separate concepts.

## Can total gastrectomy be combined with chemotherapy?

It can be part of a multimodality treatment pathway. The exact treatment sequence is determined by the broader cancer plan.

## Will I automatically receive chemotherapy after surgery?

Not automatically. Downstream treatment depends on stage, pathology, prior treatment and current guidelines.

## Can total gastrectomy be done for stage I disease?

Yes, in selected situations. NCI describes total gastrectomy for selected stage I tumors involving the cardia, diffuse stomach or body tumors extending toward the cardia.

## Can total gastrectomy be done for stage II or III disease?

Yes, when disease is appropriately resectable. NCI describes total gastrectomy as an option for selected stage II/III disease based on location and extent.

## What if scans show disease outside the stomach?

That changes the treatment context. PP-0198 does not own metastatic-disease management.

## Can total gastrectomy be used to stop bleeding?

Gastric resection can be used selectively for palliation of uncontrollable bleeding when nonsurgical options are not feasible. The intent is symptom relief.

## What should my family know about eating after surgery?

Meal size, frequency and nutritional monitoring may change for the long term, so family support can be useful.

## Will I ever feel full again?

People can experience fullness and other eating dysfunction after gastrectomy. The pattern varies, and nutrition teams can help manage it.

## Is life without a stomach impossible?

No. Patients can live without a stomach, but they require substantial digestive and nutritional adaptation.

# Extended Patient Preparation and Postoperative Reasoning

## Before Surgery — What the Patient Should Understand

Before total gastrectomy, the patient should understand why the surgical team believes the entire stomach must be removed rather than preserving part of it. The key questions are tumor location, extent, resectability and whether adequate oncologic margins can be achieved.

The patient should also understand that total gastrectomy changes digestion permanently. This does not mean eating becomes impossible. It means eating capacity, meal frequency and nutritional follow-up become different from before surgery.

## After Surgery — What Is Expected to Change

The immediate recovery from major abdominal surgery is only one phase. Long-term adaptation is a separate phase. NCCN describes lifelong nutritional and functional issues after gastrectomy, so patient education should not stop at wound healing or discharge.

## What to Discuss at Follow-up

Useful topics include weight trend, appetite, meal tolerance, diarrhea, reflux, swallowing difficulty, symptoms after meals, fatigue and adherence to nutritional follow-up. These questions help distinguish expected adaptation from problems requiring assessment.

## Why Family Education Matters

Family members may interpret smaller meals as poor appetite or inadequate recovery. Education should clarify that smaller and more frequent meals can be a normal adaptation after total gastrectomy and that nutritional adequacy should be judged clinically rather than by comparing meal size with preoperative habits.

## When Persistent Symptoms Need Assessment

Persistent or worsening weight loss, inability to maintain oral intake, recurrent vomiting, severe diarrhea, significant swallowing difficulty, repeated post-meal symptoms, or other concerning changes should be discussed with the treating team. PP-0198 does not provide an emergency algorithm; it establishes the principle that long-term symptoms deserve assessment.

## Why the Operation and Long-term Plan Are One Continuum

Total gastrectomy changes anatomy permanently. The surgical decision therefore connects directly to reconstruction, eating adaptation, nutritional monitoring and survivorship. This continuity is why PP-0198 intentionally extends beyond the operating room while delegating detailed management to downstream packages.
