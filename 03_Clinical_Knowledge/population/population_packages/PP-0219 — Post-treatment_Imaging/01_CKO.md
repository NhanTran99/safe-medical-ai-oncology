# Educational Objectives

After reading this Population Package, the reader should be able to:

1. Explain what post-treatment imaging means in the context of gastric cancer.
2. Understand why imaging may be performed after a treatment milestone and what clinical question the scan is intended to answer.
3. Understand the difference between post-treatment assessment/restaging and long-term surveillance.
4. Understand why the timing of post-treatment imaging depends on the treatment pathway and the clinical question.
5. Recognize the role of CT as a major imaging modality for post-treatment restaging in relevant gastric-cancer pathways.
6. Understand that FDG-PET/CT and MRI may have selected roles depending on the clinical situation.
7. Understand why pre-treatment/baseline imaging is important for meaningful comparison with post-treatment imaging.
8. Recognize the main types of information that post-treatment imaging can provide, including improvement, persistent/residual abnormality, new disease, or indeterminate findings.
9. Understand that treatment itself can produce imaging changes and that not every abnormality represents cancer progression or recurrence.
10. Understand the interface between post-treatment imaging, restaging, clinical response assessment, and RECIST-based assessment.
11. Recognize situations in which imaging may need to be supplemented by endoscopy, biopsy, or other assessment.
12. Understand why multidisciplinary clinical interpretation is important.
13. Understand the limitations of imaging and why a scan showing no detectable disease does not automatically prove microscopic eradication or permanent cure.
14. Recognize the distinction between post-treatment imaging, long-term surveillance, and recurrence detection.
15. Use appropriate questions when discussing a post-treatment imaging result with the cancer-care team.

# Scope

## Primary Educational Question

> **How is imaging used after treatment for gastric cancer to reassess the disease, support restaging, and inform the next clinical assessment?**

PP-0219 is a specialized, patient-facing Population Package describing **post-treatment imaging as a defined clinical reassessment event**.

The package explains:

**Treatment milestone**
→ **clinical question**
→ **appropriate imaging approach**
→ **post-treatment imaging**
→ **comparison with baseline/prior imaging**
→ **interpretation of findings**
→ **restaging / response-assessment interface**
→ **multidisciplinary clinical interpretation**

It does not turn an imaging result into an individualized treatment prescription.

# Clinical Knowledge Blocks

## Knowledge Block 1 — What Is Post-treatment Imaging?

### Patient Explanation

Post-treatment imaging means using an imaging examination **after a defined treatment step** to look again at the cancer and surrounding anatomy.

The purpose is not simply to “take another picture.”

The imaging is performed because the care team has a clinical question such as:

- Has the disease changed after treatment?
- Is disease still visible?
- Has the disease become resectable?
- Is there new disease?
- Is there a finding that needs clarification?
- Is additional treatment or another clinical assessment needed?

### Clinical Importance

A post-treatment scan is therefore part of a clinical pathway.

It sits between treatment and the next clinical assessment.

### Key Concepts

- Post-treatment imaging is a reassessment event.
- The clinical question determines what information is needed.
- The result is interpreted in the context of the treatment already received.
- Imaging is evidence for clinical assessment, not an automatic treatment decision.

---

# Knowledge Block 2 — Why Is Imaging Performed After Treatment?

### Patient Explanation

Treatment changes the disease.

After treatment, doctors may need to know whether the visible disease has:

- decreased;
- remained similar;
- disappeared from the images;
- persisted;
- developed new areas;
- developed changes that cannot yet be interpreted confidently.

This information may help the team decide what type of clinical assessment should happen next.

### Clinical Importance

NCCN gastric-cancer pathways explicitly include **post-treatment assessment** and **restaging** in relevant treatment settings.

The post-treatment assessment may help distinguish resectable disease from unresectable or metastatic disease and therefore connects imaging with subsequent management pathways.

### Key Concepts

**Treatment**
→ **post-treatment imaging**
→ **restaging/reassessment**
→ **clinical decision**

The last step requires broader clinical judgment.

---

# Knowledge Block 3 — What Is Restaging After Treatment?

### Patient Explanation

**Restaging** means reassessing the extent of cancer after treatment.

It asks whether the disease is still localized, has become resectable, remains unresectable, or has developed additional disease.

Restaging is different from the original staging work-up because it occurs after treatment and is intended to understand the disease state at that later point.

### Clinical Importance

NCCN GAST-6 identifies post-treatment **restaging** using chest/abdomen/pelvis CT with oral and IV contrast, with FDG-PET/CT as clinically indicated in the relevant pathway.

### Key Concepts

Restaging is:

- time-specific;
- treatment-specific;
- based on the current disease state;
- interpreted against earlier information.

It is not simply “doing the original scan again.”

---

# Knowledge Block 4 — When Is Post-treatment Imaging Performed?

### Patient Explanation

There is no single imaging schedule that applies to every patient.

Timing depends on:

- what treatment was given;
- whether treatment was neoadjuvant/perioperative or another type;
- whether the purpose is response assessment or surgical planning;
- what the previous imaging showed;
- what clinical question needs to be answered.

### Source-supported Example

In an NCCN pathway for patients receiving preoperative/perioperative therapy, the relevant assessment is specified at approximately **5–8 weeks after completion of therapy** in the indicated context.

This is an example of a treatment-specific timing rule, not a universal rule for every patient.

### Key Concepts

> **The right timing is the timing that fits the treatment pathway and clinical question.**

Do not generalize one treatment pathway's timing to every gastric-cancer patient.

---

# Knowledge Block 5 — Why Is Baseline Imaging Important?

### Patient Explanation

To understand what changed after treatment, doctors need something to compare the new scan with.

The earlier examination may show:

- the location of the tumor;
- measurable disease;
- lymph nodes;
- distant lesions;
- other findings that require follow-up.

The post-treatment scan can then be compared with that earlier information.

### Clinical Importance

Without a meaningful baseline, it can be harder to decide whether an abnormality is:

- new;
- persistent;
- smaller;
- larger;
- unchanged;
- treatment-related.

### Key Concepts

**Baseline**
→ **Treatment**
→ **Post-treatment scan**
→ **Comparison**
→ **Interpretation**

---

# Knowledge Block 6 — Why Does Imaging Modality Matter?

### Patient Explanation

Different imaging methods show different types of information.

For gastric-cancer post-treatment assessment, CT is a major modality in guideline-defined pathways.

PET/CT and MRI may be useful in selected situations.

The best examination depends on the clinical question and patient circumstances.

### Clinical Importance

ESMO-ASCO emphasizes that imaging modality at baseline and during follow-up matters for response assessment because measurement error can affect interpretation.

### Key Concepts

Do not assume:

> “The newest or most advanced scan is automatically the best scan.”

The useful question is:

> **“Which examination best answers the clinical question in this patient?”**

---

# Knowledge Block 7 — What Is the Role of CT?

### Patient Explanation

CT can show the anatomy of the chest, abdomen and pelvis and is a major imaging tool for post-treatment assessment in gastric-cancer pathways.

NCCN specifies chest/abdomen/pelvis CT with oral and IV contrast for restaging in relevant post-treatment pathways.

### Clinical Importance

CT can help evaluate:

- the primary region;
- lymph nodes;
- liver and other organs;
- lungs;
- peritoneal or other metastatic findings;
- changes compared with prior imaging.

### Key Concepts

CT is a major post-treatment assessment tool, but it is not automatically required in every clinical circumstance.

---

# Knowledge Block 8 — When Might PET/CT Be Used?

### Patient Explanation

FDG-PET/CT combines anatomical information with information about glucose metabolism.

In gastric cancer, NCCN includes FDG-PET/CT **when clinically indicated** in relevant post-treatment pathways.

### Clinical Importance

PET/CT may provide additional information when metabolic activity is clinically relevant.

It does not automatically replace CT in every patient.

### Key Concepts

- PET/CT is selected according to the clinical question.
- Metabolic findings must be interpreted in context.
- PET/CT may complement anatomical imaging.

---

# Knowledge Block 9 — When Might MRI Be Used?

### Patient Explanation

MRI is another imaging modality that can be useful in selected circumstances.

NCCN notes that MRI can be considered as an alternative imaging approach for patients who cannot undergo CT in relevant follow-up contexts.

### Clinical Importance

MRI is not simply a “better CT.”

It is a different examination with different strengths, limitations and practical considerations.

### Key Concepts

Modality selection depends on:

- the clinical question;
- the anatomy of interest;
- previous imaging;
- patient-specific circumstances;
- availability and appropriateness.

---

# Knowledge Block 10 — Why Might Contrast Be Used?

### Patient Explanation

Contrast material can help make certain anatomical structures and abnormalities easier to see.

NCCN post-treatment pathways specify CT with oral and IV contrast in relevant settings.

However, contrast is not appropriate in exactly the same way for every patient.

Factors such as renal function and prior contrast reactions can affect imaging planning.

### Boundary

Detailed contrast-agent safety and pharmacology belong to dedicated contrast packages.

---

# Knowledge Block 11 — What Can a Post-treatment Scan Show?

A post-treatment scan may show:

### 1. Improvement

Visible disease may become smaller or less extensive.

### 2. Persistent abnormality

Some abnormality may remain visible.

### 3. No detectable disease

The examination may show no visible disease within the limits of the modality.

### 4. Stable findings

The visible findings may appear similar to previous imaging.

### 5. New findings

New lesions or abnormalities may appear.

### 6. Indeterminate findings

A finding may not be clearly classifiable from the available examination.

### 7. Treatment-related changes

Inflammation, fibrosis, postoperative changes or other treatment effects may alter the appearance.

These categories are not interchangeable.

---

# Knowledge Block 12 — What Does Residual Abnormality Mean?

### Patient Explanation

An abnormality that remains visible after treatment does not automatically mean the treatment failed.

Depending on the treatment and timing, imaging may show:

- viable residual tumor;
- scar tissue;
- fibrosis;
- inflammation;
- postoperative change;
- other non-cancerous abnormalities.

Additional clinical information may be needed.

### Clinical Importance

This is one reason post-treatment imaging cannot be interpreted in isolation.

---

# Knowledge Block 13 — What Does “No Evidence of Disease” Mean?

### Patient Explanation

A scan may show **no detectable evidence of disease**.

This means that the imaging examination did not show detectable disease using that method at that time.

It does not automatically mean:

- every cancer cell has disappeared;
- microscopic disease is absent;
- recurrence is impossible;
- permanent cure is guaranteed.

### Clinical Importance

The distinction is especially important after neoadjuvant therapy, where imaging may be encouraging but pathology or additional assessment may still matter.

---

# Knowledge Block 14 — What Does a New Finding Mean?

### Patient Explanation

A new finding needs interpretation.

It may represent:

- new cancer;
- a treatment-related change;
- infection/inflammation;
- another benign process;
- an indeterminate abnormality.

Therefore:

> **“New on the scan” does not automatically mean “cancer recurrence.”**

The clinical team may recommend additional imaging, endoscopy, biopsy, or another assessment depending on the situation.

---

# Knowledge Block 15 — What Is the Relationship to RECIST?

### Patient Explanation

Post-treatment imaging provides information about what is visible.

**RECIST** is a standardized framework that can be used to describe treatment response in solid tumors under specified circumstances.

Therefore:

**Imaging**
→ provides observations

**RECIST**
→ applies standardized assessment rules

**Clinical assessment**
→ integrates imaging with the rest of the patient's information.

### Boundary

PP-0218 owns detailed RECIST-based assessment.

PP-0219 owns the post-treatment imaging event and the imaging-to-assessment interface.

---

# Knowledge Block 16 — Why Is RECIST Not the Whole Clinical Decision?

ESMO-ASCO explicitly notes that RECIST was developed for standardized response assessment and that, for an individual patient outside a clinical trial, treatment benefit should be based on medical judgment integrating clinical, imaging and laboratory information.

Therefore a post-treatment imaging result should not be converted automatically into:

> “continue treatment”

or

> “stop treatment”

without the broader clinical context.

---

# Knowledge Block 17 — When Might Endoscopy and Biopsy Be Needed?

### Patient Explanation

Imaging does not answer every question.

In selected post-treatment pathways, NCCN includes:

- EGD;
- biopsy.

For example, in the specified MSI-H/dMMR perioperative/neoadjuvant immunotherapy pathway, response assessment includes FDG-PET/CT as clinically indicated and EGD with biopsy, with CT used according to the stated pathway.

### Clinical Importance

Endoscopy and biopsy can provide information that imaging alone cannot establish.

### Boundary

Detailed endoscopy and biopsy methodology are delegated.

---

# Knowledge Block 18 — When Might Laparoscopy Be Needed?

### Patient Explanation

Some questions, particularly questions about peritoneal disease, cannot always be answered completely by imaging.

NCCN describes a role for laparoscopy with cytology in selected settings.

### Clinical Importance

This reinforces an important principle:

> **Imaging is powerful, but it is not the only way to assess disease.**

### Boundary

Detailed laparoscopy technique and peritoneal cancer index methodology are outside PP-0219.

---

# Knowledge Block 19 — What If the Imaging and Clinical Findings Do Not Agree?

### Patient Explanation

Sometimes:

- symptoms suggest progression but imaging is stable;
- imaging looks abnormal but the clinical picture is reassuring;
- PET activity and anatomical appearance do not tell exactly the same story;
- a postoperative change looks suspicious.

When this happens, the answer may require:

- review of previous images;
- another imaging modality;
- repeat imaging;
- endoscopy;
- biopsy;
- laboratory information;
- multidisciplinary review.

### Key Concept

> **Discordance is a reason for careful assessment, not an automatic reason to choose the most alarming interpretation.**

---

# Knowledge Block 20 — What Is Special About Post-treatment Imaging After Immunotherapy?

Immunotherapy can create response-assessment challenges that have led to specialized frameworks in clinical trials, including iRECIST.

The project ESMO-ASCO source identifies iRECIST as a response framework developed for trials testing immunotherapeutics.

PP-0219 should therefore acknowledge that:

- post-treatment imaging after immunotherapy may require special interpretation;
- apparent progression may require context;
- specialized response criteria may be relevant in clinical trials.

Detailed iRECIST methodology belongs outside this PP.

---

# Knowledge Block 21 — What Is Special About MSI-H/dMMR Gastric Cancer?

NCCN v2.2026 provides a particularly important post-treatment example for MSI-H/dMMR tumors receiving preoperative/perioperative immune checkpoint inhibition.

The pathway includes:

- FDG-PET/CT as clinically indicated;
- EGD and biopsy;
- chest/abdomen CT with oral and IV contrast, with pelvis CT when clinically indicated;
- a specified post-therapy assessment window.

NCCN also notes that the role of surgery after a **biopsy-proven and radiologic/metabolic complete response** to neoadjuvant immunotherapy is unclear.

This is an important example of why:

> **a very favorable imaging result does not automatically answer every clinical question.**

---

# Knowledge Block 22 — Why Is Multidisciplinary Interpretation Important?

### Patient Explanation

A post-treatment scan is usually interpreted together with other information.

The team may consider:

- symptoms;
- physical examination;
- blood tests;
- pathology;
- treatment received;
- prior imaging;
- current imaging;
- surgical options;
- patient-specific circumstances.

NCCN emphasizes multidisciplinary review in gastric-cancer care.

### Key Concept

> **The scan contributes evidence; the clinical team interprets what that evidence means for the patient.**

---

# Knowledge Block 23 — Is Post-treatment Imaging the Same as Surveillance?

### No.

**Post-treatment imaging** asks:

> “What is the disease status after this treatment episode or milestone?”

**Surveillance** asks:

> “How should the patient be followed over time after treatment?”

NCCN GAST-6 and GAST-7 explicitly separate post-treatment assessment/restaging from follow-up/surveillance.

PP-0220 owns long-term surveillance.

---

# Knowledge Block 24 — Is Post-treatment Imaging the Same as Recurrence Detection?

### No.

A post-treatment scan may detect recurrence, but recurrence detection is a separate clinical concept.

PP-0221 owns the question:

> “How is recurrence recognized and established?”

PP-0219 owns:

> “What does imaging after treatment contribute to reassessment?”

---

# Knowledge Block 25 — Why Are Repeated Examinations Compared Carefully?

### Patient Explanation

Small differences between two scans do not always represent a true biological change.

Differences can arise from:

- imaging modality;
- technical factors;
- timing;
- lesion measurement;
- reader variability;
- treatment-related change.

ESMO-ASCO specifically identifies measurement error as relevant to response assessment.

### Key Concept

The care team considers whether an apparent difference is likely to represent a meaningful change.

---

# Knowledge Block 26 — What Questions Should a Patient Ask About a Post-treatment Scan?

Useful questions include:

1. **Why am I having this scan now?**
2. **What clinical question are you trying to answer?**
3. **Which earlier scan are you comparing it with?**
4. **What changed compared with my baseline?**
5. **Is the finding clearly treatment-related, clearly cancer-related, or uncertain?**
6. **Does the scan indicate a response, stable disease, or possible progression under the relevant assessment framework?**
7. **Is additional imaging needed?**
8. **Would endoscopy or biopsy add information?**
9. **Does the result affect surgical assessment or restaging?**
10. **What other clinical information is being considered before deciding the next step?**

# Clinical Importance

Post-treatment imaging is important because it creates a structured bridge between treatment and the next clinical assessment.

The clinically meaningful sequence is:

**Treatment**
→ **appropriate reassessment timing**
→ **post-treatment imaging**
→ **comparison with baseline/prior imaging**
→ **disease findings**
→ **restaging / response assessment**
→ **multidisciplinary interpretation**
→ **next clinical step**

The scan is therefore not an isolated diagnostic event.

# Common Misconceptions

## Myth 1

**“The first scan after treatment tells us whether I am cured.”**

**Fact:** It provides evidence about the disease state at that time. It does not by itself establish permanent cure.

---

## Myth 2

**“No visible disease means there is definitely no cancer left.”**

**Fact:** Imaging can miss microscopic or very small disease.

---

## Myth 3

**“Any new spot means the cancer has returned.”**

**Fact:** New findings can have multiple causes and may require further assessment.

---

## Myth 4

**“Any remaining abnormality means the treatment failed.”**

**Fact:** Residual imaging abnormalities can represent viable disease, but they can also reflect treatment-related change.

---

## Myth 5

**“PET/CT is always better than CT.”**

**Fact:** Different modalities answer different clinical questions.

---

## Myth 6

**“MRI should always replace CT because it has no radiation.”**

**Fact:** Modality selection depends on the clinical question and patient circumstances.

---

## Myth 7

**“RECIST is the same as the scan.”**

**Fact:** Imaging supplies observations; RECIST is a standardized assessment framework applied to appropriate imaging information.

---

## Myth 8

**“RECIST decides my treatment.”**

**Fact:** Treatment decisions require integration of clinical, imaging, laboratory, pathological and patient-specific information.

---

## Myth 9

**“Post-treatment imaging and surveillance are the same thing.”**

**Fact:** Immediate/defined reassessment and long-term surveillance have different purposes.

---

## Myth 10

**“If a scan is unclear, it means the doctor cannot know what is happening.”**

**Fact:** Additional imaging, endoscopy, biopsy, repeat assessment or multidisciplinary review may clarify the situation.

---

## Myth 11

**“More scans always mean better care.”**

**Fact:** Imaging should be clinically justified and appropriately timed.

---

## Myth 12

**“A favorable PET/CT after immunotherapy automatically means surgery is unnecessary.”**

**Fact:** In selected MSI-H/dMMR neoadjuvant immunotherapy pathways, NCCN specifically notes uncertainty about the role of surgery even after biopsy-proven and radiologic/metabolic complete response.

# Patient Explanation — The Core Mental Model

A useful way to think about post-treatment imaging is:

> **The scan is a snapshot. The clinical team is looking at the movie.**

The team compares:

- what the disease looked like before treatment;
- what treatment was given;
- what the disease looks like now;
- what symptoms and tests show;
- whether the findings are clear or uncertain;
- what the next clinical question is.

That is why one imaging phrase should not be interpreted in isolation.

# Patient Questions

A patient can ask:

- Why is this scan being done now?
- What treatment milestone does it follow?
- What are you looking for?
- What was my baseline scan?
- Is this scan directly comparable with my earlier scan?
- Which findings improved?
- Which findings remained?
- Is anything new?
- Could any of the findings be caused by treatment?
- Is the result being interpreted using RECIST?
- Do I need another test to clarify an uncertain finding?
- Does this affect surgical assessment?
- Is this part of restaging or surveillance?
- What does the result mean for the next step?
- What does the result **not** tell us?

# Clinical Safety Boundary

This Population Package provides general education.

It does not:

- diagnose recurrence;
- determine whether an individual patient has residual cancer;
- determine whether a treatment worked for an individual patient;
- prescribe or stop treatment;
- determine surgical eligibility for an individual patient;
- determine whether an individual patient is cured;
- replace radiologist interpretation;
- replace multidisciplinary oncology review.

A patient-specific imaging result must be interpreted using the actual images/report, treatment history, symptoms, examination, laboratory results, pathology and other relevant clinical information.
