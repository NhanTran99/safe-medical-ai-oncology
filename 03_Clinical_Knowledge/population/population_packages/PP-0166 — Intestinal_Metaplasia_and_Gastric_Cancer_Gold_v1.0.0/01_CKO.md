## B. Educational Objectives

After reading this Population Package, the reader should be able to:

1. Explain what gastric intestinal metaplasia is.
2. Understand how intestinal metaplasia relates to chronic atrophic gastritis and H. pylori.
3. Understand why intestinal metaplasia is associated with increased gastric-cancer risk.
4. Understand its position within Correa's cascade.
5. Understand why intestinal metaplasia does not mean that cancer is already present or inevitable.
6. Understand why biopsy may identify intestinal metaplasia even when it is not obvious during endoscopy.
7. Understand that follow-up decisions are individualized and that a universal surveillance schedule is not established by the supplied evidence.

## D. Clinical Knowledge Blocks

### KB1 — What Is Gastric Intestinal Metaplasia?

Intestinal metaplasia is a change in the stomach lining in which normal gastric cells are replaced by cells that resemble cells normally found in the intestine.

### KB2 — Where Does Intestinal Metaplasia Fit in Gastric Carcinogenesis?

The supplied NCI material describes Correa's cascade:
1. chronic gastritis;
2. gastric mucosal atrophy;
3. gastric intestinal metaplasia;
4. gastric dysplasia;
5. gastric cancer.

The sequence represents a risk/progression framework, not an inevitable pathway.

### KB3 — Relationship With Atrophic Gastritis

Intestinal metaplasia commonly occurs in the setting of chronic atrophic gastritis. PP-0165 owns atrophic gastritis itself; PP-0166 owns intestinal metaplasia.

### KB4 — H. pylori and Intestinal Metaplasia

H. pylori is identified by NCI as the most common risk factor for intestinal metaplasia. H. pylori belongs to the etiologic context of this PP, while H. pylori prevention and eradication are delegated to PP-0160 and PP-0161.

### KB5 — Other Risk Factors

The supplied NCI material identifies additional risk factors including pernicious anemia, autoimmune gastritis, ethnicity, family history of gastric cancer, dietary habits, smoking, and alcohol use. These are contextual here and do not create duplicate risk-factor packages.

### KB6 — Is Intestinal Metaplasia a Precancerous Condition?

Yes. NCI identifies intestinal metaplasia as a precursor/risk condition associated with gastric-cancer risk.

### KB7 — Does Intestinal Metaplasia Mean Cancer?

No. Intestinal metaplasia is a gastric mucosal change associated with increased risk; it is not itself a diagnosis of gastric cancer.

### KB8 — Does Everyone With Intestinal Metaplasia Develop Cancer?

No. NCI states that each step of Correa's cascade occurs only in a minority of patients. Risk should therefore not be presented as inevitable progression.

### KB9 — Can Intestinal Metaplasia Be Seen on Endoscopy?

Not always. NCI states that intestinal metaplasia may not be visible during endoscopy and is often identified when gastric biopsies are taken.

### KB10 — Why Does Biopsy Matter?

Histologic assessment can identify intestinal metaplasia that is not visually obvious. This package explains the concept but does not define a detailed biopsy protocol.

### KB11 — Intestinal Metaplasia and Dysplasia

Dysplasia is a downstream precancerous change in the Correa sequence. Detailed dysplasia classification and management are outside PP-0166.

### KB12 — H. pylori Eradication After Intestinal Metaplasia

H. pylori treatment is clinically relevant when intestinal metaplasia is present, but the supplied NCI prevention evidence is nuanced and does not justify claiming that eradication reliably reverses intestinal metaplasia or eliminates subsequent cancer risk.

### KB13 — Does Intestinal Metaplasia Require Surveillance?

It may prompt consideration of follow-up in selected patients, but the supplied NCI source explicitly describes considerable controversy regarding appropriate surveillance. No universal interval is asserted here.

### KB14 — What Does an IM Diagnosis Mean for a Patient?

It means that a gastric mucosal precursor/risk change has been identified. The next clinical questions concern the underlying cause, other risk factors, extent/context of the finding, and whether individualized follow-up is appropriate.

### KB15 — Individual Risk

The supplied materials do not provide a validated individual absolute-risk calculator for intestinal metaplasia. The package therefore uses risk categories and qualitative interpretation rather than numerical individualized prediction.

## Patient Explanation

> Intestinal metaplasia is a change in the stomach lining in which normal stomach cells are replaced by cells that resemble cells normally found in the intestine.
>
> It often develops in the setting of chronic gastritis and can be associated with H. pylori infection. It is considered a precursor or precancerous change because it is associated with an increased risk of stomach cancer and can occur within a sequence of changes that may progress to dysplasia and, eventually, cancer.
>
> Having intestinal metaplasia does not mean that you already have stomach cancer, and it does not mean that you will definitely develop cancer. The appropriate next steps depend on the underlying cause and the person's overall risk and clinical findings.

## Common Misconceptions

### Myth 1
“Intestinal metaplasia means I already have stomach cancer.”

**Fact:** It is a precursor/risk condition, not cancer itself.

### Myth 2
“Everyone with intestinal metaplasia develops stomach cancer.”

**Fact:** Progression through Correa's cascade occurs only in a minority of patients.

### Myth 3
“Intestinal metaplasia and atrophic gastritis are the same condition.”

**Fact:** They are related but distinct gastric mucosal changes.

### Myth 4
“If endoscopy looks normal, I cannot have intestinal metaplasia.”

**Fact:** Intestinal metaplasia is not always visible during endoscopy and may be detected on biopsy.

### Myth 5
“H. pylori eradication always reverses intestinal metaplasia.”

**Fact:** The supplied NCI evidence is nuanced and does not support this universal claim.

### Myth 6
“Everyone with intestinal metaplasia needs the same surveillance schedule.”

**Fact:** The supplied NCI source describes considerable controversy regarding appropriate surveillance.

## Key Messages

- Intestinal metaplasia is a gastric mucosal precursor/risk condition.
- It is part of the Correa cascade between gastric atrophy and dysplasia.
- H. pylori is the most common risk factor identified in the supplied NCI source.
- Intestinal metaplasia increases gastric-cancer risk but does not mean cancer is already present or inevitable.
- It may not be visible during endoscopy and can be identified by biopsy.
- H. pylori eradication remains relevant but does not justify a universal claim of IM reversal.
- Surveillance/follow-up should not be reduced to an invented universal interval.
