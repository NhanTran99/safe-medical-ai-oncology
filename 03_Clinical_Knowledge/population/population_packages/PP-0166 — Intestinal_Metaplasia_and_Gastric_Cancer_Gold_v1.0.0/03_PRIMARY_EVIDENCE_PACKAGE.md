## Clinical Question

> What is gastric intestinal metaplasia, why is it associated with increased gastric-cancer risk, how does it fit into gastric carcinogenesis, and what should a person understand after it is identified?

## Educational Intent

Provide a patient-centered, source-grounded explanation of intestinal metaplasia as a gastric mucosal precursor/risk condition while preserving the uncertainty around progression, H. pylori eradication effects, and surveillance.

## Primary Evidence Sources

### NCI — Genetics of Gastric Cancer (PDQ®)

NCI describes chronic gastritis as a histological diagnosis and states that chronic gastritis can lead to gastric intestinal metaplasia. It identifies H. pylori as the most common risk factor for IM and lists pernicious anemia, autoimmune gastritis, ethnicity, family history, diet, smoking, and alcohol as additional risk factors.

NCI describes Correa's cascade as:
1. chronic gastritis;
2. gastric mucosal atrophy;
3. gastric intestinal metaplasia;
4. gastric dysplasia.

The source states that these changes can eventually result in gastric cancer and that each step occurs only in a minority of patients.

NCI also states that there is considerable controversy regarding appropriate surveillance in patients with intestinal metaplasia.

### NCI — Stomach (Gastric) Cancer Prevention (PDQ®)

NCI identifies intestinal metaplasia as a precursor/risk condition. The source discusses H. pylori treatment in patients with atrophic gastritis and intestinal metaplasia.

In the cited Colombian randomized study, anti-H. pylori treatment was associated with borderline statistically significant regression of IM compared with placebo, but progression rates were comparable between treatment groups. This evidence does not support a universal statement that eradication reverses IM.

### NCI — Causes of Stomach Cancer

NCI identifies atrophic gastritis with intestinal metaplasia as a medical condition associated with increased stomach-cancer risk.

### ACS — Stomach Cancer

ACS describes intestinal metaplasia as replacement of normal stomach-lining cells with cells that normally line the intestines. It explains the relationship with atrophic gastritis, H. pylori, dysplasia, and stomach cancer in patient-facing language.

## Evidence Notes

### 1. Intestinal Metaplasia as a Precursor Condition

NCI classifies IM among conditions associated with increased gastric-cancer risk and places it within a recognized histologic progression pathway.

### 2. Correa's Cascade

The cascade is central to the package because it explains why IM matters:

**Chronic gastritis → gastric mucosal atrophy → intestinal metaplasia → dysplasia → gastric cancer**

The source explicitly cautions that each step occurs only in a minority of patients.

### 3. Endoscopic Visibility

A small focus of IM may not be visible on endoscopy. NCI states that IM is often identified when random gastric biopsies are taken.

This supports patient education but does not establish a biopsy protocol.

### 4. H. pylori Eradication

The NCI Prevention PDQ describes a trial in which anti-H. pylori therapy had borderline evidence of IM regression, but the progression rate was comparable between placebo and antibiotic recipients.

Therefore the package deliberately avoids the claim:

> “H. pylori eradication reverses intestinal metaplasia.”

### 5. Surveillance

NCI states that there is considerable controversy regarding appropriate surveillance in patients with IM.

Therefore no universal interval is generated.

## Clinical Claims Summary

### Established / Supported
- Gastric intestinal metaplasia is a gastric mucosal precursor/risk condition.
- H. pylori is the most common risk factor identified in the cited NCI source.
- IM occurs in Correa's cascade.
- IM is associated with increased gastric-cancer risk.
- IM may be invisible during endoscopy and detected on biopsy.

### Emerging / Context-dependent
- Magnitude of risk according to distribution and other patient factors.
- Effects of H. pylori eradication on established IM.
- Appropriate surveillance strategy for individual patients.

### Not Established
- IM inevitably progresses to cancer.
- H. pylori eradication reliably reverses established IM.
- A universal surveillance interval.
- A validated individual absolute-risk calculator based on IM alone.

## Evidence Consistency Review

The supplied NCI and ACS materials are directionally consistent:

- IM is a gastric mucosal change.
- It is associated with increased gastric-cancer risk.
- It occurs within Correa's cascade.
- H. pylori is an important risk factor.
- Progression is not inevitable.

The NCI Prevention PDQ adds important nuance regarding H. pylori eradication and IM progression/regression.

No unresolved contradiction affecting the Gold scope was identified.

## Evidence Gaps

1. No validated individual absolute-risk calculator is provided.
2. No universal surveillance interval is established in the supplied NCI evidence.
3. Complete/incomplete IM classification is not sufficiently supported for a dedicated algorithm from the current source set.
4. Exact location/extent-based risk quantification is not established in the current package evidence.
5. Detailed pathology and biopsy mapping are delegated.
