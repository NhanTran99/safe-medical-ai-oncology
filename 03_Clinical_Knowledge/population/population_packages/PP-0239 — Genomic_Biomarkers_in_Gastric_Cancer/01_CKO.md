# Educational Objectives

After reading this Population Package, the reader should be able to:

- Understand what a genomic biomarker is.
- Understand how genomic biomarkers differ from other types of cancer biomarkers.
- Recognize the major genomic biomarker types relevant to gastric cancer.
- Understand representative clinically relevant gastric-cancer genomic biomarkers, including HER2/ERBB2 amplification, MSI/dMMR, TMB-H, NTRK fusions, RET fusions, and BRAF V600E.
- Understand that EBV status is an emerging/context-dependent biomarker rather than a routinely recommended biomarker in all gastric cancers.
- Understand at a high level how genomic biomarkers can be detected.
- Understand why genomic biomarkers may matter for prognosis, treatment prediction, or treatment selection.
- Recognize that a genomic finding does not automatically determine treatment.
- Distinguish somatic tumor biomarkers from inherited (germline) cancer-risk genes.
- Distinguish genomic biomarkers from protein-expression biomarkers such as PD-L1 and CLDN18.2.
- Feel more confident discussing genomic biomarker results with the healthcare team.

# Knowledge Block 1 — What Is a Genomic Biomarker?

## Patient Explanation

A **genomic biomarker** is a measurable feature of a tumor's genetic or genomic information that can provide clinically useful information about the cancer.

Genomic biomarkers can describe different kinds of molecular changes. They are not limited to a single gene mutation.

---

## Clinical Importance

Genomic biomarkers can help doctors characterize a tumor and, in some situations, understand prognosis or identify treatments that may be relevant.

---

## Key Concepts

- Genomic biomarker
- Tumor genome
- Molecular alteration
- Clinical relevance

---

# Knowledge Block 2 — What Types of Genomic Biomarkers Can Be Found?

## Patient Explanation

Genomic biomarkers can include different kinds of changes, such as:

- mutations;
- gene amplifications or other copy-number changes;
- gene fusions;
- genomic instability, such as microsatellite instability (MSI);
- tumor mutational burden (TMB).

Therefore, a genomic biomarker is not simply another name for a gene mutation.

---

## Clinical Importance

Understanding the type of genomic change helps explain what a test result means.

---

# Knowledge Block 3 — Which Genomic Biomarkers Matter in Gastric Cancer?

## Patient Explanation

Several genomic or genome-derived biomarkers have clinical relevance in gastric cancer.

Examples include:

- **HER2/ERBB2 amplification**;
- **MSI/dMMR**;
- **TMB-H**;
- **NTRK gene fusions**;
- **RET gene fusions**;
- **BRAF V600E mutation**.

These biomarkers do not all represent the same type of molecular change.

---

## Clinical Importance

Different genomic findings can have different clinical implications. Their relevance depends on the cancer setting, the test result, available evidence, and current clinical guidance.

---

# Knowledge Block 4 — What Is EBV Status in Gastric Cancer?

## Patient Explanation

Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) status is a molecular characteristic that can help describe some gastric cancers.

However, EBV should not be presented as a routine biomarker that every patient with gastric cancer needs. Current NCCN guidance describes EBV as an **emerging biomarker** and does not recommend routine testing except in selected pathological contexts.

---

## Clinical Importance

This distinction helps separate established clinical biomarkers from biomarkers that remain emerging or context-dependent.

---

# Knowledge Block 5 — How Are Genomic Biomarkers Tested?

## Patient Explanation

Different genomic biomarkers can require different testing methods.

Depending on the biomarker, testing may use approaches such as:

- PCR;
- in situ hybridization (ISH);
- next-generation sequencing (NGS);
- and, for some biomarker dimensions, immunohistochemistry (IHC).

NGS can assess multiple genomic changes at the same time, but **NGS is a testing method, not another name for genomic biomarkers**.

---

## Clinical Importance

The appropriate method depends on what is being measured and the clinical question.

---

# Knowledge Block 6 — Why Do Genomic Biomarkers Matter?

## Patient Explanation

A genomic biomarker may help:

- characterize a tumor;
- identify a molecular subgroup;
- provide prognostic information in some settings;
- predict whether a treatment may be beneficial;
- identify a potentially targetable alteration.

However, a genomic biomarker result is only one part of clinical decision-making.

---

# Knowledge Block 7 — What Does a Genomic Biomarker Mean for Treatment?

## Patient Explanation

Some genomic biomarkers are linked to treatments that may be considered in appropriate clinical settings.

For example, current gastric-cancer guidance identifies biomarker-defined groups for which targeted or biomarker-directed treatments may be relevant.

But:

> **A genomic finding does not automatically mean that a particular treatment is appropriate for an individual patient.**

Doctors also consider cancer stage, pathology, previous treatment, overall health, treatment goals, and current evidence.

---

# Knowledge Block 8 — Genomic Biomarkers Versus Other Biomarkers

## Patient Explanation

Not all important cancer biomarkers are genomic.

### Genomic or genome-derived examples

- HER2/ERBB2 amplification
- BRAF V600E
- NTRK fusion
- RET fusion
- MSI
- TMB

### Protein-expression examples

- PD-L1 expression
- CLDN18.2 expression
- HER2 protein overexpression

### Germline examples

Inherited cancer-risk genes such as **CDH1** belong to a different category because they describe inherited susceptibility rather than a somatic tumor biomarker.

### Blood-based tumor DNA

ctDNA is a way of obtaining tumor-derived molecular information from blood. It is covered separately in the ctDNA Population Package.

---

# Knowledge Block 9 — What Are the Limitations?

## Patient Explanation

Genomic biomarkers have important limitations:

- not every genomic alteration is clinically actionable;
- different tests detect different types of alterations;
- adequate tumor material may be needed;
- some biomarkers are established while others remain emerging;
- tumor heterogeneity can affect what is detected;
- a genomic result requires interpretation in clinical context.

A genomic biomarker therefore provides useful information, but it does not describe every aspect of a person's cancer.

---

# Common Misconceptions

### Myth 1

**“A genomic biomarker is just a gene mutation.”**

**Fact:** Genomic biomarkers can include mutations, amplifications, gene fusions, MSI, and TMB.

### Myth 2

**“All gastric-cancer biomarkers are genomic.”**

**Fact:** Important biomarkers such as PD-L1 and CLDN18.2 are primarily protein-expression biomarkers.

### Myth 3

**“A genomic biomarker automatically tells the doctor which drug to prescribe.”**

**Fact:** A genomic finding may contribute to treatment selection, but treatment decisions require broader clinical context.

### Myth 4

**“Every genomic alteration is actionable.”**

**Fact:** Some findings have established clinical relevance; others do not currently have an established treatment implication.

### Myth 5

**“If a biomarker is discussed in research, it is already a standard clinical biomarker.”**

**Fact:** Some biomarkers remain emerging or context-dependent, such as EBV status in gastric cancer.

### Myth 6

**“Tumor genomic biomarkers are the same as inherited cancer genes.”**

**Fact:** Somatic tumor biomarkers and germline hereditary-risk genes answer different clinical questions.

---

# Key Messages

- Genomic biomarkers are measurable genomic or genome-derived characteristics of a tumor.
- They can include mutations, amplifications, gene fusions, genomic instability, and TMB.
- Important gastric-cancer examples include HER2/ERBB2 amplification, MSI/dMMR, TMB-H, NTRK fusions, RET fusions, and BRAF V600E.
- EBV is an emerging/context-dependent biomarker rather than a routine biomarker for every patient.
- Not every important gastric-cancer biomarker is genomic; PD-L1 and CLDN18.2 are important protein-expression biomarkers.
- Genomic biomarkers may have prognostic, predictive, or treatment-selection relevance.
- A genomic finding does not by itself determine treatment.
- Somatic tumor biomarkers are different from inherited germline cancer-risk genes.
- ctDNA is a separate blood-based pathway for obtaining tumor-derived molecular information.
