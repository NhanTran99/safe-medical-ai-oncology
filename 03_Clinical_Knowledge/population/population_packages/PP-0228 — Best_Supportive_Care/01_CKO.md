# 1. Objective

PP-0228 defines **Best Supportive Care (BSC)** in gastric cancer as an active layer of clinical care that identifies and manages disease-related, treatment-related, nutritional, functional, psychosocial and caregiver burdens.

The package is designed to answer:

> **What does Best Supportive Care actually do for a person with gastric cancer?**

It is intentionally more intervention-oriented than PP-0227 — Palliative Care.

# 4. Clinical Knowledge Blocks

## 4.1 What Is Best Supportive Care?

Best Supportive Care is active care intended to reduce symptoms, complications, treatment burden and functional impairment and to support quality of life.

In the supplied NCCN gastric-cancer guideline, the Principles of Palliative Care/Best Supportive Care section states that the goal is to prevent and relieve suffering and support the best possible quality of life for patients and families regardless of disease stage or need for other therapies.

Therefore:

> **BSC is a treatment layer, not a statement that treatment has stopped.**

---

## 4.2 BSC Is Not “No Treatment”

A common misconception is that BSC means that the healthcare team has stopped doing anything.

That is incorrect.

BSC can include:

- symptom-directed medications;
- nutritional support;
- hydration;
- rehabilitation;
- psychosocial support;
- endoscopic interventions;
- interventional radiology;
- radiation used for palliation;
- selected surgery;
- decompressive procedures;
- caregiver education;
- repeated clinical reassessment.

Some interventions are procedural or invasive.

The purpose is not to eradicate the cancer but to relieve suffering, preserve function, prevent complications and support quality of life.

---

## 4.3 BSC Can Coexist With Anticancer Treatment

BSC can be delivered:

- before systemic treatment;
- during systemic treatment;
- after treatment;
- between treatment lines;
- during recurrence;
- during metastatic disease;
- when treatment is being reassessed.

The NCCN gastric-cancer source explicitly describes systemic therapy and/or chemoradiation together with symptom-directed BSC in advanced disease.

Therefore:

**Anticancer therapy + BSC**

is often the relevant model.

BSC is not automatically an alternative to cancer-directed treatment.

---

## 4.4 BSC Can Sometimes Be the Principal Active Care

In advanced gastric cancer, the clinical balance may favor BSC alone when the expected burden of disease-directed therapy outweighs expected benefit or when performance status is poor.

The supplied NCCN algorithm uses performance status in this decision context.

However, PP-0228 does not make an individual treatment decision.

The correct educational principle is:

> **Whether BSC is used alone or together with anticancer treatment is individualized according to clinical status, expected benefit and burden, and patient goals.**

---

## 4.5 BSC Is Needs-Driven

BSC is not restricted to:

- terminal disease;
- the last days of life;
- patients who have stopped treatment.

Supportive-care needs can occur throughout cancer care.

A patient receiving curative-intent treatment may need:

- nutritional support;
- treatment-related symptom support;
- rehabilitation;
- psychosocial support.

A patient with metastatic disease may need:

- bleeding management;
- obstruction palliation;
- pain control;
- nutrition;
- hydration.

Therefore:

> **The need for BSC is driven by burden and clinical need, not by one disease-stage label.**

# 5. The Supportive-Care Reasoning Model

A central PP-0228 principle is:

**Problem identification**

↓

**Clinical assessment**

↓

**Likely cause**

↓

**Severity / functional impact**

↓

**Patient goals and context**

↓

**Supportive intervention**

↓

**Reassessment**

This sequence is preferable to treating symptoms as isolated labels.

---

## 5.1 Why Assessment Comes First

The same symptom can have different causes.

For example:

### Nausea

May reflect:

- treatment;
- obstruction;
- medication;
- metabolic disturbance;
- another illness.

### Fatigue

May reflect:

- cancer;
- anemia;
- treatment toxicity;
- nutritional deficiency;
- sleep disturbance;
- infection;
- psychological distress;
- deconditioning.

### Pain

May reflect:

- tumor;
- procedure;
- treatment;
- obstruction;
- another medical condition.

Therefore:

> **The symptom does not automatically reveal its cause.**

---

# 6. Bleeding

## 6.1 Why Bleeding Matters

Gastric-cancer bleeding may be:

- acute;
- chronic;
- tumor-related;
- treatment-related.

Acute severe bleeding can present with:

- hematemesis;
- melena;
- hemodynamic compromise.

NCCN recommends prompt endoscopic assessment for acute severe bleeding.

---

## 6.2 Supportive-Care Strategy

At the PP-0228 level, the pathway is:

**Recognize bleeding**

↓

**Assess severity**

↓

**Stabilize when necessary**

↓

**Identify source**

↓

**Choose appropriate hemostatic/palliative intervention**

↓

**Reassess for recurrent bleeding**

Possible intervention categories include:

- endoscopic treatment;
- interventional radiology/angiographic embolization;
- radiation;
- selected palliative surgery.

The NCCN source notes that recurrent bleeding after endoscopic treatment can be common and that other modalities may be needed.

---

## 6.3 What PP-0228 Does Not Own

PP-0228 does not teach:

- injection technique;
- clip deployment;
- argon plasma coagulation technique;
- angiographic catheter technique;
- embolization technique;
- radiation planning;
- gastrectomy technique.

These are intervention-specific domains.

---

# 7. Malignant Gastric Obstruction

## 7.1 Clinical Problem

Malignant gastric obstruction can cause:

- nausea;
- vomiting;
- inability to tolerate oral intake;
- dehydration;
- nutritional deterioration;
- weight loss;
- functional decline;
- major reduction in quality of life.

NCCN identifies the principal goals of palliation as reducing nausea/vomiting and, when possible, restoring oral intake.

---

## 7.2 Supportive-Care Reasoning

**Suspected obstruction**

↓

**Clinical and anatomical assessment**

↓

**Assess severity and nutritional/hydration consequences**

↓

**Determine whether decompression or restoration of passage is feasible**

↓

**Select appropriate intervention**

↓

**Reassess intake, symptoms and function**

Potential intervention categories include:

- endoscopic stenting;
- gastrojejunostomy;
- decompression;
- venting gastrostomy;
- selected feeding access;
- nutritional support.

---

## 7.3 Individualization

There is no single universal obstruction procedure.

Choice depends on factors such as:

- location;
- extent;
- anatomy;
- performance status;
- expected prognosis;
- previous treatment;
- feasibility;
- expected benefit;
- procedural burden;
- patient goals.

---

# 8. Nausea and Vomiting

Nausea and vomiting should be treated as symptoms requiring assessment.

Potential contributors include:

- obstruction;
- chemotherapy;
- radiotherapy;
- medication;
- metabolic abnormalities;
- other disease-related causes.

Therefore:

> **Anti-nausea treatment should not replace evaluation of an important underlying cause.**

PP-0228 owns:

- symptom recognition;
- assessment principle;
- cause-oriented reasoning;
- supportive treatment categories;
- reassessment.

Detailed antiemetic regimens and dosing are excluded.

---

# 9. Pain

Pain is an important supportive-care domain.

Pain may be:

- tumor-related;
- treatment-related;
- procedure-related;
- mixed.

The supportive-care process includes:

1. assess location;
2. assess severity;
3. assess functional impact;
4. identify likely cause;
5. select multimodal support;
6. reassess response and burden.

Potential approaches can include:

- pharmacologic treatment;
- radiation for selected painful disease;
- procedures in selected circumstances;
- rehabilitation;
- psychological support.

Detailed opioid conversion, dose calculation and procedural technique are outside this package.

---

# 10. Fatigue

Fatigue is often multifactorial.

Potential contributors include:

- cancer burden;
- anemia;
- treatment toxicity;
- poor nutrition;
- sleep disturbance;
- infection;
- depression/anxiety;
- deconditioning.

Supportive care therefore asks:

> “Why is this patient fatigued?”

rather than simply:

> “What drug treats fatigue?”

Management may involve:

- addressing reversible causes;
- nutritional support;
- activity and rehabilitation;
- sleep support;
- psychosocial support;
- treatment-burden review.

---

# 11. Nutrition

## 11.1 Why Nutrition Is a Core BSC Domain

Gastric cancer may interfere with nutrition through:

- reduced appetite;
- obstruction;
- nausea/vomiting;
- early satiety;
- pain;
- treatment toxicity;
- altered anatomy after surgery;
- advanced disease.

Nutrition can influence:

- strength;
- function;
- treatment tolerance;
- recovery;
- quality of life.

---

## 11.2 Nutritional Support Framework

**Screen**

↓

**Assess**

↓

**Identify reversible barriers**

↓

**Support oral intake when appropriate**

↓

**Escalate nutritional support when necessary**

↓

**Reassess benefit and burden**

Potential support may include:

- dietary counseling;
- oral nutritional support;
- enteral nutrition;
- parenteral nutrition in selected circumstances;
- management of obstruction or other barriers.

The exact intervention depends on clinical context.

---

## 11.3 Nutrition Is Not “Force Feeding”

Nutritional support should be individualized.

It should consider:

- expected benefit;
- burden;
- prognosis;
- gastrointestinal function;
- goals;
- feasibility;
- patient preferences.

Detailed nutritional prescriptions belong to dedicated nutrition content.

---

# 12. Hydration

Hydration may become problematic because of:

- vomiting;
- poor oral intake;
- obstruction;
- diarrhea;
- treatment effects;
- advanced disease.

Supportive care may involve:

- identifying the cause;
- oral hydration when feasible;
- clinically appropriate fluid replacement;
- monitoring;
- reassessment.

The package does not provide universal IV-fluid prescriptions.

Near end of life, hydration decisions are particularly individualized and must be aligned with goals and clinical circumstances.

---

# 13. Functional Decline

Symptoms are not the only outcome.

Gastric cancer can affect:

- mobility;
- strength;
- independence;
- ability to eat;
- ability to work;
- ability to perform daily activities.

Supportive care therefore includes functional assessment.

---

# 14. Rehabilitation

Rehabilitation may address:

- weakness;
- mobility;
- strength;
- activities of daily living;
- postoperative recovery;
- treatment-related deconditioning.

Prehabilitation and rehabilitation are recognized within broader supportive-care frameworks.

PP-0228 owns the principle and supportive-care interface.

Detailed rehabilitation protocols remain delegated.

---

# 15. Psychosocial Support

Cancer can produce:

- anxiety;
- depression;
- distress;
- uncertainty;
- social isolation;
- financial burden;
- work disruption;
- family-role changes.

Supportive care should recognize these needs and facilitate appropriate support.

Potential contributors include:

- oncology team;
- nurses;
- social workers;
- psychologists/counsellors;
- palliative-care professionals;
- community resources.

Detailed psycho-oncology treatment is outside PP-0228.

---

# 16. Caregiver Support

Caregivers may experience:

- emotional distress;
- physical workload;
- sleep disruption;
- financial burden;
- uncertainty;
- difficulty managing symptoms;
- anticipatory grief.

Supportive care should include:

- caregiver education;
- symptom-monitoring guidance;
- practical support;
- referral when distress is significant;
- communication with the clinical team.

---

# 17. Patient-Reported Symptoms and Early Reporting

Patients and caregivers are important sources of information about symptom burden.

Patients should understand that new or worsening:

- bleeding;
- vomiting;
- inability to eat;
- severe pain;
- dehydration;
- weakness;
- confusion;
- other concerning symptoms

may require prompt medical assessment.

PP-0228 does not establish emergency triage rules for every symptom; those belong to patient-safety and emergency packages.

---

# 18. Palliative Local Interventions as BSC

BSC can include active local or procedural interventions.

Examples include:

- endoscopic palliation;
- radiation for symptom control;
- interventional radiology;
- decompression;
- selected surgery.

The purpose is to:

- relieve symptoms;
- restore function;
- prevent complications;
- improve quality of life.

This does not mean that the intervention is intended to cure the cancer.

---

# 19. BSC and Palliative Systemic Therapy

Palliative systemic therapy is owned by PP-0208.

PP-0228 owns supportive care surrounding treatment.

For example:

**Systemic therapy**

+

**nutrition**

+

**nausea management**

+

**fatigue management**

+

**psychosocial support**

+

**caregiver support**

can coexist.

BSC therefore acts as a clinical layer that accompanies disease-directed therapy.

---

# 20. BSC and Treatment Toxicity

Treatment toxicity is an important source of supportive-care need.

However, PP-0231 owns:

- recognition;
- grading;
- investigation;
- treatment;
- dose modification;
- treatment discontinuation when appropriate.

PP-0228 should only establish the interface:

> **Treatment toxicity may create supportive-care needs.**

Example:

**Chemotherapy → nausea**

PP-0228:
- symptom-support framework.

PP-0231:
- chemotherapy-specific toxicity assessment and management.

This prevents duplication.

---

# 21. BSC and Performance Status

Performance status is relevant because it affects:

- treatment tolerance;
- expected benefit;
- functional burden;
- appropriateness of intensive therapy.

NCCN uses ECOG/Karnofsky performance status within advanced-disease treatment decisions.

PP-0228 should explain performance status conceptually.

It should not use one score as a stand-alone treatment prescription.

---

# 22. BSC and Quality of Life

Quality of life is not simply absence of symptoms.

BSC may support:

- comfort;
- eating;
- mobility;
- independence;
- communication;
- sleep;
- family participation;
- meaningful activities;
- reduced hospital burden.

Therefore supportive care should consider what matters to the patient.

---

# 23. BSC and Reassessment

Supportive care requires reassessment because:

- symptoms change;
- cancer progresses;
- treatment changes;
- interventions have side effects;
- nutrition changes;
- functional status changes;
- patient goals change.

A supportive intervention that no longer provides meaningful benefit may need to be reconsidered.

---

# 24. Multidisciplinary Supportive Care

Supportive care may involve:

- oncologists;
- surgeons;
- radiation oncologists;
- nurses;
- palliative-care clinicians;
- dietitians;
- pharmacists;
- rehabilitation professionals;
- psychologists;
- social workers;
- interventional specialists.

PP-0228 owns the principle that BSC is multidisciplinary.

PP-0232 owns the operational multidisciplinary-management framework.

# 26. Common Misconceptions

## Myth 1 — “Best supportive care means no treatment.”

**Fact:** BSC is active supportive treatment.

## Myth 2 — “BSC is only for terminal cancer.”

**Fact:** Supportive needs can occur throughout cancer care.

## Myth 3 — “BSC means chemotherapy has stopped.”

**Fact:** BSC can be delivered together with systemic therapy.

## Myth 4 — “BSC means pain medication only.”

**Fact:** BSC includes symptom, nutrition, hydration, functional, psychosocial and caregiver support.

## Myth 5 — “BSC cannot include procedures.”

**Fact:** Selected endoscopic, radiologic, radiation and surgical interventions may have supportive/palliative purposes.

## Myth 6 — “Every gastric obstruction should be treated the same way.”

**Fact:** Intervention depends on anatomy, clinical condition, expected benefit and goals.

## Myth 7 — “All nausea is chemotherapy-related.”

**Fact:** Obstruction and other causes may need evaluation.

## Myth 8 — “Nutrition means forcing a patient to eat.”

**Fact:** Nutritional support is individualized and should consider benefit, burden and goals.

## Myth 9 — “Supportive care begins only after anticancer therapy fails.”

**Fact:** It can be integrated before, during and after cancer-directed treatment.

## Myth 10 — “A supportive intervention means the cancer is no longer being treated.”

**Fact:** Supportive interventions can coexist with active anticancer treatment.

# 27. Patient-Facing Explanation

## What is Best Supportive Care?

It is active medical care that helps manage symptoms, complications, nutrition, function, emotional needs and treatment burden.

## Can I receive BSC while receiving cancer treatment?

Yes.

## Does BSC mean my doctors have given up?

No.

## Can BSC include procedures?

Yes, when an intervention can relieve symptoms or restore function.

## What can it help with?

Depending on the situation:

- bleeding;
- obstruction;
- nausea/vomiting;
- pain;
- fatigue;
- nutrition;
- hydration;
- weakness and functional decline;
- emotional distress;
- caregiver needs.

## Does every patient receive the same BSC?

No.

Supportive care is individualized.

---

# 28. Patient Questions

Patients and caregivers may ask:

1. What supportive-care needs do I have right now?
2. Could my symptoms be caused by the cancer or by treatment?
3. What can be done about nausea or vomiting?
4. Could vomiting indicate an obstruction?
5. What should we do if there is bleeding?
6. How can my pain be controlled?
7. Am I at risk of malnutrition or dehydration?
8. Would a dietitian or nutrition team help?
9. What can be done to maintain my strength and independence?
10. Could rehabilitation help?
11. How can treatment side effects be managed?
12. Which symptoms require urgent medical attention?
13. Can supportive care continue while I receive chemotherapy, immunotherapy or targeted therapy?
14. If cancer treatment is stopped, what supportive care will continue?
15. What support is available for my caregiver?

# 29. Clinical Safety Boundary

This package is educational.

It does not:

- prescribe medication;
- calculate doses;
- determine individualized treatment;
- select a specific procedure for an individual patient;
- determine hospice eligibility;
- determine when anticancer treatment should stop;
- provide individualized prognosis;
- replace urgent clinical assessment.

Severe bleeding, inability to keep fluids down, severe pain, marked weakness, confusion, breathing difficulty or other acute deterioration requires appropriate medical assessment.

# 30. Evidence Maturity

## Established / Guideline-Supported

- BSC is an integral component of gastric-cancer care.
- BSC aims to relieve suffering and support quality of life.
- BSC can occur regardless of disease stage when needs exist.
- BSC can coexist with other therapies.
- Symptom assessment is essential.
- Bleeding and obstruction require symptom-directed management.
- Nutrition is a major supportive-care domain.
- Supportive care is multidisciplinary.
- Patient/caregiver education is important.
- Functional and psychosocial needs are relevant.

## Context-Dependent

- specific bleeding intervention;
- stent versus bypass;
- decompression;
- feeding access;
- radiation;
- palliative surgery;
- hydration intensity;
- nutritional escalation;
- rehabilitation intensity;
- place of care.

## Emerging / Context-Dependent

The package does not add unsupported claims for novel supportive interventions.

## Not Established / Excluded

- one universal BSC pathway;
- one universal symptom threshold;
- one universal obstruction intervention;
- one universal bleeding intervention;
- BSC as equivalent to “no treatment.”
