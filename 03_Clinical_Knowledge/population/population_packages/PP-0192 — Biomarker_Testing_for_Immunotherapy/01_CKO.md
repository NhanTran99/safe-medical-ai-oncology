# Educational Objectives

After reading this Population Package, the reader should be able to:

1. Explain why biomarker testing may be performed when immunotherapy is being considered for gastric adenocarcinoma.
2. Understand the difference between a biomarker test and the treatment decision that may follow from its result.
3. Recognize PD-L1 and MSI/MMR as the principal immunotherapy-relevant biomarker-testing domains in the current gastric-cancer guideline framework.
4. Understand why PD-L1 and MSI/MMR are complementary rather than interchangeable tests.
5. Recognize the role of TMB and broader NGS testing as selected molecular-testing considerations rather than universal substitutes for biomarker-specific assays.
6. Understand at a high level why the timing, specimen, and adequacy of biomarker testing matter.
7. Understand that a positive, negative, or inadequate biomarker result does not by itself constitute an individualized treatment recommendation.
8. Recognize EBV as an emerging/contextual biomarker rather than a routine immunotherapy-testing requirement in current NCCN gastric-cancer guidance.
9. Understand why other treatment-relevant biomarkers, such as HER2 and CLDN18.2, may coexist with immunotherapy biomarkers without becoming immunotherapy biomarkers themselves.
10. Use a patient-facing framework for discussing biomarker testing and results with the cancer care team.

# Clinical Knowledge Blocks

## Block 1 — Why Is Biomarker Testing Used Before Immunotherapy?

Immunotherapy does not work in exactly the same way for every gastric cancer.

Tumors differ in their molecular and immune characteristics. Biomarker testing is one way of identifying some of those characteristics before treatment decisions are made.

The central concept is:

> **A biomarker test provides information about the tumor that may help the clinical team decide whether a treatment approach is relevant.**

A biomarker result is therefore an **input into a clinical decision**, not the decision itself.

The NCI patient-facing treatment material states that doctors may suggest biomarker tests to help predict response to certain immunotherapy drugs. The NCCN gastric-cancer guideline provides the disease-specific testing framework for several immunotherapy-relevant biomarkers.

---

## Block 2 — What Is a Predictive Biomarker?

A **predictive biomarker** is a biological characteristic that may help identify patients more or less likely to benefit from a particular treatment.

This is different from a **prognostic biomarker**, which describes information about cancer outcome or risk independently of a particular treatment.

For immunotherapy, the important question is not simply:

> “Is this biomarker associated with a better or worse outcome?”

The more relevant question is:

> **“Does this biomarker provide information that helps determine whether an immunotherapy approach may be appropriate?”**

Even when a biomarker is predictive, it does not guarantee that treatment will work.

A positive biomarker can increase the relevance of a treatment option while the final decision still depends on disease setting, treatment history, other biomarkers, overall health, patient preferences, and the clinical judgment of the treating team.

---

## Block 3 — Which Immunotherapy Biomarkers Are Most Important?

For gastric adenocarcinoma, the current guideline framework places particular emphasis on:

- **PD-L1**;
- **MSI/MMR**.

The NCCN Version 2.2026 gastric-cancer guideline recommends universal MSI testing by PCR/NGS or MMR testing by IHC for newly diagnosed gastric cancer. It also recommends universal PD-L1 IHC testing for newly diagnosed patients who are candidates for treatment with PD-1 or PD-L1 inhibitors.

These tests therefore occupy a central position in the immunotherapy biomarker-testing strategy.

---

## Block 4 — What Is PD-L1 Testing?

**PD-L1** is a protein that participates in an immune-checkpoint pathway.

In gastric cancer, PD-L1 testing evaluates PD-L1 expression in tumor tissue, generally using immunohistochemistry (IHC).

The NCCN framework describes PD-L1 testing as a qualitative IHC assay performed on FFPE tumor tissue and uses a companion-diagnostic framework for identifying patients for PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor treatment.

PD-L1 results may be reported using scoring systems such as the **combined positive score (CPS)**. NCCN also discusses tumor area positivity (TAP) in specified contexts.

The detailed biology, assay methodology, and scoring rules belong to the dedicated PD-L1 testing package and are not reproduced here.

### What PP-0192 owns

> **Why PD-L1 testing is part of immunotherapy biomarker assessment.**

### What PP-0183 owns

> **How PD-L1 is tested and how its score is interpreted.**

---

## Block 5 — Why Does PD-L1 Matter for Immunotherapy?

PD-L1 can provide information relevant to the likelihood that certain immunotherapy approaches will be considered.

The relationship is not simply:

**PD-L1 positive → immunotherapy definitely works.**

The appropriate conceptual chain is:

**PD-L1 testing**

→ **PD-L1 result**

→ **interpretation within the relevant treatment context**

→ **possible contribution to immunotherapy selection**

→ **clinical decision**

NCCN Version 2.2026 demonstrates that PD-L1 criteria can be integrated into different treatment contexts and may be expressed through thresholds such as CPS or TAP for specified regimens.

Therefore, a PD-L1 result should always be read together with the treatment setting rather than as a universal yes/no instruction.

---

## Block 6 — What Are MSI and MMR?

**Microsatellite instability (MSI)** and **mismatch repair (MMR)** are related but distinct ways of assessing abnormalities in the DNA mismatch-repair system.

MSI testing looks for instability in selected microsatellite regions.

MMR testing commonly evaluates the presence or absence of proteins involved in mismatch repair using IHC.

The NCCN gastric-cancer framework recognizes MSI testing by PCR or NGS and MMR testing by IHC.

Detailed MSI/MMR biology, laboratory methods, and interpretation are delegated to PP-0182.

---

## Block 7 — Why Is MSI/MMR Testing Important for Immunotherapy?

MSI-H/dMMR is an important immunotherapy-relevant tumor characteristic.

NCCN recommends universal MSI/MMR testing in newly diagnosed gastric cancer. The guideline also includes immunotherapy options for MSI-H/dMMR tumors in appropriate treatment settings.

This means MSI/MMR information can be important even when the original reason for testing was not limited to immediate immunotherapy selection.

The key patient-facing message is:

> **Knowing whether a gastric cancer is MSI-H or dMMR can provide clinically important information for later treatment planning.**

---

## Block 8 — Are PD-L1 and MSI/MMR the Same Test?

No.

They measure different biological characteristics.

### PD-L1

Provides information about expression of a protein involved in an immune-checkpoint pathway.

### MSI/MMR

Provides information about the tumor's mismatch-repair/microsatellite-instability status.

Because they answer different biological questions, one does not universally replace the other.

A tumor can have an MSI/MMR result that provides important immunotherapy information even if its PD-L1 result is different.

The practical principle is:

> **PD-L1 and MSI/MMR are complementary parts of biomarker-informed immunotherapy planning.**

---

## Block 9 — Does MSI-H/dMMR Replace PD-L1 Testing?

No.

The NCCN framework supports testing of both domains in the relevant gastric-cancer population.

The reason is that:

- MSI/MMR identifies one biological feature;
- PD-L1 identifies another;
- treatment pathways may use one, the other, or both depending on the clinical setting.

Therefore, it would be incorrect to tell a patient:

> “Because MSI/MMR was tested, PD-L1 testing is unnecessary.”

The appropriate decision depends on the clinical context and current guideline recommendations.

---

## Block 10 — What Is TMB?

**Tumor mutational burden (TMB)** describes the number of qualifying mutations identified within a defined amount of tumor DNA.

TMB is usually expressed as mutations per megabase.

TMB is a broader genomic characteristic than PD-L1 expression or MSI/MMR status.

NCCN Version 2.2026 includes TMB assessment within the broader NGS testing framework and identifies TMB-high tumors as a selected immunotherapy-relevant context.

TMB therefore belongs in PP-0192 as a **selected broader molecular-testing consideration**, not as an equivalent universal test for every patient.

Detailed TMB definitions, thresholds, limitations, and interpretation belong to PP-0185.

---

## Block 11 — What Role Does NGS Play?

**Next-generation sequencing (NGS)** can assess multiple molecular events at the same time.

NCCN states that NGS can provide information about several molecular alterations and may include assessment of TMB and MSI.

This creates two complementary testing approaches:

### Biomarker-specific testing

A focused test asks:

> “Is this particular biomarker present or expressed?”

### Broader molecular profiling

NGS asks a wider question:

> “What molecular alterations can be identified across the genes and molecular features included in this assay?”

Both approaches can be useful.

However:

> **NGS should not be presented as a universal replacement for every biomarker-specific assay.**

NCCN continues to identify IHC/ISH/targeted gene PCR as preferred initial approaches for specified biomarkers, while NGS may be considered in selected clinical circumstances.

---

## Block 12 — Does Every Patient Need TMB or Broad NGS?

Not necessarily.

The current framework distinguishes between:

- core biomarker testing;
- selected broader molecular profiling.

PD-L1 and MSI/MMR have a much more established routine testing position in gastric cancer.

TMB and broader NGS can provide additional information in selected circumstances.

Therefore, the patient-facing message should be:

> **More testing is not automatically better testing. The useful test is the one that answers a clinically relevant question in the appropriate context.**

---

## Block 13 — What About EBV?

**Epstein-Barr virus (EBV)** is associated with a biologically distinct subset of gastric cancers.

However, the current NCCN gastric-cancer framework treats EBV as an **emerging biomarker** rather than a routine biomarker that should be tested universally for immunotherapy selection.

The project Registry separately assigns EBV-associated gastric cancer and EBV testing to a dedicated package.

Therefore PP-0192 may explain:

> **EBV is biologically relevant and may have immunologic implications, but it is not currently equivalent to routine PD-L1 or MSI/MMR testing in the gastric-cancer immunotherapy-testing framework.**

Detailed EBV testing belongs to the dedicated EBV package.

---

## Block 14 — Why Might HER2 or CLDN18.2 Appear in the Same Treatment Discussion?

A gastric cancer may have several biomarkers at the same time.

For example:

- HER2 may influence targeted-treatment planning.
- CLDN18.2 may influence targeted-treatment planning.
- PD-L1 may contribute to immunotherapy planning.
- MSI/MMR may provide immunotherapy-relevant information.
- TMB or other genomic findings may provide additional information.

These biomarkers do not all belong to the same biological category.

They are part of a broader **precision-oncology profile**.

The patient-facing principle is:

> **The cancer care team may consider several biomarker results together because more than one treatment pathway may depend on the tumor's molecular characteristics.**

Detailed HER2 and CLDN18.2 testing remain delegated to their dedicated packages.

---

## Block 15 — Why Does Testing Need to Happen Early?

Some biomarker results may be needed when treatment planning reaches a particular decision point.

NCCN recommends universal MSI/MMR testing for newly diagnosed gastric cancer and PD-L1 testing in newly diagnosed patients who are candidates for PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor treatment.

This means testing is not necessarily something that should wait until the day a treatment is about to start.

The broader principle is:

> **Testing early enough to have reliable results available when treatment decisions require them can prevent avoidable delays and repeat procedures.**

The exact timing still depends on disease stage, treatment plan, specimen availability, and the clinical team.

---

## Block 16 — What Specimen Is Used?

Many core immunotherapy biomarkers are evaluated using tumor tissue.

NCCN describes:

- MMR testing on FFPE tissue;
- PD-L1 testing on FFPE tumor tissue;
- specific specimen adequacy considerations for PD-L1 evaluation.

The specimen must contain enough suitable material for the test to answer its intended question.

A specimen that is technically inadequate is not the same as a specimen that is adequate and produces a negative result.

---

## Block 17 — Why Does Tissue Adequacy Matter?

A biomarker test can only evaluate the biological material that is actually available.

Problems such as:

- too little tumor tissue;
- insufficient tumor cells;
- poor specimen quality;
- inadequate material for the specific assay;

can make a result difficult or impossible to interpret.

Therefore:

> **“No result” and “negative result” are not the same thing.**

This distinction is especially important for patient understanding.

The detailed assay-specific adequacy rules belong to the relevant biomarker-testing packages.

---

## Block 18 — Can Blood-Based Testing Be Used?

Blood-based molecular testing can sometimes provide additional genomic information.

NCCN recognizes blood-based ctDNA testing as a form of liquid biopsy and describes selected circumstances in which blood-based genomic testing may be considered, including limited tissue or situations where traditional biopsy is not feasible.

However, blood-based molecular testing should not be presented as a universal replacement for tissue-based PD-L1 or MSI/MMR testing.

Different tests examine different biological material and may answer different questions.

Detailed ctDNA and liquid-biopsy concepts are delegated to dedicated packages.

---

## Block 19 — What Is a Companion Diagnostic?

A **companion diagnostic** is a test associated with the use of a particular treatment and intended to help identify patients for whom that treatment may be appropriate.

NCCN explicitly describes a companion-diagnostic framework for PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor treatment and specifies the use of FFPE tumor tissue for PD-L1 assessment.

At the patient-facing level, the key concept is:

> **Some treatment decisions depend not only on the biomarker itself but also on using an appropriate validated testing approach.**

Detailed regulatory and companion-diagnostic methodology is outside PP-0192.

---

## Block 20 — What Does a Positive Biomarker Result Mean?

A positive result means that the feature being tested was identified according to the test's definition and interpretation framework.

It does not mean:

- the cancer will definitely respond;
- the treatment must be given;
- all other biomarkers become irrelevant;
- the result is sufficient without considering the disease setting.

The result is one piece of information used by the cancer care team.

---

## Block 21 — What Does a Negative Biomarker Result Mean?

A negative result generally means that the biomarker was not identified according to the test and criteria used.

It should not automatically be interpreted as:

> “Immunotherapy can never work.”

Different biomarkers answer different questions.

For example, a low or negative PD-L1 result does not erase information from MSI/MMR testing.

Likewise, an MSI/MMR result does not describe PD-L1 expression.

A negative result therefore needs to be interpreted within the complete biomarker and clinical context.

---

## Block 22 — What Does an Inadequate or Indeterminate Result Mean?

An inadequate or indeterminate result means that the test did not provide a sufficiently reliable answer.

Possible reasons may include:

- insufficient tissue;
- inadequate tumor content;
- technical limitations;
- specimen quality;
- assay-specific limitations.

This is different from a true negative result.

The next step may involve review of the specimen, another test, another specimen, or a different testing approach depending on the clinical situation.

The package does not prescribe which option should be chosen for an individual patient.

---

## Block 23 — Can Biomarker Results Be Discordant?

Yes.

Different tests may sometimes produce different findings.

Reasons can include:

- different specimens;
- different assays;
- different biological features being measured;
- tumor heterogeneity;
- changes in the tumor over time;
- technical limitations.

A discordant result does not automatically mean that one laboratory is wrong.

It means the clinical team may need to understand what each test actually measured and which result is relevant to the current clinical decision.

---

## Block 24 — Why Are Biomarkers Considered Together?

Precision oncology is increasingly based on combinations of information rather than a single isolated marker.

A patient may have:

**PD-L1 result**

+

**MSI/MMR result**

+

**HER2 result**

+

**CLDN18.2 result**

+

**other molecular information**

The clinical team then asks:

> **Which of these findings are relevant to the treatment decisions that apply to this particular clinical setting?**

This is why biomarker testing should be viewed as a coordinated testing strategy rather than a collection of unrelated laboratory tests.

---

## Block 25 — Does a Biomarker Result Automatically Determine Treatment?

No.

A biomarker result can be highly informative while still requiring clinical interpretation.

Treatment decisions can also depend on:

- stage;
- resectability;
- disease location;
- prior treatments;
- performance status;
- comorbidities;
- patient preferences;
- available treatment options;
- current guidelines;
- clinical trials.

Therefore:

> **A biomarker result informs a treatment decision; it does not replace the treatment decision.**

---

## Block 26 — Does Biomarker Testing Guarantee Immunotherapy Response?

No.

Even a biomarker that is strongly associated with benefit does not guarantee that an individual patient will respond.

Cancer is biologically heterogeneous.

The test measures a defined feature. It does not measure every factor that determines whether a tumor will respond.

This is one reason biomarker testing should be described as **predictive information**, not a promise of treatment success.

---

## Block 27 — Why Can Biomarker Testing Be More Than One Test?

Different tests answer different questions.

For example:

| Test | Main question |
|---|---|
| PD-L1 | Is PD-L1 expression present according to the relevant assay/scoring framework? |
| MSI/MMR | Is the tumor MSI-H or dMMR according to the testing method? |
| TMB | Is the tumor's mutational burden high according to the relevant assay/definition? |
| HER2 | Is HER2 overexpression/amplification present according to gastric-cancer testing criteria? |
| CLDN18.2 | Is CLDN18.2 expression present according to the relevant testing criteria? |
| NGS | Which molecular alterations are detectable across the assay's broader scope? |

The tests are therefore complementary rather than redundant.

---

## Block 28 — What Happens After Biomarker Testing?

The conceptual pathway is:

**Clinical question**

↓

**Biomarker testing**

↓

**Laboratory result**

↓

**Biomarker-specific interpretation**

↓

**Integration with stage, treatment history and other clinical factors**

↓

**Treatment decision**

PP-0192 owns the **testing strategy layer**.

Dedicated biomarker packages own detailed interpretation.

Downstream immunotherapy packages own treatment application.

This separation prevents a biomarker-testing package from becoming an individualized treatment algorithm.

---

## Block 29 — What Should I Ask My Cancer Care Team?

Useful questions include:

1. Which immunotherapy-related biomarkers have been tested?
2. Were PD-L1 and MSI/MMR assessed?
3. Was the specimen adequate for each test?
4. If a test was negative, what exactly was tested and by which method?
5. If a test was inadequate, is there enough tissue for another test?
6. Does the current treatment plan depend on a particular biomarker result?
7. Are broader molecular tests such as NGS or TMB relevant in my situation?
8. Are there other treatment-relevant biomarkers, such as HER2 or CLDN18.2, that should be considered?
9. Is a companion diagnostic required for the treatment being considered?
10. Do the results need to be interpreted together rather than separately?
11. Would another specimen or another testing method add clinically useful information?
12. Which result is most relevant to the treatment decision being discussed now?

These questions help patients participate in the decision without requiring them to interpret the tests themselves.

# Clinical Importance

## Why This Package Matters

Immunotherapy biomarker testing is an important bridge between **molecular characterization** and **treatment selection**.

Without a clear testing strategy, patients may encounter several isolated test names—PD-L1, MSI, MMR, TMB, NGS, HER2, CLDN18.2—and reasonably wonder whether all of them are the same type of test or whether one positive result determines treatment.

PP-0192 provides the organizing framework:

> **Different biomarkers answer different clinical questions.**

The most important current gastric-cancer immunotherapy-testing domains are PD-L1 and MSI/MMR.

Broader testing such as TMB and NGS can add information in selected contexts.

Other biomarkers may matter because treatment planning can involve more than one therapeutic pathway.

---

# Key Concepts

## Concept 1 — Testing is not treatment

A biomarker test produces information.

Treatment selection is a separate clinical decision.

---

## Concept 2 — PD-L1 and MSI/MMR are complementary

They evaluate different biological characteristics and should not be treated as interchangeable.

---

## Concept 3 — Universal testing and selected testing are different

NCCN places MSI/MMR testing in a universal newly diagnosed framework and PD-L1 testing in a universal framework for newly diagnosed patients who are candidates for PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors.

TMB and broader NGS occupy a more selected role.

---

## Concept 4 — NGS is broad, not automatically better

A broader test can identify more types of information, but the usefulness of that information depends on the clinical question.

---

## Concept 5 — Negative is not the same as inadequate

A negative test answered its intended question and did not identify the biomarker.

An inadequate test may not have been able to answer the question reliably.

---

## Concept 6 — Biomarkers work in context

A result should be interpreted in relation to:

- assay;
- specimen;
- disease setting;
- other biomarkers;
- treatment history;
- current clinical question.

---

## Concept 7 — A biomarker is not a guarantee

A predictive biomarker may identify a group more likely to benefit, but no biomarker guarantees an individual treatment response.

# Common Misconceptions

## Myth 1 — “PD-L1 is the only immunotherapy biomarker.”

**Fact:** MSI/MMR is also a major immunotherapy-relevant testing domain, and selected broader molecular testing can provide additional information.

## Myth 2 — “If PD-L1 is negative, immunotherapy is impossible.”

**Fact:** Treatment relevance can depend on other biomarkers and clinical circumstances, including MSI-H/dMMR status.

## Myth 3 — “If MSI/MMR is negative, immunotherapy cannot work.”

**Fact:** MSI/MMR is one component of biomarker-informed treatment planning.

## Myth 4 — “PD-L1 positive means immunotherapy will definitely work.”

**Fact:** A biomarker can support treatment consideration without guaranteeing response.

## Myth 5 — “Every patient needs TMB testing.”

**Fact:** TMB is a selected broader molecular-testing consideration rather than equivalent to the routine PD-L1/MSI/MMR framework.

## Myth 6 — “NGS replaces PD-L1 IHC.”

**Fact:** NGS and biomarker-specific assays answer different questions.

## Myth 7 — “EBV is already a routine immunotherapy biomarker.”

**Fact:** Current NCCN guidance treats EBV as an emerging biomarker rather than a routine clinical immunotherapy-testing requirement.

## Myth 8 — “A failed test is the same as a negative test.”

**Fact:** An inadequate test may not have answered the clinical question.

## Myth 9 — “One biomarker result determines the entire treatment plan.”

**Fact:** Treatment planning integrates biomarker results with clinical setting and other patient information.

## Myth 10 — “More biomarker tests are always better.”

**Fact:** Testing should be driven by clinically relevant questions and appropriate evidence.

## Myth 11 — “A biomarker result is a prescription.”

**Fact:** Biomarker information contributes to clinical decision-making but does not independently prescribe treatment.

## Myth 12 — “Biomarker testing only matters after the cancer becomes advanced.”

**Fact:** NCCN recommends early testing for important biomarker domains so relevant information can be available when treatment planning requires it.

# Key Messages

1. **Biomarker testing helps identify tumor characteristics that may matter for immunotherapy.**
2. **PD-L1 and MSI/MMR are the core immunotherapy-relevant testing domains in the current gastric-cancer framework.**
3. **PD-L1 and MSI/MMR answer different biological questions and are complementary.**
4. **TMB and NGS can provide broader molecular information in selected circumstances.**
5. **EBV is biologically relevant but is not currently a routine immunotherapy-testing requirement in NCCN gastric-cancer guidance.**
6. **HER2 and CLDN18.2 may be important to the overall treatment plan without being immunotherapy biomarkers themselves.**
7. **An adequate negative test is different from an inadequate or failed test.**
8. **A biomarker result is an input into treatment planning, not an automatic treatment order.**
9. **A positive predictive biomarker does not guarantee treatment response.**
10. **The final meaning of a biomarker result depends on the clinical context and the current treatment question.**
