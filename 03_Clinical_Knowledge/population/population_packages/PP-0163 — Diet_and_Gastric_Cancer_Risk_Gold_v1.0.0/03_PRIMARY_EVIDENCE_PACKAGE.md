## Clinical Question

> How does diet affect gastric-cancer risk, which dietary patterns or foods are associated with higher or lower risk, and what can people reasonably do about diet for gastric-cancer prevention?

## Evidence Scope

This package focuses on gastric-cancer-specific dietary risk and prevention. It deliberately separates:
1. dietary associations;
2. evidence strength;
3. plausible risk-reduction direction;
4. claims that remain unproven.

## Primary Evidence Sources

### NCI — Stomach (Gastric) Cancer Prevention PDQ®

The strongest direct prevention source for this PP.

NCI classifies the dietary evidence under interventions with inadequate evidence to establish whether they reduce stomach-cancer risk. It reports:
- consistent association between high salt intake and gastric-cancer risk;
- association between increased fresh fruit/vegetable intake and decreased gastric-cancer rates;
- association of dietary vitamin C, whole grains, carotenoids, allium compounds, and green tea with lower risk;
- uncertainty about whether changing diet directly reduces gastric-cancer incidence.

NCI also summarizes randomized supplement trials that did not establish a significant gastric-cancer incidence reduction.

### NCI — Causes of Stomach Cancer

Supports:
- low fruit/vegetable diet as a risk factor;
- high intake of salted, smoked, or poorly preserved foods as a risk factor.

### NCI — Genetics of Gastric Cancer (PDQ®)

Supports:
- regional variation influenced by diet;
- salt-preserved foods;
- low fruit intake;
- large amounts of processed, grilled, or barbecued meats/fish;
- migrant effects consistent with environmental influences.

### NCCN Gastric Cancer Version 2.2026

Supports:
- high salt intake and other dietary factors within the environmental-risk framework;
- relevance particularly to intestinal-type gastric cancer.

### ACS Stomach Cancer materials

Supports:
- healthy dietary pattern with fruits, vegetables, whole grains;
- limiting highly processed foods and red/processed meats in broader health guidance;
- uncertainty about whether diet changes prevent progression or recurrence;
- lack of established benefit from dietary supplements for preventing progression/recurrence.

## Key Evidence

### Salt

NCI reports a consistent association between high salt intake and gastric-cancer risk. The evidence is based on correlation and case-control/cohort data rather than a gastric-cancer prevention RCT.

**Clinical wording:** reduce excessive salt intake.

**Not supported:** a gastric-cancer-specific numeric salt threshold.

### Fruits and Vegetables

NCI reports epidemiologic evidence associating increased fresh fruit and vegetable intake with decreased gastric-cancer rates. It explicitly notes the absence of randomized controlled trials establishing causal association.

**Clinical wording:** maintain a dietary pattern containing fresh fruits and vegetables.

**Not supported:** “vegetables prevent gastric cancer.”

### Salted, Smoked, and Poorly Preserved Foods

NCI identifies these categories as risk-associated.

**Clinical wording:** limit heavily salted, smoked, and poorly preserved foods.

### Other Dietary Factors

NCI reports lower-risk associations for whole-grain cereals, carotenoids, allium compounds, green tea, and dietary vitamin C. These should remain supporting evidence rather than individual preventive prescriptions.

### Supplements

NCI summarizes randomized trials of antioxidant/vitamin supplementation. These did not demonstrate a statistically significant reduction in gastric-cancer incidence.

Therefore:
> food-based dietary quality should not be equated with taking vitamin or antioxidant supplements.

## Evidence Strength and Causality

### Stronger evidence within this PP
- consistent observational association for high salt;
- repeated association for salted/smoked/poorly preserved foods;
- repeated association for low fruit/vegetable intake.

### Important limitation
Most diet evidence is observational.

Therefore:
- association ≠ proven causation;
- risk association ≠ guaranteed prevention from dietary modification;
- one food ≠ a validated cancer-prevention intervention.

This distinction is central to PP-0163.

## Diet and H. pylori

The sources support a multifactorial environmental framework in which diet and H. pylori can both contribute to gastric carcinogenesis.

This PP does not:
- estimate combined risk;
- replace H. pylori testing;
- replace H. pylori eradication.

## Geographic / Migrant Evidence

NCI describes regional differences in gastric-cancer incidence influenced by diet and notes that gastric-cancer incidence among immigrants can move toward that of the host country over one or two generations.

This supports the importance of environmental exposures but does not justify individualized dietary risk prediction.

## Practical Evidence-Based Direction

A reasonable, non-prescriptive risk-reduction direction is:

**Reduce**
- excessive salt;
- heavily salted foods;
- smoked foods;
- poorly preserved foods;
- potentially high amounts of processed foods/meats.

**Maintain / increase as part of a healthy dietary pattern**
- fresh fruits;
- vegetables;
- whole grains and other nutrient-rich foods.

**Do not assume**
- garlic;
- green tea;
- vitamin C supplements;
- antioxidant supplements

are proven gastric-cancer prevention treatments.

## Evidence Gaps

1. No validated gastric-cancer-specific individual dietary risk calculator in the supplied sources.
2. No gastric-cancer-specific numeric salt threshold supported as a prevention target in the current source set.
3. No single dietary pattern proven by randomized trial to prevent gastric-cancer incidence.
4. No basis for universal claims about individual foods.
5. No evidence in the supplied sources supporting dietary change as a proven method to prevent gastric-cancer recurrence/progression.
