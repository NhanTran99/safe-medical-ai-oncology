## Objective

Explain which dietary exposures are associated with gastric-cancer risk, how strong the evidence is, what dietary patterns are reasonably supported for risk reduction, and what should not be overclaimed.

## Atomic Clinical Question

> How does diet affect gastric-cancer risk, which dietary patterns or foods are associated with higher or lower risk, and what can people reasonably do about diet for gastric-cancer prevention?

## Scope

## Clinical Knowledge Blocks

### KB1 — Why Diet Matters
Diet is part of the environmental-risk framework for gastric cancer. NCI identifies low fruit and vegetable intake and high intake of salted, smoked, or poorly preserved foods as risk-associated dietary patterns.

### KB2 — High Salt Intake
NCI Prevention PDQ reports a consistent association between high salt intake and gastric-cancer risk. This is an important risk association but should not be converted into a claim that salt independently causes cancer in every individual.

### KB3 — Salt-Preserved Foods
Foods preserved with salt are associated with increased gastric-cancer risk. This belongs within the core dietary-risk message.

### KB4 — Smoked and Poorly Preserved Foods
NCI states that diets high in salted, smoked, or poorly preserved foods may increase stomach-cancer risk.

### KB5 — Low Fruit and Vegetable Intake
Low consumption of fruits and vegetables is associated with increased gastric-cancer risk.

### KB6 — Fresh Fruits and Vegetables
Epidemiologic evidence suggests increased intake of fresh fruits and vegetables is associated with lower gastric-cancer rates. NCI notes that randomized trials have not established a causal association.

### KB7 — Whole Grains and Other Potentially Protective Factors
NCI reports associations between lower gastric-cancer risk and diets high in whole-grain cereals, carotenoids, allium compounds, and green tea. These remain supporting associations rather than proven single-food interventions.

### KB8 — Processed, Grilled, or Barbecued Foods
NCI Genetics PDQ reports that large amounts of processed, grilled, or barbecued meats/fish may increase risk. This should remain supporting context rather than an exhaustive food-specific causal list.

### KB9 — Vitamin C From Food
Dietary vitamin C contained in fruits, vegetables, and other plant foods is associated with reduced gastric-cancer risk. This does not establish that vitamin C supplements prevent gastric cancer.

### KB10 — Green Tea and Allium
These have been associated with lower risk in epidemiologic evidence, but current sources do not support presenting green tea or garlic/allium as proven preventive treatments.

### KB11 — Dietary Supplements
Randomized trials summarized by NCI did not establish a statistically significant reduction in gastric-cancer incidence from antioxidant/vitamin supplementation strategies. Food-based dietary patterns should not be equated with supplement use.

### KB12 — Diet and H. pylori
Diet and H. pylori can coexist as environmental contributors to gastric carcinogenesis. This package does not provide a combined-risk calculator or replace H. pylori prevention/eradication packages.

### KB13 — Geographic and Migrant Context
NCI describes regional variation and migrant effects consistent with environmental influences, including diet. This is contextual rather than a basis for individualized risk prediction.

### KB14 — Association Versus Causation
Most dietary gastric-cancer evidence in the supplied sources is observational. This is a critical distinction from interventions supported by randomized prevention evidence, such as H. pylori eradication.

### KB15 — Is There a Gastric-Cancer Prevention Diet?
No single diet has been proven by the supplied evidence to prevent gastric cancer. A reasonable risk-reduction direction is to reduce excessive salt and salted/smoked/poorly preserved foods while maintaining a healthy dietary pattern containing fresh fruits and vegetables and other nutrient-rich foods.

### KB16 — What Can a Person Reasonably Do?
Use the evidence as a risk-reduction direction, not a guarantee:
- reduce excessive salt;
- limit heavily salted, smoked, and poorly preserved foods;
- include fresh fruits and vegetables;
- favor a generally healthy dietary pattern;
- do not substitute supplements for dietary quality;
- address other gastric-cancer risk factors separately.

## Patient Explanation

> There is no single diet proven to prevent stomach cancer. However, some dietary patterns are associated with stomach-cancer risk. Eating a lot of salt or foods that are heavily salted, smoked, or poorly preserved is associated with higher risk, while eating more fresh fruits and vegetables is associated with lower risk.
>
> The important limitation is that much of the evidence about diet comes from observational studies. This means these foods or patterns are associated with risk, but it does not prove that changing one food by itself will prevent cancer.
>
> A reasonable approach is to reduce excessive salt and heavily preserved foods and maintain a healthy dietary pattern with plenty of fresh fruits and vegetables. Dietary supplements should not be assumed to provide the same benefit.

## Common Misconceptions

### Myth 1
“Eating vegetables prevents gastric cancer.”

**Fact:** Higher fruit and vegetable intake is associated with lower risk, but randomized evidence has not established that increasing these foods alone prevents gastric cancer.

### Myth 2
“Salt directly causes gastric cancer in everyone.”

**Fact:** High salt intake is consistently associated with gastric-cancer risk, but individual cancer development is multifactorial.

### Myth 3
“Garlic or green tea prevents gastric cancer.”

**Fact:** These have epidemiologic associations with lower risk, but they are not established as proven preventive treatments.

### Myth 4
“Vitamin supplements prevent gastric cancer.”

**Fact:** Randomized supplement trials have not established a significant reduction in gastric-cancer incidence.

### Myth 5
“There is one special gastric-cancer prevention diet.”

**Fact:** No single validated prevention diet is established by the current source set.

### Myth 6
“If I change my diet, I no longer need to consider H. pylori or other risk factors.”

**Fact:** Gastric-cancer risk is multifactorial.

### Myth 7
“A healthy diet is proven to prevent recurrence after gastric-cancer treatment.”

**Fact:** The supplied ACS material notes that it is not known whether dietary changes prevent progression or recurrence.

## Key Messages

- Diet is an important environmental component of gastric-cancer risk.
- High salt intake is consistently associated with increased risk.
- Salted, smoked, and poorly preserved foods are associated with increased risk.
- Low fruit and vegetable intake is associated with increased risk.
- Fresh fruits and vegetables are associated with lower risk.
- Whole grains and selected dietary factors have supporting but heterogeneous evidence.
- Dietary supplements should not be equated with food-based dietary patterns.
- Most dietary evidence is observational.
- There is no single proven gastric-cancer prevention diet.
- Practical dietary change should be framed as risk reduction, not a guarantee.
