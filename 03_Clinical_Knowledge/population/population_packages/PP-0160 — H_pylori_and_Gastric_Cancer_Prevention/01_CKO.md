## Educational Objective

Explain why chronic Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) infection is an established cause of gastric adenocarcinoma, how chronic infection contributes to the gastric carcinogenic pathway, what the evidence shows about cancer prevention through eradication, how benefit varies with baseline risk and clinical context, why residual risk may remain after eradication, and why prevention must be balanced with appropriate antibiotic stewardship.

## Atomic Clinical Question

> How does H. pylori contribute to gastric cancer risk, and what does H. pylori-related gastric cancer prevention mean?

## Core Scope

### Included

- Strongest relationship with noncardia gastric carcinoma.
- Chronic infection and chronic gastric inflammation.

## Clinical Knowledge Blocks

### KB1 — What Is H. pylori and Why Does It Matter for Gastric Cancer?

H. pylori is a common gastric bacterium. Chronic infection produces long-lasting gastric inflammation and can lead to atrophic gastritis and gastric adenocarcinoma. NCI identifies chronic H. pylori infection as an accepted cause of gastric adenocarcinoma and IARC classifies it as a Group 1 human carcinogen.

### KB2 — How Does H. pylori Contribute to Gastric Cancer?

The conceptual pathway is:

**H. pylori infection**
→ chronic gastritis/inflammation
→ gastric atrophy
→ intestinal metaplasia
→ dysplasia
→ gastric adenocarcinoma.

This is a prevention framework, not a deterministic sequence that every infected person follows.

### KB3 — Which Gastric Cancers Are Most Strongly Linked to H. pylori?

The strongest established causal relationship is with **noncardia gastric carcinoma**, particularly carcinogenesis involving the gastric body and antrum. H. pylori is also causally related to gastric low-grade MALT lymphoma, but lymphoma is supporting context rather than the primary focus of this package.

### KB4 — Can H. pylori Eradication Prevent Gastric Cancer?

Yes. Randomized and meta-analytic evidence summarized by NCI shows that eradication can reduce subsequent gastric-cancer incidence.

A meta-analysis of seven randomized studies estimated gastric-cancer risk reduction from approximately 1.7% to 1.1% (RR 0.65, 95% CI 0.43–0.98). Most studies were conducted in high-risk Asian populations, so generalizability varies by population.

### KB5 — What Does the Long-Term Randomized Evidence Show?

In the Shandong randomized trial, short-term H. pylori treatment was associated with a 39% reduction in gastric-cancer incidence over 15 years. With 22-year follow-up, reductions in gastric-cancer incidence and mortality were also observed.

NCI's patient-facing summary describes the long-term reduction in incidence as nearly 50% over 22 years.

### KB6 — Does Prevention Benefit Asymptomatic Infected People?

Evidence supports benefit in healthy asymptomatic infected individuals. This is important because cancer prevention is not limited to people who have peptic ulcers or other H. pylori-related symptoms.

### KB7 — Does Family History Matter?

Family history independently increases gastric-cancer risk. NCI summarizes a randomized trial in people with a first-degree relative with gastric cancer in which eradication reduced gastric-cancer incidence from 2.7% to 1.2% (HR 0.45, 95% CI 0.21–0.94).

Family history therefore provides a useful example of how baseline risk can influence the potential absolute value of prevention.

### KB8 — Can Eradication Help After Early Gastric Cancer?

Yes. In people who underwent endoscopic treatment for early gastric cancer, eradication has been associated with a lower risk of metachronous gastric cancer. This is a secondary-prevention context.

### KB9 — Does Eradication Eliminate Gastric-Cancer Risk?

No.

Eradication removes the ongoing H. pylori infection but does not guarantee that gastric cancer will never develop. People with established advanced gastric mucosal damage, such as atrophic gastritis or intestinal metaplasia, may retain elevated future risk.

### KB10 — Why Might Timing Matter?

Eradication can remove an important carcinogenic stimulus before more advanced precursor changes develop. The evidence does not support a single universal age or “point of no return.” The important concept is that prevention may be more effective before irreversible or persistent precancerous changes are established.

### KB11 — What If Atrophic Gastritis or Intestinal Metaplasia Is Already Present?

These conditions represent downstream gastric mucosal changes associated with chronic H. pylori-related injury. Eradication may still be clinically relevant, but removal of the infection does not necessarily erase established mucosal damage or residual cancer risk.

Detailed management of these precursor states belongs to PP-0165 and PP-0166.

### KB12 — Should Everyone Be Tested and Treated?

No universal “test everyone” rule should be inferred from this PP.

NCI notes that available evidence does not support widespread testing and eradication in all people and that unnecessary treatment may contribute to antimicrobial resistance. Testing and treatment recommendations depend on clinical context and applicable guidance.

### KB13 — Why Does Antibiotic Stewardship Matter?

H. pylori eradication uses antibacterial therapy. Treating people without an appropriate indication can contribute to resistance in H. pylori and other microorganisms.

Therefore prevention must balance:

**potential gastric-cancer prevention benefit**
against
**appropriate antimicrobial use**.

### KB14 — What About Reinfection and Family Transmission?

Transmission and recurrence patterns vary by population and remain incompletely understood. A small randomized trial suggested that untreated infected household members may contribute to recurrent infection in an index patient.

This is supporting context, not a universal family-eradication protocol.

### KB15 — Primary Versus Secondary Prevention

**Primary prevention:**

H. pylori-infected person without gastric cancer
→ appropriate eradication
→ lower future gastric-cancer risk.

**Secondary prevention:**

Person treated for early gastric cancer
→ H. pylori eradication
→ lower risk of metachronous gastric cancer.

### KB16 — What Does H. pylori Prevention Mean in Practice?

The prevention concept is:

**Recognize appropriate H. pylori infection**
→ **eradicate infection when clinically indicated**
→ **reduce future gastric-cancer risk**
→ **continue risk-appropriate management when residual risk remains**.

Exact eradication regimens and test-of-cure belong to PP-0161.

### KB17 — What H. pylori Prevention Does Not Mean

It does not mean:

- every infected person has or will develop cancer;
- eradication guarantees zero cancer risk;
- eradication reverses all established gastric damage;
- one testing/treatment policy applies to every population;
- H. pylori eradication replaces management of other gastric-cancer risk factors.

## Patient Explanation

> H. pylori is a common stomach infection, but chronic infection can cause long-lasting inflammation and contribute to the development of gastric cancer. It is one of the clearest preventable causes of gastric cancer.
>
> Treating H. pylori can lower the chance of developing gastric cancer, including in some people who have no symptoms. The benefit may be particularly meaningful in populations with higher baseline gastric-cancer risk.
>
> However, treatment does not guarantee that cancer will never develop. If the stomach has already developed important precancerous changes such as atrophy or intestinal metaplasia, some risk may remain. Treatment decisions should therefore be based on the person's clinical context and appropriate medical guidance, while avoiding unnecessary antibiotic exposure.

## Common Misconceptions

### Myth 1

**“H. pylori is only a cause of ulcers, not cancer.”**

**Fact:** Chronic H. pylori infection is an established cause of gastric adenocarcinoma, particularly noncardia gastric carcinoma.

### Myth 2

**“If I have no symptoms, H. pylori does not matter.”**

**Fact:** Gastric-cancer prevention evidence includes asymptomatic infected individuals.

### Myth 3

**“Eradicating H. pylori guarantees that I will never get gastric cancer.”**

**Fact:** Eradication lowers risk but does not eliminate all future gastric-cancer risk.

### Myth 4

**“Eradication reverses all damage already caused by H. pylori.”**

**Fact:** Established atrophic or metaplastic changes may persist and can continue to carry risk.

### Myth 5

**“Everyone should automatically be tested and treated.”**

**Fact:** NCI notes that evidence does not support indiscriminate widespread testing and eradication, and unnecessary treatment can contribute to antimicrobial resistance.

### Myth 6

**“H. pylori prevention means taking antibiotics indefinitely.”**

**Fact:** Prevention is based on appropriate eradication treatment, not indefinite antibiotic use.

## Key Messages

- Chronic H. pylori infection is an established cause of gastric adenocarcinoma.
- The strongest established relationship is with noncardia gastric carcinoma.
- H. pylori contributes to a gastric inflammatory/precancerous pathway.
- Eradication reduces gastric-cancer incidence in randomized and observational evidence.
- Benefit has been demonstrated in asymptomatic infected people and higher-risk groups.
- Eradication can also reduce metachronous gastric cancer after treatment of early gastric cancer.
- Eradication does not guarantee zero future gastric-cancer risk.
- Established atrophic gastritis or intestinal metaplasia may leave residual risk.
- Prevention must balance cancer-risk reduction with antimicrobial stewardship.
- Exact eradication treatment belongs to PP-0161.
